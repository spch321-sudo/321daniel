@@392@@
The second question: we are used to “one less trouble,” not wanting to bother people and not wanting to be called unsociable. So many people’s “resolve” often stays only in the heart; they dare not speak, and they dare not ask. Daniel’s way is worth learning: he neither declared it loudly nor swallowed it in silence, but spoke in a clear, courteous way that could be tested. You do not need to confront, and you do not need to hide; in the time it takes to drink a cup of tea, you can say one sentence: “This may not be convenient for me; how could we arrange it?” What we need is first to have a clear position before God, and then to say it in the gentlest tone. This way we do not lose the relationship; very often it makes the relationship more real.
@@393@@
In front of family and elders, how can I “ask” without being disrespectful?
@@394@@
“Asking” is itself a respectful way of speaking. First affirm the other person’s kindness, then state your need, and offer a workable alternative, for example, “Thank you for preparing this; I can’t have this dish. May I have more of the other dishes?” If the other person does not accept it, there is no need to argue or leave; expressing it gently and clearly again and again matters more than winning once. What elders see is not only your words but your attitude. Often a person who is neither servile nor haughty, and who speaks with thanks, helps the family lower their guard more than one who argues his case. If your family is especially sensitive about faith, you can also start with thanks, care and company, letting them see that faith makes you softer, not more distant.

VII. Living Out 321—A Small Table
@@396@@
This chapter lets us see the three foundations: Jesus is my Role Model, the Bible is my Standard, the Holy Spirit is my Guide. Jesus also lived in a world that was not his own; he had honor, yet did not grasp it, and he did not change who he was in order to fit in. The Bible is my Standard, because resolve is to be set upon God’s word, not upon circumstances. The Holy Spirit is my Guide, because at the table no one sets resolve for you; only he can give us a place in the heart. When we read the Bible, it is not to find a rule to force others to keep, but so that God’s word becomes the place in our heart where, even in the smallest detail, we know to whom we belong.
@@397@@
The two core values: Let Jesus be King does not begin with big things but with a small table: inside other people’s standards, first ask who the master of this table is. Let Jesus receive all the glory, because in every turn of this chapter the verb is God’s: the Lord gave, God made him find favor, God gave them understanding. The credit is not Daniel’s but God’s. If today you can take away only one sentence, please take this one: God moves first, and only then is it possible for me to have a settled heart; it is not that I first stand firm and then God is willing to help.

Four young men supported one another and resolved together; in a kingdom that did not belong to God, there was still a small group living under God’s sovereignty. This is not escape; it is staying in place and becoming God’s witness. Selflessness is not suppressing yourself, but letting go of the thought “I will arrange the outcome in God’s place”: I settle my heart, and leave the consequences with him.

@@400@@

The text says that Daniel resolved, and also asked others; a truly strong person is one who knows he needs God and needs other people. @@401@@

<p><strong>וַתִּתְפָּעֶם רוּחוֹ</strong> (his spirit was troubled): the verb is a reflexive stem, literally “his spirit was struck of itself.” The same verb is used in Gen 41:8 for Pharaoh’s dream, and in Ps 77:4 for “I am so troubled”; it is “the kind of unease that will not let you sleep.”</p><p>“The second year” seems to differ from the three years of training in 1:5. A common explanation is that Babylon counted the year of a king’s accession as the “accession year,” not counting it as year one, so that the three years of training span several calendar years without needing to be three full twelve-month periods. The text does not ask us to count days here.</p>

<p><strong>אֲרָמִית</strong> (Aramaic) is the marker of the language switch in the Old Testament: from 2:4b, through 7:28, the original changes to Aramaic. Why the switch comes here is discussed in the issues section; for now notice the scene where the switch happens: <strong>the Chaldeans spoke in Aramaic</strong>, that is, the common language of the court, and the text simply continues writing in that language.</p><p><strong>אַזְדָּא מִנִּי מִלְּתָא</strong> (2:5, 8): “the matter is settled by me” or “the matter has gone from me.” <strong>אַזְדָּא</strong> is a Persian loanword whose meaning is disputed: one view is “certain, decided,” another “gone, forgotten.” The CUV takes the latter (“I have forgotten”); whichever is taken, what the king wants is: “first tell me the dream.”</p>
@@508@@
<p><strong>The king made a demand no one could meet: not only the interpretation but the dream itself had to be told first.</strong></p><p>This is a very interesting conversation. The Chaldeans said in polite court language: “O king, live forever; tell your servants the dream.” This was their usual way: first hear the dream, then interpret it, and the interpretation looks very professional but no one can ever verify it. But the king changed the rules: <strong>you tell me the dream first, and then I will believe your interpretation.</strong> “The dream is settled by me” (or “forgotten”)—whichever reading is taken, the king’s meaning is clear: I want to know whether you really see, or are making up a story.</p><p>The Chaldeans’ answer is the most honest sentence in the passage: “There is not a man on the earth who can show the king’s matter.” Then they said a theological sentence: “except the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh.” <strong>They were half right.</strong> The gods they spoke of “do not dwell with flesh,” that is, they are far from people and do not live among them; but in verse 22 that follows, Daniel will say: “the light dwells with him.” The true God is a God who is with people.</p><p>Human expertise shows its limit just when it is truly needed. Today too we often use “more methods, more experts” to deal with unease; the text reminds us that there is a kind of problem where only when people say, “We have no way,” does the God of heaven have room to speak.</p>

<p><strong>וְחַכִּימַיָּא מִתְקַטְּלִין</strong> (the wise men were to be slain) is a participle, describing “in progress”: as soon as the decree went out, the killing was already on its way. Daniel and his three friends had not appeared before the king, yet were counted among the “wise men,” a group caught up in it.</p>
@@511@@
<p><strong>Human cruelty often begins with the sentence “I have no way.”</strong></p><p>The king’s anger was not at one person but at the whole system: he “commanded that all the wise men of Babylon be destroyed.” He was not punishing those who had failed in duty; he was announcing: this group I once trusted is useless.</p><p>The reader should see a detail here: <strong>Daniel and his three friends had not been part of that failure, yet were to be killed along with them.</strong> They had not failed, but they belonged to that group, and so they faced the same judgment. This is the situation of exiles: you have done nothing wrong, yet because you “belong to the system,” you are counted in with the rest.</p><p>In any environment where power is unstable, there are such moments: a sudden order, a sudden purge, a sudden fear. The text does not gloss over it; it says directly: the decree has gone out, and the killing is on its way. God’s servants are not exempted from such situations.</p><p>But then, in verse 14, the story turns. <strong>He did not run, did not cry injustice, did not fight; he answered with a tactful word.</strong> He took in the pressure first, and only then acted. This order is worth learning in every sudden pressure: do not rush to strike back, and do not rush to flee; ask first, and then speak.</p>

@@514@@
<p><strong>A man who still asks quiet questions under a death order is one who truly knows who rules.</strong></p><p>Arioch, the captain of the guard, had come to carry out the order. Daniel did not resist him but asked: “Why is the decree so urgent from the king?” It was a very quiet question, and it made a man who was ready to kill stop and talk.</p><p>Daniel then did three things: first, he went in to see the king and asked for time, and he did not say “I guarantee I can,” only “grant me a delay”; second, he returned to his house and told his companions; third, he asked them to “desire mercies of the God of heaven.” The order of the three things is very clear: <strong>ask first, seek first, pray first, and only then the interpretation.</strong></p><p>Please notice especially the word “companions.” He did not carry this alone but called in three friends to seek together. In chapter 1 they resolved together; in chapter 2 they prayed together. <strong>In someone else’s kingdom we are not isolated individuals; we are a small group that guards one another.</strong></p><p>And that title: “the God of heaven.” It is the most common title in the Aramaic sections. The Babylonians said: the gods do not dwell with flesh. They could not find the answer on earth. Daniel said: we seek the God of heaven. <strong>A different direction gives a different answer.</strong> When people meet something they cannot solve, they often look first for a way on earth; the text teaches us to look up first.</p>

This is not a single act but a continuing rule.</p>
@@517@@
<p><strong>Before giving the answer, he spoke a hymn of praise.</strong> This is the heart of the chapter, and the first prayer of the whole book.</p><p>Look at the order: God had revealed the secret, and Daniel did not run straight to the king; he stopped first and blessed the God of heaven. This praise is a miniature map of the world: time is in his hand (“changes the times and the seasons”), kingship is in his hand (“removes kings, and sets up kings”), wisdom is in his hand (“gives wisdom to the wise”), and even darkness is in his hand (“knows what is in the darkness”).</p><p>Then comes the sentence that answers the Chaldeans: <strong>“the light dwells with him.”</strong> Earlier they said: the gods do not dwell with flesh. Now Daniel says: this God not only dwells with people, he himself is the dwelling of light. This is the greatest rebuttal of this chapter, and also its gentlest comfort: the God we serve is not a God far away.</p><p>He also said: “wisdom and might are his.” Then in verse 23 he says in the first person: “You have given me wisdom and might.” Please notice that this “give” is on the same line as the “give” of chapter 1: wisdom is given, not one’s own.</p><p>In 321 language, this is the starting point of “Let Jesus receive all the glory”: first see to whom the credit belongs. This passage is not the result of one person’s prayer, but of a small group seeking together, and then God himself answering. <strong>God’s answer often comes in the night; and people’s praise need not wait for the morning.</strong></p>

Daniel did not say “I am not very smart,” but: it is not that my wisdom surpasses all the living; this secret was revealed so that the king might know.</p>
@@520@@
<p><strong>Daniel’s first words were not “I can,” but “I am not.”</strong></p><p>First look at the first thing he did on going in: he did not rush to claim credit but said to Arioch: “Do not destroy the wise men of Babylon.” He pleaded for those who could not interpret the dream. These were his “colleagues” and also his “rivals,” and he did not let them be killed just because he could interpret the dream. <strong>His ability was to save people, not to beat others.</strong></p><p>Then he stood before the king. The king asked him: “Are you able?” This is a question that makes it very easy to say “I can,” because saying “I can” brings a future. But Daniel’s answer first says “cannot”: “wise men, enchanters, magicians, or soothsayers” cannot tell the king, and then “there is a God in heaven.” He turned the king’s eyes away from people and pointed to heaven.</p><p>Most important is verse 30: “But as for me, this secret is not revealed to me for any wisdom that I have more than any living.” This is a humble declaration and also the watershed of the whole book: <strong>pride is leaving one’s position and grasping power; humility is returning to one’s position and giving the glory back to God.</strong> Before him was a king used to hearing praise; behind him was a post that would keep being raised by the king. Yet what he said at that moment was: this is not me.</p><p>We too often stand in such a position: able to do what others cannot. At that moment, what is the first thing we say? “I am good,” or “This was given by God”?</p>

<p><strong>דִּי לָא בִידַיִן</strong> (not by hands): this negation is the pivot of the passage. The image was made by human hands, but the stone was “not by hands.” This expression is later used in the New Testament to describe “what is made by God, not made by hands” (compare Mark 14:58; Heb 9:11), a connection the New Testament itself makes; here we say only: the source of this stone is not in the world of men.</p><p><strong>הִתְגְּזֶרֶת</strong> (cut out) is a reflexive-passive form that does not say who cut it, but makes clear that it was “not cut by man.” This passive is a theological passive, pointing to God.</p>
@@523@@
<p><strong>The biggest role in the dream is not the image but the stone.</strong></p><p>First look at the image: tall, bright, terrifying. “Stood before you”: what the king saw was a projection of his own kingdom: head of gold, breast of silver, belly of bronze, legs of iron, and the lower it went, the harder and the less precious the metal; at the feet, half iron and half clay. <strong>This image, from top to bottom, goes from precious to cheap, from pure to mixed, and finally stands on an unstable foundation.</strong></p><p>Then a stone appeared. Notice its source: “cut out without hands.” The image was made by hands and set up by a human kingdom, but the stone was not. This “without hands” is the key phrase of the chapter: <strong>God’s Kingdom does not grow out of human kingdoms, and is not built by human strength.</strong></p><p>Where did the stone strike? Not at the head of gold but at the feet, at the most fragile place. The image fell, and gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay were broken in pieces “together,” becoming chaff of the threshing floor, carried away by the wind, with “no place found for them.” This is a total end: all the kingdoms, high or low, vanish at the same moment.</p><p>Then the stone that broke everything became a great mountain, filling the whole earth. <strong>Human kingdoms will at last be replaced; God’s Kingdom will fill.</strong> This dream is not a historical timetable but a declaration: everything people build, however splendid, is not final.</p>

Kingdom, power, strength and glory were not seized by Nebuchadnezzar; the God of heaven gave them to him.</p><p>“King of kings” is a royal title of the ancient Near East; here it is rewritten: you, this “king of kings,” were set up by another “God.”</p>
@@526@@
<p><strong>The highest compliment, and the heaviest reminder: “the God of heaven has given the kingdom to you.”</strong></p><p>Daniel spoke to a world ruler without avoiding the point. He first respected his rank: “You are king of kings,” and then at once put the title back in its right place: “the God of heaven has given the kingdom, the power, the strength, and the glory.” <strong>All four were given.</strong></p><p>This is a shrewd way of speaking, and also a big message: kingdom, power and glory were not taken by the king through his own ability; they were entrusted. One who is entrusted must give account to the one who entrusted. This sentence will appear again in chapter 4, and that time the king will pay a very great price for forgetting it.</p><p>Look at one more detail: “You are the head of gold.” The head of gold is the most precious part of the whole image, and the first to appear. Nebuchadnezzar must have been very pleased to hear this. But the text has already prepared the words that follow: after the head of gold come silver, bronze, iron and clay, and the head of gold itself will end. <strong>Being called “most precious” does not mean “forever.”</strong></p><p>For us today this is also a reminder: the positions, talents and influence we have were all given. They are not unimportant, but we must not forget who gave them.</p>

<p>This passage names only the “head of gold” as Nebuchadnezzar (2:38); for the other three kingdoms the text says only “another kingdom,” “a third kingdom,” “a fourth kingdom,” without naming them. This is why later generations read the “four kingdoms” in different ways (Babylon—Medo-Persia—Greece—Rome, or Babylon—Media—Persia—Greece); the views of the various schools are set out in the issues section.</p><p><strong>לָא דָּבְקִין</strong> (not joining): this word describes a mixed kingdom whose parts do not cling to one another. The weight of the text lies not on “which two peoples,” but on “mixed, yet unable to be one.”</p>
@@529@@
<p><strong>This passage is the one that most tempts people to draw up a chart, and it most needs the reader to put his hands down.</strong></p><p>The text says clearly only: the head of gold is Babylon. The silver, bronze and iron that follow are not named. Many people reading this immediately want to match each metal with an empire, a year, a present-day nation. This is the road most easily taken by those who read this book through the ages, and also the road that most easily takes people away from the point of the text.</p><p>So what is this passage really saying? Look at the “character” it describes: the second kingdom is “inferior to you,” the third “will bear rule over all the earth,” the fourth “will be strong as iron, because iron breaks in pieces and subdues all things,” and the last is “partly strong and partly broken,” iron mixed with clay, “not holding together.” <strong>Every kingdom has a beginning and an end; every one grows worse and more mixed, and in the end stands on an unstable foundation.</strong></p><p>This is a very honest view of history: human kingdoms grow harder and also more brittle the later they come. The hardest iron is at last mixed with the softest clay, becoming a kingdom whose parts cannot cling together. <strong>Power can break everything, but it cannot make people truly one.</strong></p><p>So we need not turn this passage into a map. We need only remember what it says to us: any kingdom that looks solid, strong and splendid is limited; and there is one Kingdom that is different. That is the point of the next verse.</p>

Human kingdoms change hands; God’s Kingdom will not.</p>
@@532@@
<p><strong>The most important verse of the whole chapter: human kingdoms come one after another, and God’s Kingdom does not grow out of them.</strong></p><p>“In the days of those kings the God of heaven will set up a kingdom.” Please notice the subject and the verb: the subject is the God of heaven, the verb is “set up.” It was not set up by man, not reformed into being by man, not the natural evolution of human history, but set up by God himself. Read with 2:21: since he can “remove kings, and set up kings,” now he will “set up a kingdom.”</p><p>This kingdom has three characteristics: first, it is <strong>never destroyed</strong>. All the earlier kingdoms “break in pieces and crush”; this one will not. Second, it is <strong>not left to another people</strong>. It will not be transferred, absorbed or replaced. Third, it will <strong>stand forever</strong>. Human kingdoms have a time; God’s Kingdom has no end.</p><p>Then verse 45 returns to that stone: “cut out without hands.” This stone is the mark of this Kingdom. God’s Kingdom does not rest on human hands, human armies, economies or systems; its source is outside the human world.</p><p>This gives us two comforts today. First, we need not treat any kingdom as our final hope, nor despair at the rise and fall of any kingdom. Second, we are invited to become people of this Kingdom. <strong>It is not that we build God’s Kingdom, but that God brings us into the Kingdom he has set up.</strong> This is what 321 means by “to build God’s system”: not that we invent a system, but that we live in the Kingdom God has already set up.</p>

<p><strong>סְגִד</strong> (fall down, worship) is the Aramaic verb specifically for “worship,” and it will become a key word in chapter 3 (3:5–6, 10–15, 18, 28). In chapter 2 the king “worships” Daniel, and in chapter 3 the king commands people to “worship” the golden image; the contrast between these two will be developed later.</p><p><strong>וּמִנְחָה וְנִיחֹחִין</strong> (an offering and sweet odors) is the vocabulary of worship, but the text does not say whether Daniel accepted or refused. His response is discussed in the issues section.</p>
@@535@@
<p><strong>The king fell down and worshiped, but the text puts its weight on the sentence in his mouth: “Of a truth your God is the God of gods, and the Lord of kings.”</strong></p><p>This is a big change. At the beginning of chapter 2 this king trusted magicians, gods and his own power; at the end he says: your God is the God of gods, the Lord of kings, and a revealer of secrets. His mouth spoke the right words. But please notice: what he said was “your God,” not “my God.” This is an acknowledgment, not necessarily a conversion. In chapters 3 and 4 he still has a long road to walk.</p><p>Now look at Daniel being promoted: ruler over the whole province and chief of all the wise men. This was his promotion and also his test. With power came more difficulties in not defiling himself. <strong>But he did one thing: he asked the king to appoint his three companions over the affairs of the province too.</strong> He did not monopolize it; he raised his friends. This is the 920 practice: the few “2s” beside him, he did not forget.</p><p>The last sentence is easily overlooked: “but Daniel was in the gate of the king.” He stayed at the king’s gate, that is, in a place where he could speak at any time and also face risk at any time. <strong>He did not leave his calling because he had been promoted; he stayed there, awaiting God’s next entrusting.</strong> In chapter 3 his three companions are sent to the plain of Dura; Daniel is not present. The text does not explain this difference, and we leave it there until chapter 3.</p>
