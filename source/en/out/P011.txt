What does “being raised up” have to do with having no self?
@@3482@@
The relationship is direct. A person with self always wants to stand by his own strength, so he fears falling and fears being seen to fall; a person without self admits that he will fall asleep, get sick and fail to understand, and admits that there is a hand that will raise him. Having no self does not mean becoming nothing; it means no longer using our own strength to hold up the look of standing very straight. When you learn to say, “Lord, raise me up,” you begin to enter the no-self life, and you find that standing because you were raised is steadier than standing by holding on. As you live this way, those around you will slowly be affected too: no longer pressing one another with calculating and holding on, but encouraging one another by raising up.

He first admitted righteousness before speaking of mercy; and mercy was already present in the midst of judgment.</p>
@@3570@@
<p><strong>Righteousness belongs to him, shame belongs to us; and mercies and forgiveness also belong to him.</strong></p><p>This is the middle of the whole prayer, and the longest stretch of confession. Notice its sentence pattern: “Lord, righteousness belongs to you, but to us confusion of face.” Two “belongs” separate the two sides very clearly: God is righteous; we are ashamed. This is not self-belittling but honesty, which gives the prayer a place to stand.</p><p>But look at verse 9, the same pattern, but the content turned over: “To the Lord our God belong mercies and forgiveness; for we have rebelled against him.” Mercies and forgiveness also belong to him. This sentence, placed in the middle of the confession, is the hinge of the whole prayer. As he confesses he remembers: he is a forgiving God. Without this, confession would become only heavy self-blame.</p><p>Then he mentions the curse and the oath written in the law of Moses, poured out on them (9:11). For many modern readers this is hard. We talk about its meaning in the issues. The point is: Daniel did not defend God or make excuses for him; he only said that God is righteous, and that all this is not that he broke the covenant but that we did. In verse 13 he adds a very deep word: “Yet we have not entreated the favor of Yahweh our God, that we should turn from our iniquities and have discernment in your truth.” The calamity came, and they had not yet turned.</p><p>Please do not turn this passage into blame on anyone who suffers. This is Daniel, on behalf of the whole people, speaking a review of history before God. He himself was one of the exiles. If you are suffering, the text does not ask you to say “I deserved it”; it wants you to know that there is a God who is righteous and also merciful, and you may bring your pain, and your weakness, into his presence.</p>

This is the shortest and most urgent sentence of the whole prayer.</p><p><strong>לְמַעַנְךָ אֱלֹהַי</strong> (for your own sake, my God): the final ground of prayer is not I, nor the people, but “you yourself,” “your name.”</p>
@@3573@@
<p><strong>The final ground of his petition is only three words: “for your own sake.”</strong></p><p>This is the most moving stretch of prayer in the whole chapter. He first remembers the exodus from Egypt: “who has brought your people out of the land of Egypt with a mighty hand, and have gotten yourself renown, as it is today.” In the darkest hour he first remembers what God has done. That memory gives him courage to open his mouth. Then he asks God to turn his anger away from Jerusalem, “your holy mountain.” Then he says: “cause your face to shine on your sanctuary that is desolate.” This “face to shine” recalls the blessing in Numbers: “Yahweh make his face to shine on you.”</p><p>Then the heart of the chapter: “we do not present our petitions before you for our righteousness, but for your great mercies’ sake.” Please read this sentence slowly: we ask, not because we have any merit. Even prayer is not our merit. The verb in the original is more vivid: <strong>“we cast our petitions down before you.”</strong> Like putting down a heavy load. That load is shame, desolation, helplessness. He did not say, “Lord, look how repentant we are”; he said, “look at your mercy.”</p><p>The last verse is three short cries: “Lord, hear. Lord, forgive. Lord, listen and do. Don’t defer, for your own sake, my God, because your city and your people are called by your name.” The ground of the whole prayer moves from the people to God’s name. This is the steadiest place in prayer: what we ask is not our face but his name.</p><p>If you have ever measured your prayer by “am I worthy?” this passage will set you free. You need not be worthy; you only need to bring your petition and set it down. Exchange “my righteousness” for “your mercy.” This is the deepest grace of this chapter.</p>

<p><strong>עוֹד אֲנִי מְדַבֵּר בַּתְּפִלָּה</strong> (while I was still speaking in prayer): his prayer was not yet finished. @@3576@@
<p><strong>Before the prayer was finished the answer was already flying to him; and the name he was given was “greatly beloved.”</strong></p><p>Verses 20 and 21 each have “while I was speaking,” a very deliberate repetition: Gabriel arrived in the midst of his prayer. His prayer was not yet finished, and the answer came. This reminds us that God’s response does not wait until we have finished speaking before it begins to move. His word had gone out long before.</p><p>Look at the second thing Gabriel said: “At the beginning of your petitions the commandment went out, and I have come to tell you.” From the moment you began to ask, God’s “word” went out, and I (Gabriel) have come to tell it to you. This sentence deserves a pause and slow thought: one of your prayers may look as if it has no answer yet; but the text says the word has already gone out. He moves earlier than we do.</p><p>Then this name: “for you are greatly beloved.” The original is חֲמוּדוֹת, literally “very precious, very much desired.” He was not called beloved because he prayed well; he was beloved before the prayer. Prayer is not an exchange to earn love; it is speaking within love. For a man who had just confessed sin for a whole chapter, how his heart must have been comforted to hear this sentence.</p><p>One more thing: “the time of the evening offering.” At that time the temple was gone and the evening offering had stopped, but Daniel still remembered that hour. Prayer became his evening offering: Psalm 141 says, “the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice.” With no temple, there was still an evening offering: he had carried the altar into his own prayer.</p>

<p><strong>What the seventy sevens are to accomplish is six things: to finish the transgression, to make an end of sins, to make atonement for iniquity, to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal up the vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most holy.</strong></p><p>This verse is the most studied in the whole book, and also the one that most easily leads people to calculate days. First, our position: in the stance of the whole book we have already said that <strong>these notes do not count days and do not set up a timetable</strong>. There have long been many different readings, and we set them side by side in the issues, including: that the seventy sevens point to a stretch of history beginning in ancient times; that they point to the coming of Christ; that they point to a time still further on; or that they are a symbolic, structured period. Daniel had prayed: the seventy years are up; forgive, and let Jerusalem be desolate no more. God’s answer was a larger vision: “Seventy weeks are decreed on your people and on your holy city” (the word is literally “sevens”). The seventy years stretched into “seventy sevens.” God did not answer only the matter of seventy years; he lifted the eyes to something farther.</p><p>This verse lists six things in two groups. The first group, three things, is about “sin”: to finish the transgression, to make an end of sins, to make atonement for iniquity. The second group, three things, is about “fulfillment”: to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal up the vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most holy. Please notice that not one of these six is military, political or geographical; all of them deal with sin and righteousness.</p><p>This is the center of this chapter: <strong>God’s answer to Daniel was not a schedule of days but a promise that sin would be dealt with.</strong> If today you are confused by a complicated history or a great suffering, please remember: what God cares about most is dealing with sin and bringing in everlasting righteousness.</p>

<p><strong>These three verses are the hardest passage in the whole book. We do not calculate; we look only at their direction: the Anointed One is cut off, desolation is decreed, and God’s end is decreed too.</strong></p><p>First let us say plainly: these three verses are among the most disputed texts in the whole Bible. There have long been many different readings. Some point the “Anointed One, the prince” to Cyrus or to a certain high priest; some to Christ; some point “the people of the prince” to an ancient empire, some to later Rome, some to a figure of the last days. We set the views side by side in the issues; we do not decide for you, and we certainly do not set up a timetable here.</p><p>So what can we say? First, we see the order the text gives: first the rebuilding of Jerusalem, in troubled times; then the Anointed One is cut off; then destruction and war, to the end. I. Learning from the Books: Not Calculating but Praying (9:1–3)
@@3897@@
Peace, brothers and sisters. If one day you read one of God’s promises, what is your first reaction? Do you take out a calculator and start to calculate, or do you kneel down? In this lesson we read of a man who read a promise and did not pick up a pen; he fasted, put on sackcloth and ashes, and turned his face to God. This is Daniel chapter 9, the chapter in the whole book most like a prayer book.
@@3898@@
But this chapter does not begin with prayer; it begins with reading. The text says: “In the first year of his reign I, Daniel, understood by the books the number of the years about which Yahweh’s word came to Jeremiah the prophet, for the accomplishing of the desolations of Jerusalem, even seventy years.” He was reading Jeremiah. In Jeremiah there are two places that speak of seventy years. He had read them. Please notice that what he read was “the books,” and in the original this is plural: the scrolls. By then God’s word had already been collected and was read over and over. He did not rely on dreams or on visions; by reading what God had already said, he understood the time. This gives us a very practical reminder: to understand God’s heart today, the steadiest road is also to return to what he has already said, reading it again and again, thinking it over again and again, and not running after fresh prophecies.
@@3899@@
Then what did he do? An ordinary person who read “seventy years are fulfilled” might calculate the days, might get excited, might say: it is nearly time. Daniel did not. The text says: “I set my face to the Lord God, to seek by prayer and petitions, with fasting and sackcloth and ashes.” He turned his face to God. This is the first lesson of this chapter: God’s promise is not for calculating; it is for praying.
@@3900@@
Why? Because a promise is a word of relationship, not a timetable. When God said seventy years, he was not sending Daniel a countdown timer; he was saying: I remember you, and I will bring you back. When Daniel read this sentence, his reaction was: I must go and meet the God who is speaking. Is there a promise of God in your life that you have always wanted to calculate? Perhaps about a child, a job, the reconciliation of a family. Try, like Daniel, first putting down the abacus and turning your face to him. Such a turning needs a little courage, because calculating often gives us a feeling of control, while prayer needs us to let go.
@@3901@@
As for fasting, sackcloth and ashes, these outward postures are not the goal; they are ways of putting the whole person down. Your body and your circumstances are not the same as Daniel’s, and you need not do exactly the same. What God looks at is the heart that turns its face to him.
@@3902@@
When I read God’s promise, how do I avoid only “calculating,” and truly “pray”?
@@3903@@
You can try three steps. First, copy that promise on paper, without adding any explanation of your own. Second, write down the urge to calculate too: “I want to know, when?” Third, put the two sheets before God and pray one sentence: Lord, I give you this promise, and my heart that wants to calculate. Doing this does not suppress your expectation; it lets expectation return to relationship. You will find that when the face is turned to God, waiting changes from anxiety to quiet. You can do this small practice once each morning, and a week later look back at your heart: has it slowly turned from calculating to relying?
@@3904@@
II. We Have Sinned and Done Wrong; Righteousness Belongs to Him (9:4–14)
@@3905@@
Then he opened his mouth: “I prayed to Yahweh my God, and made confession, and said, ‘Oh, Lord, the great and dreadful God, who keeps covenant and loving kindness with those who love him and keep his commandments.’” Please notice this order: first praise, then confession. God is the one who keeps covenant and shows loving kindness; this is the foundation of the whole prayer. He was not confessing to a cold judge but to a covenant-keeping God. Before confession there is first loving kindness. If you feel you are not qualified to open your mouth, begin with this sentence: Lord, you keep covenant, you show loving kindness.
@@3906@@
Then five verbs: “we have sinned, and have dealt perversely, and have done wickedly, and have rebelled, even turning aside from your precepts and from your ordinances.” Please notice that every verb is “we.” Not one is “they.” This is not easy. Daniel is the most praised man in the whole book, and the text records no fault of his. Yet when he prayed he said “we.” This is the posture of an intercessor: he puts himself inside the people and does not stand outside pointing the finger. Have you ever prayed for a group you belong to, a family, a church, a country? When you prayed, did you say “they” or “we”? This “we” turned his prayer from the mouth of one man into the mouth of a people.
@@3907@@
Then comes the hinge of this passage: “Lord, righteousness belongs to you, but to us confusion of face”; and then, in verse 9: “To the Lord our God belong mercies and forgiveness; for we have rebelled against him.” The sentence pattern is the same, and the content is turned over. Righteousness belongs to him; shame belongs to us; and mercies and forgiveness also belong to him. This sentence, placed in the middle of the confession, is the hinge of the whole prayer. Without it, confession would become only heavy self-blame.
@@3908@@
Then he mentions the curse in the law of Moses, poured out on them. For many modern readers this is hard, and we talk about it in the issues. Daniel’s point is: he did not defend God or make excuses for him; he only said that God is righteous, and that all this is not that he broke the covenant but that we did. In verse 13 he adds a very deep word: “Yet we have not entreated the favor of Yahweh our God, that we should turn from our iniquities and have discernment in your truth.” The calamity had come, and they had not yet turned. True repentance begins with admitting, and also with believing that God is still willing to listen.
@@3909@@
Please do not turn this passage into blame on those who suffer. This is Daniel, on behalf of the whole people, speaking a review of history before God. He himself was one of the exiles. If you are suffering, the text does not ask you to say “I deserved it”; it wants you to know that there is a God who is righteous and also merciful, and you may bring your pain, and your weakness, into his presence.
@@3910@@
Will confessing with “we” make me responsible for other people’s sins?
@@3911@@
No. “We” does not mean carrying others’ sin; it means not standing outside to judge. You are still responsible only for your own conduct, but before God you are willing to stand together with the family, church or country you belong to, and say: we need mercy. This is a kind of humility, and also a kind of love. You can open your mouth this way: Lord, our family has been wrong in this matter; I have a share in it too. Saying it this way is not self-blame but bringing the whole relationship before God so that he may heal it. You can also find a companion and open your mouths with “we” together, so that confession is no longer a lonely thing but two people walking together toward mercy.
@@3912@@
III. Light from the Original: Confess, Cast Down, Greatly Beloved (9:4, 18, 23)
@@3913@@
Three words in the original of this chapter deserve a slow reading. The first is “confess,” Hebrew וָאֶתְוַדֶּה (va'etvaddeh). This root יָדָה (yadah) in Hebrew means both “to give thanks” and “to confess.” Confession and praise are in the same root: admitting what we are, and admitting what God is. This is why Daniel’s confession begins with praise: great and dreadful, keeping covenant and showing loving kindness.
@@3914@@
The second is “cast down,” Hebrew מַפִּילִים (mappilim). 9:18 says: “we do not present our petitions before you for our righteousness, but for your great mercies’ sake.” The WEB renders it “present”; the verb literally means “to cause to fall”: we cause our petitions to fall before you. Like putting down a heavy load. That load is shame, desolation, helplessness. He did not say, “Lord, look how repentant we are”; he said, “look at your mercy.”
@@3915@@
The third is “greatly beloved,” Hebrew חֲמוּדוֹת (chamudot). Literally “desired, very precious.” Gabriel said to Daniel: “for you are greatly beloved.” This name appears three times in this book (9:23, 10:11, 10:19). Think about it: he was beloved before he prayed, not beloved because he prayed well. Prayer is not an exchange to earn love; it is speaking within love. This lets us say quietly: I do not open my mouth because I have done well enough; I dare to open my mouth because I am loved.
@@3916@@
Together these three words are the route of this chapter: confession begins with praise; petition puts the load down; and the answer comes from the certainty of being “greatly beloved.” You can ask yourself: is my prayer trying to “exchange” something, or to “set something down”? Am I proving whether I am worthy, or admitting his mercy? Today, choose one of these three words as your prayer for this week: praise, setting down, or receiving being loved.
@@3917@@
How do I actually “set the petition down”?
@@3918@@
You can use a very simple action: put your hands palm up on your knees; then say the heaviest thing you have, as if pouring it out of your hands and setting it before God. Then close with one sentence: not because of my righteousness, but because of your mercy. No need to say much, and no need to say it beautifully. This action exchanges “I will prove” for “I will entrust.” You will find that the lightness after prayer is not because the matter is solved but because you no longer carry it alone. If it is hard for you to say, do not force it; you can start practicing with something very small. God does not hurry you; he waits for you.
