<p><strong>רוּחַ אֱלָהִין קַדִּישִׁין</strong> (spirit of the holy gods): <strong>אֱלָהִין</strong> is plural, and <strong>קַדִּישִׁין</strong> is also plural, so the literal sense is “spirit of holy gods.” The CUV and most Chinese translations take “holy God” because these are the words of a foreign king in his own vocabulary. Later interpreters understand this in two ways: one, that it is the king’s address within a polytheistic setting; two, that the plural means “the Most Holy God.” See the issues.</p><p><strong>שְׁלֵה...וְרַעְנַן</strong> (at rest, flourishing): the king used two words, one saying he was secure, one saying he was flourishing like a tree, which lays a clue in advance for the later dream of the “tree.”</p>
@@1353@@
<p><strong>The dream began from the place of “I was at ease.”</strong></p><p>Verse 4 says: I was at rest in my house, and flourishing in my palace. The original uses two words, one for “ease” and one for “flourishing.” This was the king at his best: the nation secure, the building works complete, no visible threat. Yet at just that time he had a dream that made him afraid and troubled. This is an important reminder: <strong>the greatest unease often does not come from hardship, but from the time when “everything is going well.”</strong></p><p>Notice the king’s reaction: he first summoned all the experts. The magicians, enchanters, Chaldeans and diviners: he did what he had done in chapter 2, and the result was the same: “but they couldn’t make known to me its interpretation.” This time the king did not rage or order killing; he only admitted: they could not. Human methods had again reached their end.</p><p>Then he remembered Daniel. Listen to how he described him: “Belteshazzar, according to the name of my god, and in whom is the spirit of the holy gods.” The king used his own name and the name of his own god, yet he admitted: in this man is a “spirit” that I do not have. This is the king’s language, not our confession of faith. We need not read it as a complete doctrine, but we can see that a foreign king, at his most uneasy, remembered the one for whom “no secret troubles him.”</p><p>In your life there are also moments when unease comes in the “smoothest” times. Do not hurry to press it down, and do not hurry to find a method. Like the king, first admit that human methods are limited, and then go find a companion in whom the Spirit of God dwells.</p>

<p><strong>וְרוּמֵהּ יִמְטֵא לִשְׁמַיָּא</strong> (its height reached to the sky): the verb <strong>יִמְטֵא</strong> (to reach) appears again in 4:22, in the interpretation: “your greatness has grown, and reaches to the sky.” The dream and the interpretation use the same word, so that one cannot help seeing that this tree is the king himself.</p><p><strong>וּמָזוֹן לְכֹלָּא</strong> (food for all): the tree’s merit is not its height but that it fed all life. This is the best part of the dream, and also, in the interpretation that follows, God’s gentlest reminder to this king.</p>
@@1356@@
<p><strong>This was a good tree, and that is the most unsettling thing.</strong></p><p>In the first scene of the dream there is no bad part. The tree was exceedingly tall and strong, its height reached to the sky, and it could be seen from the ends of the earth; its leaves were beautiful and its fruit plentiful, all were fed from it, the animals had shade under it and the birds lived in its branches. It is an almost perfect picture. This king was not merely a tyrant who oppressed people; his kingdom did feed and shelter many. The text does not deny this.</p><p>But notice one detail: this tree’s height “reached to the sky.” How high can human achievement go? In the Bible the picture of “reaching to the sky” brings to mind the tower of Babel, where people wanted to make a name for themselves and reach heaven. This tree is not wrong because it grew badly, but because it grew to a height that did not belong to it. <strong>Pride does not grow from bad places; it often grows from good ones.</strong> Ability, achievement, being needed are all good, but when we forget that all of it was given, good turns into height.</p><p>This gives us a very practical reminder: do not look for the traces of pride only in our failures; look for them even more in our successes. The place where you are most praised, the place where you are most relied upon: is there a little spot there where God’s place has quietly been taken? The tree was not wrong, but it needed someone to remind it: you were planted.</p>

<p><strong>שִׁבְעָה עִדָּנִין</strong> (seven times): <strong>עִדָּן</strong> is a “period,” without saying whether days, months or years. Some read it as seven years, some as seven seasons or seven months, and some as a symbol of “a full period.” This commentary does not decide the length for the text; see the issues.</p><p><strong>דִּי שַׁלִּיט ... בְּמַלְכוּת אֲנָשָׁא</strong> (the Most High rules in the kingdom of men): <strong>שַׁלִּיט</strong> is “having sovereignty,” and is the central sentence of this chapter. It appears three times in 4:17, 25, 32, and is said once more at last as the king’s confession in 4:34–35.</p><p><strong>עִיר וְקַדִּישׁ</strong> (a watcher, a holy one): “watcher” is rare elsewhere in the Old Testament; here it refers to a heavenly sent one, and echoes 4:24 “the decree of the Most High.”</p>
@@1359@@
<p><strong>This passage states the most important sentence of the whole book: “the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will.”</strong></p><p>The second scene of the dream is a watcher coming from heaven. He came down from heaven and cried aloud: cut down this tree. Four commands in a row: cut down, cut off, shake off, scatter. It is a complete demolition. But notice: <strong>the stump was left.</strong> The root remained in the earth, bound with a band of iron and bronze. This judgment is not destruction; it is a judgment with something left. The purpose of the judgment was not to wipe out this man, but “let his heart be changed from man’s, and let an animal’s heart be given to him. Then let seven times pass over him.”</p><p>Why an animal’s heart? Because this man, in the heights, forgot he was a man. He thought he was a god, so God let him, in the least human way, learn again to be human. It is a very deep picture: <strong>pride is leaving the place of man and wanting to take God’s place; and this judgment sent him back to the place of man, even lower.</strong></p><p>Then comes the key of this chapter: “to the intent that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, gives it to whomever he will, and sets up over it the lowest of men.” Notice that this sentence is not spoken only to the king; it is “that the living may know,” spoken to everyone. Every kingdom and every position is given. In chapter 2 this sentence was “he changes the times and the seasons; he removes kings, and sets up kings,” and in chapter 7 it is “the kingdom given to the saints of the Most High.” The whole book is saying this sentence.</p><p>What you are doing, the position where you are, the influence you have: all were given. To admit this is not to make oneself small but to put oneself in the right place: to be a good servant, not a false master.</p>

<p><strong>שְׁמַיָּא שַׁלִּטִן</strong> (heaven rules, 4:26): “heaven” here stands for God, a circumlocution of the late Old Testament, meaning “the God of heaven rules.” This usage later echoes in the New Testament phrase “kingdom of heaven.”</p><p><strong>פְרֻק</strong> (break off, 4:27): the original sense is “to break apart, to tear off,” and some read it as “to redeem, to set free.” The CUV takes “cut off sin,” but the verb can also be understood as “to pull oneself out of sin by righteousness.” See the issues.</p><p><strong>צִדְקָה</strong> (righteousness): in late Hebrew and Aramaic this word often carries the sense of “almsgiving, doing good,” and so is set beside the next line, “showing mercy to the poor.”</p>
@@1362@@
<p><strong>The difficulty of the interpreter is to say a painful truth and still love the person.</strong></p><p>First look at Daniel’s reaction: “stricken mute for a while, and his thoughts troubled him.” He was not a man without feeling. He understood the dream, and knew it was a judgment on the king. The king, seeing his face, comforted him instead: do not be troubled. He said, “My lord, may the dream be for those who hate you.” This is a very human sentence: he truly hoped the dream was not about the king. <strong>A true interpreter comes with tears to speak of judgment.</strong></p><p>Then he said very directly: “it is you, O king.” No detours, no technique. He matched each part of the dream with the king’s life: grown and strong, that is you; greatness reaching to the sky, that is you; dominion to the end of the earth, that is you. Then the verdict: you will be driven from men, dwell with the animals of the field, eat grass as oxen. Notice that Daniel did not treat the verdict as his own matter; he said, “the decree of the Most High.” It was not his idea or his judgment; he only carried the word from heaven into the palace.</p><p>Then the most important line: he did not stop at judgment, he gave the king a way: “let my counsel be acceptable to you, and break off your sins by righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the poor. Perhaps there may be a lengthening of your tranquility.” There is a word here to look at slowly: “perhaps.” He gave no guarantee and made no promise on God’s behalf. The judgment was set, yet the door of repentance remained open. To a mighty king he said: see the poor. This is the most concrete repentance: not more sacrifices, but more mercy.</p><p>If one day you need to say a hard truth to someone, remember Daniel: first grieve for the person, then speak clearly, and finally give a way out.</p>

<p><strong>בָּבֶל רַבְּתָא דִי־אֲנָה בֱנַיְתַהּ</strong> (this great Babylon, which I have built): in the king’s words, “I” occurs most; he says “I,” by “my power,” for “the glory of my majesty.” In the original the “I” is piled word by word into one sentence.</p><p><strong>עִלָּיָא</strong> (the Most High, 4:32): the Aramaic Old Testament title for God, its root meaning “high.” This contrasts with the king’s tree that “reached to the sky”: the one truly on high is not the tree but the Most High.</p>
@@1365@@
<p><strong>Judgment did not come at once; God first gave twelve months.</strong></p><p>Verse 29 says: “At the end of twelve months.” The dream, the interpretation and the counsel were all given, and yet nothing happened. One month, two months, half a year, a year. These twelve months were God’s patience, and also the most dangerous time for man: because nothing happened, people think it does not matter. <strong>God’s patience is often taken as God’s silence, and God’s silence is again taken as one’s own safety.</strong></p><p>Then the king walked in the royal palace of Babylon, looking over his city. He said one sentence: “Is not this great Babylon, which I have built for the royal dwelling place, by the might of my power and for the glory of my majesty?” Count them: I, my power, my majesty, my glory. In this one sentence there is only one protagonist. This is not an evil man speaking evil; it is a very successful man speaking the truth: the city really was built by him, the works really were completed by him. But he turned “a position given” into “my achievement.”</p><p>This is the most exact definition of pride: leaving the place of man, and taking God’s glory for oneself. <strong>“While the word was in the king’s mouth, a voice came from the sky.”</strong> God did not wait for him to finish, because the sentence had already been finished. The verdict did not change: the kingdom has departed from you, you will be driven out, and seven times will pass over you. Then the text uses only one verse to write the end: eating grass as oxen, hair grown like eagles’ feathers, nails like birds’ claws.</p><p>Notice that the text gives this condition no medical name and does not ask us to diagnose it. It lets us see only this: a man who thought he was a god became one who was not like a man. We need not use this picture to mock anyone, and still less to judge people near us who struggle in mind. This passage is a reminder to every one of us: when you begin to say “it was all me,” stop, and look up.</p>

<p><strong>עַיְנַי לִשְׁמַיָּא נִטְלֵת</strong> (I lifted up my eyes to heaven): <strong>נטל</strong> is “to lift.” His eyes had always been fixed on his own city and his own glory; now he raised his gaze from below to heaven.</p><p><strong>וּמַנְדְּעִי עֲלַי יְתוּב</strong> (my understanding returned to me): <strong>יְתוּב</strong> is “to turn back.” The order of the text is important: he first lifted his eyes, and then understanding came back. <p><strong>His understanding came back at the moment he lifted his eyes to heaven.</strong></p><p>After seven times, when the days were over, the text uses the simplest action: “I lifted up my eyes to heaven.” No ceremony, no great religious act, only a man raising his eyes from the ground. Think how hard this action is. He had been looking at his own city, his own achievement, his own glory; for seven times he had only bent his head to eat grass. Now he looked up. <strong>And understanding returned at that very moment.</strong></p><p>Notice the order: he did not first find his understanding and then bless; he first lifted his eyes, and then was restored. It is not that I get well first and then look to God, but that when I look up, I am healed. Today, is there something you have been bending your head over to handle? Perhaps it is not to solve it first, but to lift your head first.</p><p>Then his doxology: “his dominion is an everlasting dominion, and his kingdom from generation to generation.” This echoes the decree of verses 1 to 3, beginning and end. But here is one more sentence, the deepest: “All the inhabitants of the earth are reputed as nothing; and he does according to his will … and no one can stop his hand, or ask him, ‘What are you doing?’” This king once asked the three companions: “Who is that god that will deliver you out of my hands?” Now he says: no one can stop God’s hand. “My hand” and “his hand,” he has at last told apart.</p><p>One important reminder: we stay with what the text says, that he lifted his eyes, blessed, and confessed. As for his later life and eternal end, the text does not say, and we do not pass a verdict for God. We see only this: God was pleased to let a man, in the lowest place, lift up his head.</p>

<p><strong>מְשַׁבַּח וּמְרוֹמֵם וּמְהַדַּר</strong> (praise, extol, honor): three participles, present tense, not a one-time act but a continuing state. <p><strong>The last sentence of this chapter is the conclusion written by one who had been proud: “those who walk in pride he is able to abase.”</strong></p><p>First see how the text writes the king’s restoration: understanding returned, glory returned, and his counselors and lords came to seek him. I. A Witness Written from the End (4:1–18)
@@1649@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Let me first ask you to think of one thing: when was the last time someone praised you to your face? Perhaps your manager said, this project owes much to you; perhaps a friend said, without you I could not have held on. You were glad to hear it, and grateful. But in that instant, was there a voice in your heart that softly said: yes, I did this? In this lesson we read of a man who said that sentence at the top of his voice.
@@1650@@
Daniel chapter 4 begins in a very special way. Daniel 4:1 says: “Nebuchadnezzar the king, to all the peoples, nations, and languages, who dwell in all the earth: Peace be multiplied to you.” An ordinary decree is a king giving orders and setting rules; this one is a king giving witness, and from the end at that: “How great are his signs! How mighty are his wonders! His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom. His dominion is from generation to generation.” Do you remember chapter 3? He once set up a golden image and required the whole earth to bow; now he himself proclaims to the whole earth that only one kingdom is everlasting, and that kingdom is not his. How this king came to this place, he will tell us.
@@1651@@
Verse 4 says: “I, Nebuchadnezzar, was at rest in my house, and flourishing in my palace.” Notice the moment: not a time of failure but the best of times. The nation secure, the works complete, no visible threat. Just then he had a dream that made him afraid and troubled. The greatest unease often does not come from hardship but from times that are too smooth. He then did what he knew best: he summoned all the experts. The result was the same as in chapter 2: they could not. This time the king did not rage; he only admitted: human methods had again reached their end. Then he remembered Daniel.
@@1652@@
The first scene of the dream is a tree: “I saw, and behold, a tree in the middle of the earth; and its height was great. The tree grew, and was strong, and its height reached to the sky, and its sight to the end of all the earth.” Its leaves were beautiful and its fruit plentiful, the animals had shade under it, the birds lived in its branches, and all flesh was fed from it. Look: the first scene of this dream has no bad part. This king was not only a tyrant who oppressed people; his kingdom fed many and sheltered many. The text does not deny this. But this tree “reached to the sky,” a picture that brings to mind the tower of Babel. It did not grow badly; it grew to a height that did not belong to it. Pride often does not grow from bad places but from good ones: ability, achievement, being needed are all good, only when we forget that all of it was given, good turns into height.
@@1653@@
Then the second scene: a watcher came from heaven and cried aloud: “Cut down the tree, and cut off its branches! Shake off its leaves, and scatter its fruit!” But notice that the stump was left, the root still in the earth, bound with a band of iron and bronze. This judgment is not destruction; it is a judgment with something left. His heart was to be changed from man’s, and an animal’s heart given to him, so that seven times would pass over him. Why an animal’s heart? Because this man, in the heights, forgot he was a man; so God let him, in the least human way, learn again to be human. The last sentence of this passage is the key of the whole chapter: “that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will, and sets up over it the lowest of men.”
@@1654@@
The two questions share one way out: back before God. Your achievements, set them before him; others’ failures, set them before him too. Before him, no one is high or low, only those who were given, and the God who gives.
@@1655@@
Who is the “watcher” in the dream? Is it an angel?
@@1656@@
The original says “a watcher, a holy one, came down from heaven,” a word rarely used elsewhere in the Old Testament. Most interpreters understand him as a messenger from heaven, sent by God to carry the command of judgment; in 4:24 Daniel says it is “the decree of the Most High,” which also shows that the source of the command is God. The text gives no further description of this watcher’s identity, and we need not guess more details; we need only see that the judgment did not happen by chance, but that the God of heaven rules in the kingdom of men. You can also bring this question to your small group and read the text together, rather than guessing alone. In this chapter we can also learn: first hear what the text says, then ask what it asks of us.

II. Speaking the Truth, and Giving a Way Out (4:19–27)
@@1658@@
It was Daniel’s turn to speak. Verse 19 says: “Then Daniel, whose name was Belteshazzar, was stricken mute for a while, and his thoughts troubled him.” He was not a man without feeling. When he heard this dream, he knew at once that it was a judgment on the king. The king, seeing his face, comforted him instead: “Belteshazzar, don’t let the dream, or the interpretation, trouble you.” Daniel answered: “My lord, may the dream be for those who hate you, and its interpretation to your adversaries.” This is a very human sentence: he truly hoped the dream was not about the king. A true interpreter comes with tears to speak of judgment.
@@1659@@
Then he said very directly: “it is you, O king, that have grown and become strong.” No detours, no technique. He matched each part of the dream with the king’s life: grown and strong, that is you; greatness reaching to the sky, that is you; dominion to the end of the earth, that is you. Then the verdict: you will be driven from men, dwell with the animals of the field, eat grass as oxen, be wet with the dew of the sky, and seven times will pass over you. Notice that Daniel did not treat the verdict as his own matter; he said: “the decree of the Most High, which has come on my lord the king.” It was not his idea or his judgment; he only carried the word from heaven into the palace.
@@1660@@
The most important is the last verse. He did not stop at judgment; he gave the king a way: “O king, let my counsel be acceptable to you, and break off your sins by righteousness, and your iniquities by showing mercy to the poor. Perhaps there may be a lengthening of your tranquility.” Please hear this word: “perhaps.” He gave no guarantee and made no promise on God’s behalf; the judgment was set, yet the door of repentance remained open. To a mighty king he said: see the poor. This is the most concrete repentance: not more sacrifices, but more mercy.
@@1661@@
If one day you need to say a hard truth to someone, please remember Daniel: first grieve for the person, then speak clearly, and finally give him a way out. One who speaks the truth does not do it to win, nor to prove himself right, but so that the person still has a road to walk.
@@1662@@
This passage also reminds us that interpreting and counseling are two different things. To interpret is to state God’s word clearly; to counsel is to take on a share of responsibility for the other’s road. Daniel did both, and he did not blur his words because the other was a king. May we too have this courage and gentleness: when it needs saying, to say it; when it needs accompanying, to accompany.
@@1663@@
If I speak the truth and the other does not accept it, have I wasted my effort?
@@1664@@
No. After Daniel spoke, the king did not repent at once; twelve months later the judgment came. Daniel’s responsibility was to say it clearly and faithfully, and the result was in God’s hand. After you have spoken, you can keep praying for this person, and you need not feel you did wrong because he did not change. If you spoke with love, with a way out, and not to shame the other, you have already done what you should. Finally, remember that one who speaks the truth also needs to be accompanied, and should not carry it alone. Remind yourself too: not every time you speak the truth will you see an immediate result.

III. The Light of the Original: Know, Rule, Most High (4:17, 25, 32)
@@1666@@
Three words in this chapter recur and are worth taking apart. The first is know, the Aramaic יְדַע (yeda). Verse 17: “that the living may know”; verse 25: “until you know”; verse 32 says it once more. This word does not mean knowing a piece of information, but knowing through experience. The king was driven out and ate grass for seven times, for one purpose only: to make him “know.” God’s judgment is not for revenge, but so that a person truly comes to know him.
@@1667@@
The second is rule, the Aramaic שַׁלִּיט (shallit), meaning having sovereignty, having authority to govern. “The Most High rules in the kingdom of men”: this sentence appears three times in this chapter, and is said at last in the king’s confession in 4:34–35: “his dominion is an everlasting dominion, and his kingdom from generation to generation.” This is the central sentence of the whole book, and the same thing that the long Aramaic section, from chapter 2 to chapter 7, keeps saying.
@@1668@@
The third is Most High, the Aramaic עִלָּיָא (illaya), its root meaning “high.” Think about it: that tree “reached to the sky,” but the one truly on high is not the tree but the Most High. A man who wants to stand tall can only do so on the earth, and even reaching to the sky is only a tree; he is the one above. These three words together are the route of this chapter: to know, because he rules; he rules, because he is the Most High. Our position is given; our lesson is to know.
@@1669@@
These three words also answer a common question: why does God let a person go through such hard days? Because what he wants is not that we bow our heads, but that we “know.” Some lessons we cannot learn at a desk; we can learn them only in the lowest place. This does not say that all suffering is for teaching, but that God can, at any low place, bring us back to his side.
@@1670@@
Are the “seven times” seven years? Why is the commentary unsure?
@@1671@@
The original says only “seven times”; the Aramaic עִדָּנִין means “periods,” without saying whether days, months or years. Some read it as seven years, some as seven seasons or seven months, and some as a symbol of a full period. The text gives no answer, and we need not decide for it. What this chapter asks us to notice is not the length of time, but the condition for its end: until he knows that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men. So where things are uncertain, we would rather honestly say “the text does not say” than hurry to supply an answer. A mature believer is willing to say: this point, the text does not tell us, and we put it in God’s hands.
@@1672@@
Is the “spirit of the holy gods” speaking of the Holy Spirit?
@@1673@@
In verse 8 the king says that in Daniel is “the spirit of the holy gods”; the original is plural, literally “spirit of holy gods.” This is a foreign king using words familiar to him to state his observation, not a complete doctrinal confession. Some think the plural is a form of dignity pointing to the one God. This commentary does not treat this sentence as doctrinal proof of the person of the Holy Spirit; we see only that even a foreign king admitted that this man had a spirit beyond the human. We need not teach this one phrase as a complete doctrine, nor, because it is incomplete, ignore its witness. This attitude respects both the text and the person before us.
@@1674@@
IV. After Twelve Months, He Looked Up (4:28–37)
@@1675@@
The dream was given, the interpretation was given, and the counsel was given. But nothing happened. Verse 29 says: “At the end of twelve months.” One month, two months, half a year, a year. These twelve months were God’s patience, and also the most dangerous time for man: because nothing happened, people think it does not matter. God’s patience is often taken as God’s silence, and God’s silence is again taken as one’s own safety. Then he walked in the royal palace of Babylon, looked over his city, and said: “Is not this great Babylon, which I have built for the royal dwelling place, by the might of my power and for the glory of my majesty?”
@@1676@@
Please count: I, my power, my majesty, my glory. In this one sentence there is only one protagonist. This is not an evil man speaking evil, but a very successful man speaking the truth: the city really was built by him; the works really were completed by him. But he turned a position given into my achievement. This is the most exact definition of pride: leaving the place of man, and taking God’s glory for oneself. Verse 31 says: “While the word was in the king’s mouth, a voice came from the sky.” God did not wait for him to finish, because the sentence had already been finished. Then the text uses one verse to write the end: he was driven out, ate grass as oxen, his hair grew like eagles’ feathers, his nails like birds’ claws.
@@1677@@
Notice that the text gives this condition no medical name and does not ask us to diagnose it. It lets us see only this: a man who thought he was a god became one who was not like a man. We need not use this picture to mock anyone, and still less to judge people near us who struggle in mind. This passage is a reminder to every one of us: when you begin to say “it was all me,” stop, and look up.
@@1678@@
Then comes the quietest verse of the whole chapter: “At the end of the days I, Nebuchadnezzar, lifted up my eyes to heaven, and my understanding returned to me, and I blessed the Most High.” No ceremony, no great religious act, only a man raising his eyes from the ground. He had been looking at his own city and his own achievement; for seven times he had only bent his head to eat grass. Now he looked up, and understanding returned at that very moment. Notice the order: he did not first find his understanding and then bless; he first lifted his eyes, and then was restored. It is not that I get well first and then look to God, but that when I look up, I am healed.
@@1679@@
The last verse is the conclusion written by one who had been proud: “those who walk in pride he is able to abase.” This sentence was not said to him by someone else; he said it of himself. This is a witness with no defense. As for his later life and eternal end, the text does not say, and we do not pass a verdict for God. We see only this: God was pleased to let a man, in the lowest place, lift up his head.
@@1680@@
Please bring this picture back into your own life: have you had a moment when “the days were over,” and only then found you had been bending your head all along? What many people need is not more information or methods but a moment of looking up. Some find it in a hospital corridor, some on an afternoon of unemployment, some on a very quiet night. Whichever it is, the text says: God waits for that one action. He does not wait for us to be perfect; he waits for us to look up.
@@1681@@
What if I am right now in the days of “bending my head to eat grass”?
@@1682@@
You need not wait until the days are over to ask. The point of the text is the lifting of the eyes: a very small action, yet the turning point. In the lowest place you can look up and say one sentence: “God, you are King.” You need not say it beautifully, nor first put yourself in order. You can also ask a trusted companion to walk this road with you, and do not take this season as evidence that God has abandoned you; in the text, the stump was kept. Remember, his stump was always kept; so is yours, and God has never given up on you. So you need not hurry to climb out of the low place; you can first come to know God here.
@@1683@@
The first deviation is forcing “seven times” into seven years and matching it with some year, event or person today. The original says only seven “times,” without saying whether days, months or years. What this chapter asks us to hear is “that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men,” not a timetable. If we turn this chapter into material for counting days, we go from bowing the head to turning pages.
@@1684@@
The second deviation is describing the king’s illness as some medical diagnosis, or using it to mock or judge those who struggle in mind. The text describes only “ate grass as oxen, hair like eagles’ feathers,” and gives no medical name. This passage is a reminder to all the proud, not a comment on any patient. We need to have compassion on those who suffer, not use them as examples for this chapter.
@@1685@@
The third deviation is turning 4:27, “righteousness, mercy to the poor,” into a way of exchanging good deeds for peace or forgiveness. Daniel describes what repentance looks like, and says “perhaps,” with no guarantee; being forgiven is God’s grace, and doing good is the fruit of repentance. Nor are the poor excluded by God because they have not done enough.
@@1686@@
The fourth deviation is concluding from 4:34–37 that Nebuchadnezzar was saved and became a believer, or asserting that he was not saved. The text records only that he lifted his eyes, praised and confessed, and does not state his eternal end. We stay with what the text says and do not pass a verdict for God.
@@1687@@
The fifth deviation is turning this chapter into “successful people will surely be humbled by God.” The text is not against success, nor does it say that achievement is sin; the tree itself was not wrong. The problem is turning what was given into “mine.” Humility is not refusing success, but knowing, when successful, where it came from.
@@1688@@
Does achievement make one prone to pride? Must humility mean deliberately making oneself small?
@@1689@@
No. Daniel also had a very high position and ability, yet he said, “not because of any wisdom of mine”; he did not make himself small, he only returned the glory. Humility is not underestimating oneself, but putting things in the right place: ability is given, position is given, and the glory of the result still belongs to God. You can honestly say: I did this well, and also say: this was God’s grace to me. Mature humility sees clearly what one has, and also sees clearly where it came from. Today you might also find one small thing no one saw, do it quietly well, and quietly hand it to God.
@@1690@@
The first question: we are used to “achievement” being a person’s calling card. In Chinese society, who you are, what you have done, which school your child entered often decides how others see you and how you see yourself. Many live inside an invisible comparison: not daring to admit that they are not good enough, and not daring to admit that they did well, for fear of being laughed at or called proud. This chapter gives a way out: you need not deny your achievements, nor lean on them to hold yourself up. You can say: this was given by God; and then quietly, with dignity, return the glory.
@@1691@@
The second question: it is easy, when others fall, to feel “this is the end of his pride.” Someone falls ill, someone loses a job, someone’s marriage breaks, and those nearby say: he was just too proud. Such words do not fit this chapter, and they wound. The text asks us to look at ourselves first, not at others. The story of Nebuchadnezzar was written by himself; no one drew the conclusion for him. So when facing others’ failure, we can accompany and pray; when facing our own success, we can look up and say: you gave this.
@@1692@@
I am in an environment where an elder or a manager is very forceful. How can I speak the truth as Daniel did?
@@1693@@
Daniel’s way of speaking the truth was to respect first, then be direct, and then give a way out: he called the king “my lord,” he said “may the dream be for those who hate you,” he said it clearly, and he left the door of repentance open. In a forceful environment you can first choose the right time and place, and speak privately and quietly; first acknowledge what the other has given, then say what you have seen; and finally offer one concrete way forward. In a situation that could bring danger, please first seek safe support, and do not carry it alone. If you are not sure how to say it, you can first write down what you want to say, and ask a companion to look it over. On the premise of safety and respect, your courage is wisdom, not rashness.
@@1694@@
VII. Living Out 321—Lift Your Eyes to Heaven, Give the Glory to God
@@1695@@
The three foundations: Jesus is my Role Model. He was in the form of God, yet did not consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant; he said: “For everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and whoever humbles himself will be exalted.” The Bible is my Standard, because what this chapter asks us to know is not a story but a truth: the Most High rules in the kingdom of men. The Holy Spirit is my Guide, because looking up is not by willpower but by one within who reminds me: you are King.
@@1696@@
The two core values: Let Jesus be King means that at the moment I say “it was all me,” I lift up my eyes and say: you are King. Let Jesus receive all the glory means that in success and when praised, we neither take it for ourselves nor feign modesty, but honestly say: you gave this. The one purpose: to build God’s system. In God’s system everyone’s position is given, so the great do not look down on the small, and the small do not envy the great; when someone is lifted up, someone reminds him; when someone bows his head, someone accompanies him.
@@1697@@
Changing concepts changes life: when we think a position is “what I won,” our life is tense; when we know a position is “given,” our life can relax. From selfhood to selflessness: the person with self says “is not this great Babylon what I built,” the selfless person says “this was given by the King of heaven.” Selflessness is not nothingness, but no longer giving the glory to oneself.
@@1698@@
In the family, this means that parents do not take their children’s grades as their own glory, nor their children’s failures as their own shame. In the workplace, this means that supervisors do not take the team’s results for themselves, and colleagues do not scramble for credit in order to be seen. In the church, this means that we do not make the results of our service our calling card, but after each service say together: this is what the Lord did.
@@1699@@
How do I practice “no longer giving the glory to myself” in daily life?
@@1700@@
You can begin with a very small habit: each time you are praised, first say “thank you,” and then quietly say in your heart “this was given by God.” You can also, each night before sleep, write down three things for which you were praised or thanked that day, and hand them back to God one by one. After a while you will find that you no longer need to be praised so much, nor fear criticism so much, because your worth is no longer placed in others’ mouths. A month later you can look back and see at which times your heart was quieter. This small practice will slowly loosen the hand your heart has kept clenched.
@@1701@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Tree Cut Down
@@1702@@
The proud type says: “I earned this by my effort, and I have the right to say so.” This may be true: you really did work hard. But please add another sentence beside it: “This was an opportunity God gave me.” Put the two sentences side by side, and you will be freer.
@@1703@@
The fearful type says: “If I do not hold on, everything will collapse.” What you fear may be the tree being cut down. But please notice that the text says the stump was left. God’s judgment is not to wipe you out, but to bring you back to the right place. You can let go, because it is not you alone who holds up this world.
@@1704@@
The ashamed type says: “I too once said such things; I have no right to look up.” Nebuchadnezzar spoke the proudest words and became the lowest of men, yet he still lifted his eyes, and was still restored. You need not be perfect first to look up; looking up is the first step home.
