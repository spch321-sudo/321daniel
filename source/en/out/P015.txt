<p><strong>סְתֹם הַדְּבָרִים וַחֲתֹם הַסֵּפֶר</strong>: “shut up the words, and seal the book”: two commands, both terms of ancient document storage; to seal is not to discard but to preserve, waiting for the time of opening.</p><p><strong>יְשֹׁטְטוּ רַבִּים וְתִרְבֶּה הַדָּעַת</strong>: “many will run back and forth, and knowledge will be increased”; <strong>שׁוּט</strong> (to roam) can also mean “to search, to examine closely,” so there are different readings through the ages; see the issues.</p>
@@4952@@
<p><strong>The book is to be sealed; and sealing is not throwing away but preserving, waiting for the time God sets.</strong></p><p>Verse 4 of chapter 12 is a turning point of the whole book: the messenger commands Daniel to shut up the words and seal the book. In the ancient world, to seal was to keep a document safe so that no one could alter it at will, waiting for the time it should be opened. This verse lets us see that God’s arrangement for his own words has its own time.</p><p>Then the text says: many will run back and forth, and knowledge will be increased. This verse is often used to speak of “technological progress in the last days”; but the original “run back and forth” can also be read “examine closely, search,” so there are different readings through the ages: some think it means people everywhere seeking out and studying the contents of this book so that understanding grows; some think it means the growth of knowledge in general; some think both. We set them side by side among the issues. We do not turn this verse into a prediction about technology or the times, and do not use it to draw up days.</p><p>For us today this verse holds a practical invitation: this book has not been sealed forever; today it has been placed in our hands. We can look it over back and forth, and in looking come to know God. When you read this book, please read not to gain more information but to know more deeply the God who rules history.</p>

These notes do not convert it into years or days and do not use it to draw up tables; see the issues.</p>

<p><strong>Two stand by the river and ask the question we also want to ask: how long?</strong></p><p>In verse 5 of chapter 12 the camera goes back to the river. The man clothed in linen stands above the waters; two others stand, one on this bank and one on that. One of them asks the last question of the whole book: “How long will it be to the end of these wonders?” You may find this sentence familiar: “how long” is a cry often found in the Psalms, the cry of everyone waiting in the dark.</p><p>The man clothed in linen lifts both hands to heaven and swears by the one who lives forever. It is a very solemn picture: the answer does not come from human calculation but is placed in God’s hand in an oath. The answer: a time, times, and a half, until the breaking in pieces of the power of the holy people is finished. This expression is the same as in 7:25; the readings are many, and we set them side by side among the issues. These notes do not convert it into years and do not tie it to any event of today.</p><p>Please notice one thing: this answer has no date, but it has a guarantee: one who lives forever has sworn. Is what you want a timetable, or a God you can trust? This chapter leads us from the former to the latter.</p>

<p><strong>וַאֲנִי שָׁמַעְתִּי וְלֹא אָבִין</strong>: “I heard, but I didn’t understand”; <strong>בִּין</strong> (understand) is an important word of the whole book; in 9:2 and 10:1 Daniel understood, and here he says frankly: I do not understand.</p><p><strong>לֵךְ דָּנִיֵּאל</strong> (go your way, Daniel): <strong>לֵךְ</strong> is an imperative, the same word as “go your way” in 12:13; in the last chapter there are two “go your way.”</p>

<p><strong>This old man who had understood many things says frankly: I heard, but I didn’t understand; and God’s answer is: go your way.</strong></p><p>Verse 8 of chapter 12 holds a very honest sentence: “I heard, but I didn’t understand.” It is Daniel. The man who understood in chapter 9 as he read Scripture, who set his heart on understanding in chapter 10, here says frankly: I do not understand. The text does not hide this and does not say Daniel was therefore not spiritual enough. Even he had times of not understanding.</p><p>Then God’s answer gives him no explanation but: “Go your way, Daniel.” This “go” is not being sent away but being entrusted: these words are shut up and sealed until the time of the end; now is not the time when you need to understand everything but the time when you need to keep walking. This is a deep comfort: not understanding does not stop us from walking the way God gives.</p><p>We too often have such moments: we read the Bible, we pray, and still do not understand why this, why that. This verse gives us an honest space: we can say, I do not understand; and we can hear God say: go your way. Between these two sentences there is a faith that need not understand everything yet can go forward in peace.</p>

<p><strong>The answer at the end is not a timetable but a process: being purified, made white, refined.</strong></p><p>Verse 10 of chapter 12 answers 11:35. There it said that some of the wise fall, to refine the rest and make them pure and white; here it says that many will purify themselves and be refined. The same set of words, the same line: what God does in history is not only to deal with kings and kingdoms but to carry out a work of purifying among his people.</p><p>Notice that these three verbs are reflexive or passive: be purified, be made white, be refined. This lets us see that purity does not come from ourselves, still less from comparison; it is brought out in God’s hand. This is very close to the “no-self” of 321: we do not make ourselves clean; we are willing to be refined by God.</p><p>Then the text divides people into two kinds: the wicked still do wickedly and do not understand; the wise understand. This “understanding” is not the amount of knowledge but direction: those willing to be refined gradually understand; those unwilling understand less and less as they go. So what the text gives us is not a calculated timetable but a road we can walk today: being willing to be purified and refined.</p>

@@4964@@
<p><strong>Of the two numbers of days given at the end, neither can be used for calculating; but the word given last is one we can live out: blessed is the one who waits.</strong></p><p>Verses 11 and 12 of chapter 12 are the two verses most easily misused in the whole book. The text says that from the time the continual burnt offering is taken away and the abomination that makes desolate is set up, there will be one thousand two hundred ninety days; blessed is he who waits and comes to the one thousand three hundred thirty-five days. Through the ages the explanations of these two numbers have been very many: some explain from the historical profaning of the sanctuary, some from end-time events, and some see them as symbolic numbers. We set them side by side, and say plainly: these notes do not calculate or convert, do not turn them into any timetable, and do not use them to identify people and events of today.</p><p>Look at where these two verses really land: not on the number of days but on one word: “blessed.” Blessed is the one who waits. The “waiting” of the original is a watching with hope. This word has similar uses in the Psalms and in Isaiah. What the whole book finally gives God’s people is not a date but a posture: waiting with hope.</p><p>We need to be careful: the two numbers are not to make us more tense but more quiet. Any use that turns these numbers into panic, prediction or judgment of others departs from the purpose of the text. The fruit of the text is patience; and Jesus also said that no one knows that day and hour. So we do not count days; we wait.</p>

<p><strong>וְאַתָּה לֵךְ לַקֵּץ וְתָנוּחַ</strong>: “go your way until the end; for you will rest”; <strong>לֵךְ</strong> (go), <strong>קֵץ</strong> (end), <strong>נוּחַ</strong> (rest): three words that gather the undercurrent of the whole book into one sentence.</p><p><strong>וְתַעֲמֹד לְגֹרָלְךָ</strong>: “you will stand in your inheritance”; <strong>עמד</strong> (to stand): the undercurrent of the whole book, appearing for the last time; <strong>גּוֹרָל</strong> (lot, portion) is a word used in Joshua for the dividing of the land: the inheritance God assigns to you.</p>
@@4967@@
<p><strong>The last sentence of the whole book is said to one old man: go your way, you will rest; and at the end of the days you will stand in your inheritance.</strong></p><p>The last sentence of the whole book of Daniel is not a proclamation to the whole world but is said to one person: “But go your way until the end; for you will rest, and will stand in your inheritance at the end of the days.” In this sentence there are three movements: go, rest, stand.</p><p>The first is “go your way.” This is the same word as “go your way” in 12:9. God wants him to keep walking, carrying his not-understanding. The second is “rest”: this word is not only a gentle way of saying death but also means “to find rest.” This old man went through many years of Babylon and Persia; his life was not a story of victory but a story of faithfulness; God says to him: you will rest. The third is “stand.” It is the same word as Michael’s “stand up” in 12:1: to stand; he was a man who stood before kings, and at last he too will stand and receive his portion.</p><p>Notice: this sentence does not say “then you will know everything,” and gives him no date; it gives him only three things: a road, a rest, a hope of standing. This is the conclusion of the whole book: not a heap of answers but the promise of a God who can be trusted. To you who are waiting, this sentence is also said: go, rest, stand.</p>

I. An Old Man and a Figure Who Stands Up (12:1)
@@5251@@
Peace to you, brothers and sisters. Today is our last lesson in the book of Daniel. I would like to begin by asking you to imagine an old man: he walked through Babylon, he walked through Persia, he saw several kings rise and fall; he grew up in the king’s palace, spent a night in the lions’ den, and mourned three weeks by the river. Now he comes to the last vision of his life. What do you think he most wanted to hear? A complete timetable? An answer that would untie all his questions? Today’s chapter tells us: no. What he received was a very quiet sentence.
@@5252@@
This chapter begins with a “stand up”: “At that time Michael will stand up, the great prince who stands for the children of your people; and there will be a time of trouble, such as never was since there was a nation even to that same time.” Do you remember? The word “stand” has been moving beneath the surface since chapter 1: the young men stood before the king, the three friends stood before the golden image, Daniel stood by the lions’ den. In chapter 10 the messenger said he had stood up to support; in chapter 11 no king could stand. Now, in the last chapter, it is Michael, the great prince of the children of your people, who stands up.
@@5253@@
When is this “at that time”? It has long been read in different ways: some say it follows the stretch of darkness described in chapter 11; some say it is a still later time; and some say history and the end reflect each other here. We set these readings side by side, and must say plainly: these notes set no date for this “at that time” and do not tie it to any event of today. In Matthew 24 Jesus also used similar words to describe great tribulation; we respect this link, but do not use it to draw up a table.
@@5254@@
The text gives two things, set against each other. The first is trouble: “such as never was.” This is a heavy word; we need not pretend darkness does not exist. The second is deliverance: “At that time your people will be delivered, everyone who is found written in the book.” Please notice: Michael stands up not to make the trouble vanish but so that the people may be delivered in the trouble. God’s people were never promised that they would not pass through darkness; what they were promised is a great prince who stands beside them.
@@5255@@
So the first reminder of this lesson may be this: in your darkest moment, please look up and see a figure who stands up. You need not stand firm by yourself, because there is one who stands for you. This is also the main melody of the whole book: the God of heaven rules, and his people, in other people’s kingdoms, still have someone standing beside them.
@@5256@@
Perhaps you want to ask: if such a great prince stands up, why does my trouble still not end at once? This question is very real, and the text does not avoid it. It only tells us gently: the one who stands up is the one who is with you; his standing is not so that you will not pass through darkness but so that in the darkness you are not alone. This is a promise we can take hold of again every day.
@@5257@@
Finally, please take this picture home: an old man sitting by a river hears someone say, “your people will be delivered.” He did not receive more answers, but he received more assurance. May you too, by your own river today, hear this sentence: there is one who stands up for you.
@@5258@@
“Found written in the book”: how can I be sure my name is on it?
@@5259@@
The point of this verse is comfort, not making people anxiously check themselves. Different theological readings of “written in the book” have long existed, and we set them side by side among the issues; but the New Testament tells us clearly that God remembers everyone who trusts in Christ and leaves no one out. You can practice this: each morning say one sentence to God: Lord, my name is in your hand, and today I will trust you. When you find yourself doubting because of weakness or failure, do not hurry to prove yourself by your performance; return to the great prince who stands up, and to the salvation Jesus accomplished for you, and let assurance rest on him and not on your feelings.
@@5260@@
II. Awake and Shine (12:2–3)
@@5261@@
Then the text lights up one word: “Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt.” This is one of the clearest Old Testament verses on resurrection. Death is called “sleep”; and sleep is something one wakes from. Think of chapter 11: the wise fell by the sword, in flame, into captivity and plunder. They made many understand, and paid with their lives. Their story at the end of chapter 11 is all heaviness. But in chapter 12 God answers them: you will awake. Falling is not the end.
@@5262@@
The verse goes on to say that some will have everlasting life, and some shame and everlasting contempt. This sentence is heavy; we should not pass over it lightly, nor turn it into a tool of fear. The scope of “many” has long been read in different ways, and the theological understanding of “everlasting contempt” also differs; we set them side by side among the issues. We first hold on to the most direct point: death is not the end, and God has a final justice and also a final hope. The New Testament too teaches resurrection, in John 5 and Acts 24; we need not build a complete end-time chart on this verse.
@@5263@@
Then verse 3, which is one of my own favorites: “Those who are wise will shine as the brightness of the expanse. Those who turn many to righteousness will shine as the stars forever and ever.” This “wise” is the same word as in chapter 11. In chapter 11 they fall; in chapter 12 they shine. The same word, two scenes: on earth, they fall; in heaven, they shine. Failure in men’s eyes is, in God’s eyes, the brightness of the sky.
@@5264@@
Notice how the text describes these people. Not “those of highest standing” and not “those of greatest achievement,” but “those who turn many to righteousness,” that is, those who turn people toward righteousness, toward God. Such a person is not necessarily a pastor and does not necessarily stand on a platform. A mother who prays for her children all her life; a colleague who bears witness to God by honest work; an elder who still blesses others from a sickbed: all of them are in this description.
@@5265@@
Stars do not shine by themselves; they are set by God in the sky to guide people in the night. So we need not be proud, and need not be discouraged. Our task is not to shine the brightest but, in the night, not to go out. For whom can you be a little light today?
@@5266@@
Please also think of the wise whom the world has not remembered: a person quietly praying for coworkers in a factory, a person singing hymns for the patient in the next bed, a son or daughter caring for a parent with dementia over many years. Their light may never be praised by anyone, yet God remembers each one. This verse is written for them, and for you if you are on such a road.
@@5267@@
Think again: for whom can you be that little light today? Perhaps a phone call, a presence that does not preach, a prayer offered with no return. These small things, in heaven, are all stars.
@@5268@@
How does the hope of resurrection comfort someone who has lost a loved one?
@@5269@@
Accompany first, comfort afterward. Someone who has just lost a loved one usually does not need a long theology; he needs someone beside him. You can sit quietly first and let the other tell the story of that person, and when needed simply hand him a glass of water. When the other has strength and is willing, then gently read 12:2 together: many of those who sleep in the dust will awake. Treat this sentence as a hope being shared, not a conclusion one is required to accept. Remember too: grief is real and hope is real, and the two can exist together. This practice need not be rushed; take it slowly, and God is present in every honest beginning.
@@5270@@
III. Light from the Original: Stand, Purified, Blessed, Rest (12:1, 10, 12, 13)
@@5271@@
In the original of the last chapter there are four words worth a slow reading. The first is “stand,” Hebrew עמד (amad). The undercurrent of the whole book appears here for the last time: Michael “stands up” (12:1), the two by the river “stood” (12:5), and Daniel at last will “stand” (12:13). From the young men standing before the king in chapter 1 to the old man standing to receive his portion in the last chapter, this word strings the whole book on one line: standing is the final posture of God’s people.
@@5272@@
The second is “be purified, be made white, be refined,” Hebrew בָּרַר, לָבַן, צָרַף, three verbs that in 12:10 are all reflexive or passive in form; and the same set of words as in 11:35. This lets us see that purity does not come from ourselves, still less from comparison; it is brought out in God’s hand. This is very close to the “no-self” of 321: we do not make ourselves clean; we are willing to be refined by God.
@@5273@@
The third is “blessed,” Hebrew אַשְׁרֵי (ashrei). It is the word that opens the Psalms: “Blessed is…” In 12:12 it is joined with “waits” (חָכָה): blessed is the one who waits. This “waiting” is not idle waiting but a watching with hope. What the whole book finally gives God’s people is not a day but a posture.
@@5274@@
The fourth is “rest,” Hebrew נוּחַ (nuach). It is not only a gentle way of saying death; it also means “to find rest,” and in Genesis Noah’s name is related to it, meaning “comfort” and “rest.” In 12:13 God says to Daniel: you will rest. This word brings the whole book back to a gentle end.
@@5275@@
When we join these four words, we see a very complete road: first standing, the final posture of God’s people; then being purified, the work God does in his people; then blessed, the state of the one who waits; and last rest, an old man’s gentlest end. These four words are not four doctrines but the shape of a person’s whole life.
@@5276@@
“Blessed is he who waits”: does this mean we are to wait passively?
@@5277@@
No. This “waiting” is an active trust. Imagine a mother at the airport waiting for a child returning from far away: she is not doing nothing; she prepares the meal, tidies the room, looks up and watches. Waiting for God is the same: we keep reading Scripture, praying, loving others, doing what is before us, and at the same time place the time of the end in God’s hand. What is passive is the calculation of days; what is active is holding to trust in daily life. Such waiting is blessed waiting. Please remember, this is not a lesson finished at once but a journey that needs patience and also companions.
@@5278@@
“Being refined” sounds painful. How can I face it?
@@5279@@
You can admit honestly: being refined is usually not comfortable. We need not pretend to like it. But notice that the text uses the passive voice: be purified, be made white, be refined, which means that the one doing this is God, not you. All you need to do is not run from that hand. In the most painful moments you can pray simply: Lord, I do not understand, but I will not leave your hand. At the same time, please find a safe companion and say your pain aloud, and if needed seek professional help. Refining does not mean you grit your teeth alone. What God values is not how well you perform but that you are willing to come back to him with your real self.
@@5280@@
IV. I Heard, But I Didn’t Understand (12:4–9)
@@5281@@
Then the text enters a very honest passage. The messenger commands Daniel: “shut up the words, and seal the book, even to the time of the end.” In the ancient world, to seal was to keep a document safe, waiting for the time it should be opened. Then the text says: many will run back and forth, and knowledge will be increased. This sentence is often used to speak of technological progress in the last days; but the original “run back and forth” can also be read “examine closely,” so there are different readings through the ages; we set them side by side and do not turn it into a prediction about the times.
@@5282@@
Then the camera goes to the river: two others, one on this side and one on that; in the middle, the one clothed in linen. One of them asks the last question of the whole book: “How long will it be to the end of these wonders?” You must know this question well; it is the cry of the Psalms, the cry of everyone waiting in the dark: how long. The man clothed in linen lifts his right hand and his left hand to heaven and swears by the one who lives forever, and gives an answer: a time, times, and a half.
@@5283@@
Through the ages there have been many readings of this answer; we do not convert it into years and do not arrange it into any timetable. But please notice: this answer has no date, yet it has a guarantee: one who lives forever has sworn. Do you want a timetable, or a God you can trust? This chapter leads us from the former to the latter.
@@5284@@
Then comes the most honest sentence of the chapter: “I heard, but I didn’t understand.” The speaker is Daniel, the man who understood in chapter 9 as he read Scripture, who set his heart on understanding in chapter 10. Here he says frankly: I do not understand. The text does not hide this and does not say he was therefore not spiritual enough. Even he had times of not understanding.
@@5285@@
God’s answer is: “Go your way, Daniel.” This is not being sent away but being entrusted: now is not the time when you need to understand everything but the time when you need to keep walking. Not understanding does not stop us from walking the way God gives. This is the deepest comfort the whole book gives us.
@@5286@@
If today you have something you do not understand, please do not hurry to find an answer to fill it. You can say honestly, as Daniel did: Lord, I heard, but I did not understand. Then listen quietly as he says: go your way. This conversation can happen in our lives again and again, and each time it happens it brings us a little closer to him.
@@5287@@
I keep failing to understand when I read the Bible. Is something wrong with my spiritual life?
@@5288@@
No. Even Daniel said “I heard, but I didn’t understand.” Not understanding does not mean you are not spiritual; it reminds us that some things need time, and also community. You can do this: mark the places you do not understand with a pencil; do not force yourself to understand right now; ask a brother or sister you trust, or read together in a group; then keep doing the part you already understand. God often makes us understand little by little only as we “go.” When we do not understand, still going is itself faith. May you in this process be held up little by little, and learn little by little to hold up others.
@@5289@@
The first deviation is using “a time, times, and a half” in 12:7 and the days of 12:11–12 to convert into years, months and days and draw up an end-time timetable. These numbers have long been read in many ways, historical, end-time and symbolic; these notes set them side by side and state plainly that we do not calculate, convert or draw up tables.
@@5290@@
The second deviation is using the great trouble of 12:1 to identify some event or person of today, saying that it is the great tribulation or the antichrist. The point of the text is that in the trouble a great prince stands, and all found written in the book are delivered; these notes identify no event or person of today.
@@5291@@
The third deviation is making the “everlasting contempt” of 12:2 a tool to frighten people or to judge that someone has already been condemned. This verse speaks of the final justice, and theologians have understood it in different ways; we pass final sentence on no one.
@@5292@@
The fourth deviation is simply equating “knowledge will be increased” in 12:4 with technological progress, and using this to predict the end times. “Run back and forth” can also be read as examining closely and searching; there are different readings, and we set them side by side.
@@5293@@
The fifth deviation is saying to someone who is grieving, “he is asleep, you need not be sad,” or using 12:2–3 to stop his tears. The hope of resurrection is real, but grief is real too; we accompany first, and when the other has strength we read together.
@@5294@@
Someone is eager to predict the end times with this chapter. How should I respond?
@@5295@@
You can first affirm his zeal for God’s word, then gently return to the text’s own point. You can ask three questions: First, does the text itself give a date? Second, have interpreters through the ages read it differently? Third, does this claim make the congregation love the Lord more and be more patient, or more anxious? Then bring the atmosphere back to 12:13: go your way, you will rest, you will stand. You need not win over the other and need not persuade everyone; you can let your own life of peace, patience and love be the quietest answer. If the other still insists, treat him with love and leave the arguing to the Holy Spirit.
@@5296@@
The first question: we are very used to finding security by “calculating.” Calculating fortunes, calculating days, calculating feng shui, calculating investments. This habit also easily comes into faith: we always want to know when, how, whether it has already come. This chapter answers us in one sentence: go your way until the end. When we turn the strength we spend calculating into strength for knowing God, our security moves from numbers to a God who swears and lives forever.
@@5297@@
The second question: we are used to being silent before death. Many Chinese families do not talk much about death or resurrection, and pass over it with “just live well.” This chapter gives us a space in which we can talk: death is “sleep,” and sleep is something one wakes from; falling is not the end. In a safe time you can read 12:2–3 gently together with your family, and say to one another: we are not afraid to talk about this, because there is hope.
@@5298@@
How can I talk about death and resurrection with family who do not yet believe?
@@5299@@
You can begin from love, not from doctrine. Find a quiet, natural time, perhaps when the family mentions a relative who has died; you can say one sentence: I miss him too, and I believe that one day we will meet again. Then do not hurry to say much; let the other respond. If the other is willing to listen, then share the hope you read in Daniel: death is sleep, and sleep is something one wakes from. Do not use fear and do not force; let this hope be a gift that can be received or can be set aside. A little at a time is enough; being willing to begin is already saying to God: I trust you.
@@5300@@
VII. Living Out 321: Go Your Way, You Will Rest, You Will Stand
@@5301@@
Three foundations: Jesus is my example. He fell into the dust, rose on the third day, and became the firstfruits of those who sleep; he also said that of that day and hour no one knows. The Bible is my standard, so that before the many hard numbers of this chapter we rely not on calculation and not on identifying but hold only to the clearest point of the text: the end belongs to God. The Holy Spirit is my guide, who in our not-understanding still leads us on, so that we are purified and refined and, in the night, do not go out.
@@5302@@
Two cores: Let Jesus reign, which means placing the end in his hand, not counting his days for him, and, when we do not understand, still going and still waiting. Let Jesus receive all the glory, which means that when we shine we say: this is not my light, it is his light; and when we stand up and receive our portion we say: all of this is his grace. One purpose: to build God’s system. This is a people who wait: purifying one another and turning many to righteousness; accompanying first when someone falls; and together keeping the witness this book leaves behind.
@@5303@@
Change the concept and the life changes: when we think “security comes from working out the time exactly,” our life is anxious; when we know “security comes from a God who swears and lives forever,” our life has rest. From the self-life to the no-self life: the self-life demands to understand everything; the no-self life admits “I do not understand” yet is willing to go, letting man be man and God be God.
@@5304@@
We also need to remind ourselves: 321 is not for finding a beautiful answer to this chapter’s difficulties but for keeping our heart within them. Jesus is my example, the Bible is my standard, the Holy Spirit is my guide; when we do not understand, these three foundations are the stones under our feet. Stepping on them, we can keep going until that day.
@@5305@@
What does “no-self” have to do with “waiting”?
@@5306@@
Waiting is itself a practice of no-self. The self-life cannot bear not knowing and always grabs something it can control, such as dates, predictions, identifications; the no-self life can remain at peace in not knowing, because it has returned the authority of control to God. You can try a simple practice: each time you want to “know more,” stop first and say to God: Lord, I place the time and the end of this matter in your hand. Then do one small thing that is before you. Then waiting turns from torment into a practice of trust.
@@5307@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Heart, One Promise
@@5308@@
The proud type says: “I must find out when and how it will happen, so that I can be at peace.” This heart may be afraid of being kept in the dark. But this chapter says the end belongs to God, and even Daniel said he did not understand. You can ask yourself: is my finding out for loving the Lord, or for seizing the lead?
@@5309@@
The fearful type says: “I am afraid that when the great trouble comes I will not hold on, and I fear for my family.” This fear is real. Please remember the text says that in the trouble a great prince stands up, and everyone found written in the book will be delivered. You are not holding on by yourself; you are held up. You can reduce the information that makes you panic and leave a little more time to be quietly with God.
@@5310@@
The shame type says: “I have read the Bible for so many years and still do not understand; I must not be good enough.” Even Daniel said he did not understand; you are not alone. You need not first become very understanding to deserve God’s charge; what God says is: go your way. You can bring this shame before God, and before a safe companion.
@@5311@@
The hurt type says: “I paid a price for my faith and no one remembers.” This wound is real. The text says the fall of the wise is remembered before God, and they will shine. We do not make the wound lighter, and do not ask you to let go at once; you can slowly find a safe person and tell your story. God sees, and remembers.
@@5312@@
First, each day pray one sentence: I do not understand, but I am willing to go; and hand one waiting matter to God.
@@5313@@
Second, write down one thing for which you want to find security by “working it out,” and beside it write one sentence: the end belongs to God.
@@5314@@
Third, sit for a while with someone who is waiting or has lost a loved one; accompany first, do not explain for God, and do not frighten with numbers of days.
@@5315@@
Fourth, each day do one small thing that brings someone nearer to God, such as one word of encouragement or one prayer; this is turning people to righteousness.
@@5316@@
Fifth, when someone wants to use this chapter to draw up a timetable or identify the antichrist, gently bring him back to 12:13: we only need to go, to rest and to stand.
@@5317@@
I declare: the end belongs to God; I do not count his days for him; I only wait for him.
@@5318@@
I declare: when I do not understand, I am still willing to go, because he says to me: go your way.
@@5319@@
I declare: I will be purified and refined, not go out in the night, and turn many to God.

In twelve lessons we have walked through the book of Daniel. Chapter 1: a young man who purposed not to defile himself with the king’s food; chapter 2: the stone cut out without hands; chapter 3: “But if not”; chapter 4: the Most High rules in the kingdom of men; chapter 5: you are weighed in the balances; chapter 6: praying as he had done before; chapter 7: the Ancient of Days and the son of man; chapters 8 to 11: kings and kings coming and going, God first touching, and the people who know God being strong and taking action. Now we come to the last sentence: “But go your way until the end; for you will rest, and will stand in your inheritance at the end of the days.”
@@5322@@
Brothers and sisters, let us return to that old man. He did not receive a timetable, but he received a promise: go, rest, stand. The focus of this lesson is Jesus: he is the great prince who stands up, he is the firstfruits of those who sleep, he is the place of all our hope. The focus is not how much I understand, nor how frightening others are, but the God who has placed the end in his own hand.
@@5323@@
May God let us, when we do not understand, still be willing to go; in the night, not go out; when we fall, believe that he will make us awake and shine; until his kingdom fills the whole earth. In the name of our Lord Jesus Christ we pray, amen.

What the text asks: When was this book written, and by whom? Is chapter 11 prophecy, or history written after the fact? What does the bilingual structure of Aramaic (2:4b–7:28) and Hebrew show?
@@5343@@
Late sixth century (traditional view)
@@5344@@
The traditional reading holds that the book was written by Daniel himself, taken captive to Babylon, in the Babylonian and Persian periods; the details of chapter 11 are prophecy given by God through Daniel. Aramaic was the common language of the empire at that time and reflects the world in which he lived.
@@5345@@
Second century BC (critical scholarship)
@@5346@@
Many scholars hold that the book reached its final form in the Maccabean period; the details of chapter 11 are so precise because the author is describing history that had already happened, with the aim of comforting a people under persecution. The bilingual structure is seen as a mark of editing.
@@5347@@
Depends on one’s view of prophecy
@@5348@@
Others stress that the difference in positions depends largely on whether one accepts that “God can show the future in advance”; the distribution of Aramaic and Hebrew has several literary and theological explanations.
@@5349@@
These notes read the book in the setting the text itself gives (the Babylonian and Persian periods) and set the other positions side by side; we do not make the date of composition a condition of faith. Whichever reading one takes, the effect of 4:17 and 7:13–14 on readers today does not change: the Most High rules, and the kingdom will in the end be given to the holy ones.
@@5350@@
Do not judge the faith of brothers and sisters who hold a different position; and do not, because of scholarly disputes, feel that this book is unreliable. The foundation of faith is this God who rules, not the theory of any one date.

What the text asks: Which four kingdoms are meant in chapters 2, 7 and 8?
@@5352@@
Babylon, Media-Persia, Greece, Rome (traditional)
@@5353@@
The traditional reading matches the image of chapter 2 and the four beasts of chapter 7 with the four empires of Babylon, Media-Persia, Greece and Rome, and holds that God’s kingdom arises in the time of Rome.
@@5354@@
Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece (critical scholarship)
@@5355@@
Many scholars hold that the author’s horizon runs from Babylon to the Hellenistic kingdoms; “Media” is seen as a separate kingdom, and the fourth kingdom is Greece (and its successors).
@@5356@@
The text stresses “beginning and end”
@@5357@@
Others stress that the list of four kingdoms is not the text’s most important point; the point is that human kingdoms rise and pass, one after another, while God’s kingdom is a kingdom of another kind.
@@5358@@
These notes set the various views side by side, reach no absolute conclusion, and draw up no table. What we hold on to is this: human kingdoms have a beginning and an end, and God’s kingdom does not grow out of them (2:44–45).
@@5359@@
Do not use any one matching to identify some country or regime of today; and do not, because the list is disputed, feel that these visions have nothing to do with you.
