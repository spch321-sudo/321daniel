I. A Piece of Music and an Accusation (3:1–15)
@@1213@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Please imagine a scene: a very large square, packed with people, officials, soldiers and merchants, everyone neatly dressed. In front stands a gleaming colossal image, and beside it a row of musicians. The herald cries aloud: when the music sounds, everyone must kneel. Then the music rises, and a whole field of people, like waves of wheat, bows down layer by layer. In this lesson we read of that scene, and of the few who did not bend in the waves of wheat.
@@1214@@
Daniel 3:1 says: “Nebuchadnezzar the king made an image of gold, whose height was sixty cubits, and its width six cubits. He set it up in the plain of Dura, in the province of Babylon.” Think back to chapter 2: the king dreamed of an image whose head was gold, and Daniel said to him, you are that head of gold, and after you there will be other kingdoms. The king heard this explanation and said nothing, but he went back and did one thing: he made an image, and this time not only the head but the whole image was gold. This was his answer: my kingdom will not be replaced. We are sometimes like this too. God says one sentence we do not like to hear; we do not quarrel, nor do we argue back; we only quietly make our own plan again, and make it bigger, brighter, more solid.
@@1215@@
Next comes the gathering: the local governors, the deputies, the governors, the judges, the treasurers, the counselors, the sheriffs, and all the rulers of the provinces, all present, an assembly of imperial loyalty. The herald proclaimed: “whenever you hear the sound of the horn, flute, zither, lyre, harp, pipe, and all kinds of music, you fall down and worship the golden image that Nebuchadnezzar the king has set up.” Anyone who did not would be cast into the burning fiery furnace. Please notice that this command did not ask everyone to debate, or to take a position; there was only one thing: when the music sounds, kneel. This design is very clever, because you do not need to think, you do not need to decide, you only need to follow everyone.
@@1216@@
Then the text records the result in a tone close to irony: “all the peoples, the nations, and the languages, fell down and worshiped the golden image that Nebuchadnezzar the king had set up.” No one shouted slogans, no one was dragged away. When the music sounded, everyone bowed their heads. Today the greatest pressure is often not the sword but one sentence: everyone does it this way. This sentence is harder to stand against than a sword, because it is not fierce; it is very polite, very reasonable, and carries a little goodwill. The first point of this lesson is that we must first see clearly how orderly and how natural this invitation is. Only then can we see that three people did not bend.
@@1217@@
That three people stood was not a secret. Verse 8 says: “certain Chaldeans came near, and brought accusation against the Jews.” Who accused them? Chaldeans, the very group that Daniel outshone in chapter 2. Some accusations are not because you were wrong, but because your being different pierces everyone’s uniformity. They did not warn them privately, and did not ask first; they went straight to the king, restating the king’s command as if only guarding the king’s majesty, and only then named names: “There are certain Jews whom you have appointed over the affairs of the province of Babylon: Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. These men, O king, have not respected you. They don’t serve your gods, and don’t worship the golden image which you have set up.”
@@1218@@
Please listen to this knife: you gave them high office, and they pay no heed to you. They did not oppose loudly; they only stood. But standing is itself a word. Sometimes a witness is not what you said but what you did not do. To keep standing in the midst of uniformity was their only action. They did not curse anyone, did not organize, and did not issue any statement.
@@1219@@
Then the king was angry. But he did not give the order at once; he gave a chance: “Is it on purpose, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, that you don’t serve my god, nor worship the golden image which I have set up? Now if you are ready whenever you hear the sound of the horn, flute, zither, lyre, harp, pipe, and all kinds of music to fall down and worship the image which I have made, good; but if you don’t worship, you shall be cast the same hour into the middle of a burning fiery furnace. Who is that god that will deliver you out of my hands?” This is the hardest form of pressure: not that no road is given to you, but that the road is near, easy and reasonable. Bend now, and everything can be treated as if it never happened.
@@1220@@
That last question is the core of the whole chapter: “Who is that god that will deliver you out of my hands?” What he asked was not why you will not worship, but whether your God has power. He turned faith into a contest of power. We hear this question today too, in different forms: Is what you believe any use? Why does your God not save you? This question cannot be answered by debate. The three did not debate either; their answer takes only three verses.
@@1221@@
Must I refuse every ceremony and occasion? My work requires me to attend some occasions I do not quite agree with. What should I do?
@@1222@@
This chapter does not ask us to refuse every occasion. It asks us to discern whether this action gives to something else the worship that should belong only to God. Attending, greeting and courtesy are not the same as worship, pledging loyalty and declaring allegiance. If you are not sure, first talk with a mature brother or sister, and say plainly the occasion, the action and what you feel in your heart; if it would make you declare allegiance to another lord, then, like these three, calmly and firmly do not do it. For the other small things, there is no need to make yourself a difficult person. If you meet an occasion you truly cannot discern, you may first ask a trusted companion, or explain beforehand to the host, and politely decline one particular part.

II. “But If Not” (3:16–18)
@@1224@@
Now we come to the heart of this chapter. Verse 16: “Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego answered the king, ‘Nebuchadnezzar, we have no need to answer you in this matter.’” Please notice the first words: they were calm. Not that they were not afraid, but that the answer had long been there. Do you remember chapter 1? Daniel had long ago resolved not to defile himself with the king’s food. First the answer in the heart, then facing the pressure. They did not have to decide on the spot, because the decision had long been made.
@@1225@@
Then come two sentences, a very precise structure of faith. The first, verse 17: “If it happens, our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace; and he will deliver us out of your hand, O king.” This is trust, with no doubt about God’s power. The second, verse 18: “But if not, let it be known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image which you have set up.” This is obedience, with no condition on God’s will. They were not sure that God would deliver, but they were sure that they would not worship.
@@1226@@
Please think about what fire you are facing right now. Perhaps a prayer with no answer yet, perhaps an illness that has not improved, perhaps a perseverance that no one understands. You do not know whether God will save you in the way you wish, but you can choose: not to be defiled. This is not cold calculation, but a very deep freedom of living under God’s sovereignty. Because you no longer need God to guarantee the outcome before you can feel at peace doing what is right.
@@1227@@
I also want to remind you that this sentence does not ask us to grit our teeth. Their calm was not because of how brave they were, but because the God they served is worthy. In the words of 321, this is letting Jesus be King: he may not act as I wish, but he is still the King. You can also make this sentence your own prayer today: Lord, you are able to save; but if not, I will still follow you.
@@1228@@
If I say “But if not” and really am not saved, am I abandoned by God?
@@1229@@
No. The ending of this chapter is deliverance, but the text first lets us hear that when they said these words they did not know the ending. Hebrews 11:35 and the verses after it also record others: some were tortured, not accepting their deliverance, and others were tried by mocking, scourging, bonds and imprisonment. The text says the world was not worthy of them. Not being saved does not mean being abandoned, and it is not a sign of too little faith. God’s presence is not the same as God’s exemption. You can honestly tell God your pain, and ask someone near you to walk with you for a while. You can also read verses 17 and 18 again and see that when they said these words, they did not know the ending; they knew only who was King.
@@1230@@
III. The Light of the Original: Serve, Deliver, But If Not (3:12, 17–18)
@@1231@@
There are three groups of words in this chapter worth taking apart. The first is serve and worship. Serve is the Aramaic פְּלַח (pelach), and worship is the Aramaic סְגִד (segid); these two words keep appearing as a pair. The accusers say they do not serve your gods and do not worship your image; the king says, you do not serve my god; the three say, our God whom we serve; the king says at last that they would not serve any other god. The same verb, in one chapter, changes masters four times. What this chapter speaks of is not religious form but this: to whom does your life give its loyalty?
@@1232@@
The second group is deliver, the Aramaic שֵׁיזִב (shezav), to rescue, to deliver. The king asks: who is the god who will deliver you out of my hands? The three say: God is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace, and he will deliver us out of your hand. The king says at last: there is no other god who is able to deliver like this. The same word, turned from a question into a confession, from the mouth of a king. This is the greatest irony of the chapter: the very thing he most wanted to prove no god could do, he himself in the end acknowledged.
@@1233@@
The third group is “but if not,” the Aramaic וְהֵן לָא (vehen la), literally “and if not,” meaning: even if he does not deliver. These four words are often misused. Some teach them as “if only you have enough faith, God will surely save you,” which is exactly upside down. What these words say is: being saved is God’s matter, being faithful is man’s matter. They did not guarantee the outcome for God; they guaranteed only that they would never serve another god. The first is trust in God, and the second is a decision about themselves.
@@1234@@
Put together, these three groups are the route of this chapter: Whom do you serve? Who can deliver? What if he does not? You can take these three questions into your own life and ask them one by one. Whom do you serve today? Whom do you believe can deliver? If he does not deliver in the way you wish, what is your decision? These three questions are much easier to ask in quiet than when the fire is already lit.
@@1235@@
The originals of “even so” and “but if not” are very similar. How can we tell them apart?
@@1236@@
The Aramaic הֵן (hen) at the opening of 3:17 can be “if it be so,” and it can also be “behold,” so some read it as “if God exists, he is able to deliver,” and others as “if you throw us into the fire, our God is able to deliver.” The CUV takes the former, translating it “even so.” The “but if not” of verse 18 clearly points to: even if he does not deliver. The two “ifs” run one positive and one negative, but whichever is read, the point of the two verses is the same: they believed God had power, yet did not make the outcome a condition of their own faithfulness. So we can pause here, first read the meaning of the original, and then put it back into our lives, without arguing about the word for too long.
@@1237@@
Is the “deliver” here the same as “being saved” in the New Testament?
@@1238@@
Not exactly. The “deliver” of Daniel here is being rescued from the furnace and from the king’s hand, a deliverance of body and circumstance. The “being saved” of the New Testament is mainly being saved from sin and death, and has to do with eternal life. The two do not conflict: God is a God who delivers, who delivers in history and also on the cross. When we read this chapter, we can first hear its own meaning, and then let the New Testament fill in the whole picture, rather than mixing the two together, and still less making “being delivered from the furnace” the necessary treatment of believers. In this way, our reading neither shrinks God’s deliverance nor turns deliverance into a guarantee.
@@1239@@
IV. The Fourth in the Fire, and a Witness Without a Singe (3:19–30)
@@1240@@
The king’s wrath came. Verse 19 says: “Then Nebuchadnezzar was full of fury, and the form of his appearance was changed against Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego. He spoke, and commanded that they should heat the furnace seven times more than it was usually heated.” The less it can be refused, the more it must prove that it cannot be refused; this is how power often looks when it is angry. Then the king commanded the strong men of his army to bind the three and cast them into the furnace. Please notice one detail: the three, in their pants, tunics and mantles, were bound and cast in, not treated specially, but went in just as they were. This detail will be taken up again later.
@@1241@@
Verse 22 says: “Therefore because the king’s commandment was urgent, and the furnace exceedingly hot, the flame of the fire killed those men who took up Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego.” Please listen to this sentence. The king who wanted to prove that no god could save them, before throwing them in, first lost his own strongest men. There is no miracle here; the fire was real, the furnace was real, death was real. The text does not play down what they faced, so “But if not” is so heavy: they did not speak only after being sure that God would deliver, but stood, knowing that the fire was real.
@@1242@@
Then the king could not sit still. Verse 24: “Then Nebuchadnezzar the king was astonished, and rose up in haste. He spoke and said to his counselors, ‘Didn’t we cast three men bound into the middle of the fire?’ They answered the king, ‘True, O king.’” He confirmed it once, and then said: “Look, I see four men loose, walking in the middle of the fire, and they are unharmed. The appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods.” Please read slowly: four, unbound, walking in the fire, and unharmed. Those who went in bound were loosed in the fire. The fire, which had come to devour, now became the place where they walked.
@@1243@@
The most wonderful thing is that the fire was still there, the furnace was still there, and the king’s command had not been revoked. God did not first put out the fire and then let them in; he walked in while the fire was still burning. As for who the fourth was, the various schools hold different views: some say an angel, some say an appearance of Christ in the Old Testament, and some say a sign of God’s presence. We do not treat it as a required item of doctrinal proof; we say only: this brings to mind Immanuel, God with us. God did not promise us we would not enter the fire, but he promised to be with us in the fire. The fire you are walking through may not yet have gone out, but you are not alone.
@@1244@@
The king drew near the mouth of the furnace and said: “Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, you servants of the Most High God, come out, and come here!” Did you hear what he called them? A few minutes before, they were the ones who paid no heed to you; now, they are servants of the Most High God. This is the quietest witness of this chapter. Then the officials together looked them over: the body not harmed by the fire, the hair not singed, the clothes not changed, not even the smell of fire. As they were when they went in, so they were when they came out.
@@1245@@
Last, the king’s own words: “Blessed be the God of Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, who has sent his angel, and delivered his servants who trusted in him, and have changed the king’s word, and have yielded their bodies, that they might not serve nor worship any god, except their own God.” Do you remember 3:15? Who is that god that will deliver you out of my hands? Now the king himself answers: “because there is no other god who is able to deliver like this.” The same word, the same king, turned from a question into a confession. But please notice: he said “their God,” not “my God”; he is still learning. In chapter 4 we will see how he goes on.
@@1246@@
The last verse is very short: “Then the king promoted Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego in the province of Babylon.” Promotion was a result, not a reason. When they said “But if not,” they did not know they would be promoted. Faithfulness is not for the sake of promotion, but remains faithful even if not promoted. This is the other half of verse 18. If we treat faithfulness as a way to exchange for success, we turn God into someone we can trade with.
@@1247@@
Is the fourth in the fire Jesus? May I teach it this way?
@@1248@@
The text says only “like a son of the gods,” and in verse 28 the king calls this one “his angel.” The approach of this commentary is to set the views side by side: there is a reading as an angel, and also a reading as an appearance of Christ in the Old Testament, and neither is a clear link made by the New Testament. So we say “this brings to mind Immanuel,” and do not say “this is a type.” In teaching, you can state the various views clearly, and then place the weight on the one thing the text is most sure of: God is with his servants in the fire. Whichever reading you choose, please remember that the real comfort of the text is that God is with his servants in the fire.
@@1249@@
The first deviation is turning “But if not” into “if only you have enough faith, God will surely save you.” This sounds like faith, but it is exactly upside down: the three said that God is able to deliver, and also said that even if he does not, they would still not serve another god. Being saved is God’s matter, being faithful is man’s matter, and faithfulness is not conditional on being saved. Be especially careful here with those who are losing, sick or shut out; do not say to anyone, “you were not saved because your faith was not enough.”
@@1250@@
The second deviation is turning the fourth into doctrinal proof that must be believed, or declaring “that is” Christ. The king said “like a son of the gods,” and verse 28 calls him “his angel.” The schools differ: an angel, an appearance of Christ in the Old Testament, a sign of God’s presence. We say only: this brings to mind Immanuel, God with us, and we do not treat it as a creed to be fought for to the end.
@@1251@@
The third deviation is turning the promotion for faithfulness into a reward and guarantee of faithfulness. The promotion in verse 30 was a result, not a reason; when the three said “But if not,” they did not know they would be promoted. To treat faithfulness as a method for success is to turn God into someone we can trade with.
@@1252@@
The fourth deviation is turning the three’s refusal to worship into permission to confront those in authority loudly on any occasion. They shouted no slogans and staged no protest; they simply did not bow, and calmly stated their position. For brothers and sisters who are under persecution, we should especially not press for details, nor make public any traceable details; protecting people matters more than showing courage.
@@1253@@
The fifth deviation is turning the iron rule of this chapter into a contest of who is more brave. “But if not” is obedience to God, not a standard for measuring others. You may not be brave enough, and you may once have knelt; God is still the one who is with you in the fire, and who also calls you to stand again.
@@1254@@
If I once knelt, do I still have the right to say “But if not”?
@@1255@@
Yes. The text does not say that these three companions never felt fear in their whole lives; it records only that this time they stood. Peter denied the Lord three times, and later was also called to shepherd the flock. What God values is not that you have never fallen, but that today you return to him and decide again who is King. You can honestly tell God about the time you knelt, and then say to him today’s “But if not.” He will not reject you, and does not need you to pretend you were never weak. So do not take your past weakness as a reason you cannot stand again today.
@@1256@@
The first question: we are used to the pressure of “everyone does it this way.” Festivals, business dinners, colleagues’ gatherings, family rites: on some occasions, not going is not giving face, and going leaves us unsure whether we are making a statement to another lord. In the Chinese situation, relationships weigh more than principles; this is our strength and often our pressure. This chapter does not ask us to become difficult people, but invites us to discern which things are courtesy and relationship, and which are giving to something else the worship that belongs only to God. In the latter, like the three companions, we can neither argue nor accuse, only quietly not follow, and respect others.
@@1257@@
The second question: we often treat “whether God saves” as an exam of faith. Someone at home is sick, we keep praying, and there is no improvement, and those nearby say: your faith is not enough. That sentence wounds, and it does not fit this chapter. When the three said “But if not,” they did not treat the outcome as a report card of faith. So when facing those who are losing, we can accompany and pray, and not draw conclusions for God; when facing ourselves, we can honestly tell God our pain and questions, and still decide: you are King.
@@1258@@
If I live in a place where it is not convenient to make faith public, how can I read this chapter?
@@1259@@
You can read it as a very quiet hope. The three companions shouted no slogans and staged no protest; they only did not bow, and calmly stated their position. Within a safe range, in the smallest ways, you can guard your heart, pray, and support one another with trusted companions. There is no need to write or say publicly details that could harm yourself and others. Protecting people matters more than showing courage; God also sees the faithfulness that no one else sees. You can also ask a trusted brother or sister to agree with you on a safe way to pray for each other. If you cannot yet find anyone you can trust, you can first speak the words in your heart quietly to God, and he hears them all.
@@1260@@
VII. Living Out 321—“But If Not,” Still Standing
@@1261@@
The three foundations: Jesus is my Role Model. In Gethsemane he said, “not what I desire, but what you desire,” and he too walked into the deepest fire, and on the cross was not exempted. The Bible is my Standard, because the stand of the three companions did not come from emotion, but from what they had known from childhood, the one only God. The Holy Spirit is my Guide, because to stand while everyone bows their heads takes not one’s own courage, but a Spirit who is present within.
@@1262@@
The two core values: Let Jesus be King means that he may not act as I wish, but he is still the King. Let Jesus receive all the glory means that after coming out of the furnace, it is not that people are praised, but that “blessed be their God”; the glory returns to God. The one purpose: to build God’s system. This system is not one person fighting alone, but three people, going in together and coming out together. God’s system is, in a place where everyone bows their heads, still having people who guard one another and stand together.
@@1263@@
Changing concepts changes life: when we think “the result of faithfulness must be good,” our life is afraid of failure; when we know “being saved is God’s matter, being faithful is my matter,” our life becomes free. From selfhood to selflessness: the person with self says “God must act as I wish,” the selfless person says “you are King.” In this chapter, selflessness is not nothingness, but no longer making one’s own rescue the condition.
@@1264@@
How can “But if not” be lived out in daily life, and not only in big things?
@@1265@@
It can begin with the smallest things. For example, you know that a small dishonesty would let you hand in one report fewer and have a little less trouble, yet you decide to be honest; you know that a small retelling could let others see how clever you are, yet you decide to keep your mouth guarded. In these moments you do not know whether God will let you “gain”; you only decide: he is King. Small “But if nots,” one by one, mean that before the great fire you do not have to learn to stand for the first time. As days pass you will find that small faithfulness becomes your best preparation for the great fire. This kind of habit will, little by little, bring you from relying on willpower alone to truly trusting the Lord.
@@1266@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Furnace
@@1267@@
The proud type says: “My faith is big enough; God will surely save me as I wish.” This sounds like faith, but it puts God under one’s own conditions. The three companions did not speak this way; they said: he is able, but if not, we still will not worship. Please loosen your hand, and let God be God.
@@1268@@
The fearful type says: “If I do not follow everyone, I will lose everything.” This fear is real, and the fire is real. But this chapter tells you: in the hottest place, you are not alone. Do not look only at the heat of the fire; look also at the fourth who walks in the fire.
@@1269@@
The ashamed type says: “I too once knelt down; I have no right to stand.” The text does not say how perfect these three companions were; it says only that this time they stood. If you once knelt, God still calls you to stand up again today. Return to God, beginning today.
@@1270@@
The wounded type says: “I stood, but no one saved me, and I am hurt.” This wound is real. Please do not answer it with “your faith was not enough.” The hope of the text is not a guarantee that you will not enter the fire, but that he came in. You can bring your wound and still honestly say before him: I am in great pain.

<p><strong>שְׁלָמְכוֹן יִשְׂגֵּא</strong> (may your peace be multiplied) is the greeting formula of imperial decrees of that time. Chapter 4 is a rare chapter in the whole book: from beginning to end it is a decree written by a foreign king in his own voice; see the issues.</p><p><strong>אָתוֹהִי רַבְרְבִין</strong> (how great are his signs): the king attributes “signs and wonders” to the “Most High God,” and this pair of words is often used in the Old Testament to describe what God did in Egypt.</p>
@@1350@@
<p><strong>This chapter is a witness written by the king himself, and it is written beginning from the end.</strong></p><p>First look at what kind of document this is. An ordinary decree is a king giving orders and setting rules; this one is a king proclaiming to “all the peoples, nations, and languages, who dwell in all the earth”: the signs and wonders that God has worked toward me, I am glad to make known. Please notice that he says “toward me.” This is not an onlooker’s comment, but a first-person witness by one who went through it himself.</p><p>His doxology is only three verses, yet it has already stated the conclusion of the whole chapter: “His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom; his dominion is from generation to generation.” Compare chapter 3: he once set up a golden image and required the whole earth to bow; now he himself proclaims to the whole earth that only one kingdom is everlasting, and that kingdom is not his. These are the words of a king, spoken from the top of an empire.</p><p>The text tells us the ending first so that, as we read the story below, we ask not “how will it end?” but “how did he come to this?” This is also our own story: often it is only when we look back that we can say, God has worked signs and wonders toward me. Your witness can also be like this decree: simple, honest, and in the first person: what God has done toward me.</p>
