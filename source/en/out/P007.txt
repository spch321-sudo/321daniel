@@2090@@
You may ask: if God has already counted, are people left with no chance? The text’s answer is that the chance lies behind us. Nebuchadnezzar’s story was Belshazzar’s chance; those thousand nights, every moment when he could have bowed his head, were chances. God’s patience is not so that we can put it off one more day; it is to give us one more chance to turn back.
@@2091@@
“That very night the king was killed”: does this mean God’s judgment always comes this suddenly?
@@2092@@
No. The judgment in this chapter comes after long patience: Nebuchadnezzar’s story had already happened before Belshazzar’s eyes, and he had many chances, yet he did not bow. Many times God’s patience looks like silence in human eyes. The text reminds us not to take God’s patience as God’s permission. Even more, we must not turn this chapter into material for frightening others; take it as a reason to bow our own heads today. What the text gives people is a chance, not fear; an invitation, not a threat. So bowing today need not wait for someone to remind us, nor for something to go wrong.
@@2093@@
The first deviation is to teach “MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN” as a code for working out some nation or event of our day. Daniel’s interpretation points to the end of Babylon that very night; the text does not ask us to turn it into a tool for counting days or identifying nations. Turning this passage into a guessing game makes us forget what we are really meant to do: bow our heads.
@@2094@@
The second deviation is to teach the judgment as gloating over others, or to use the “balances” to pass judgment on what others lack. When Daniel spoke of what was lacking, he did not mock and he did not gloat; this chapter is spoken to everyone who knows and yet does not humble himself, ourselves included. Weigh yourself first, then talk about others.
@@2095@@
The third deviation is to teach the “balances” as “people must reach a standard by their own works to be saved.” The balances reveal what is lacking so that people may see their need; the gospel is that Christ already stands on our side, not that we stand by our own weight. Teaching this chapter as an exam of works makes people more anxious, not more free.
@@2096@@
The fourth deviation is to teach Daniel’s refusing the rewards (5:17) and later accepting the purple robe (5:29) as inconsistency or hypocrisy. What he refused was speaking on the condition of a reward; the reward was something the king ordered afterward on his own, and Daniel never made it the precondition of his interpretation. The point is the order, not the purple robe.
@@2097@@
The fifth deviation is to use this chapter to frighten people who have not yet believed, saying, “If you still don’t believe, you will end up like Belshazzar.” The point of this chapter is knowing and not humbling oneself; its invitation is to bow today. Threats cannot make anyone love the Lord; it is God’s grace and patience that lead people to repentance.
@@2098@@
I often put things off. Am I like Belshazzar?
@@2099@@
Putting things off is not necessarily Belshazzar’s condition, but this chapter does hold up a mirror: we know, and we have not yet done it. You can first ask honestly: am I putting it off because I am afraid, because I am tired, or because I do not actually want to let go yet? If it is one of the first two, bring that weakness to God and ask him to help you; if it is the third, you need a more direct humbling. Whichever it is, please do not face it alone. Find a companion and put that matter on the calendar with a specific date. You can also ask a companion to set a specific date with you, and on that day to ask you one question: did you do it?
@@2100@@
The first question: we are very used to saying “let’s wait and see.” Wait and see, wait a little longer, watch a bit more. In a Chinese setting this phrase is often a kind of wisdom: not impulsive, not rash. But in spiritual matters the same phrase can become a way of putting things off. We know very well that God has already spoken; about our family, our marriage, our work, God has already pointed a direction, and yet we say, “let’s wait and see.” This chapter does not ask us to be reckless; it invites us to be honest: when I say “let’s wait and see,” am I seeking wisdom, or avoiding humbling myself?
@@2101@@
The second question: we care a great deal about face, and about other people’s face. In this chapter Daniel had to say to the king, in front of a thousand nobles: you have been weighed in the balances and found lacking. That is an occasion that calls for great wisdom and courage. In a Chinese setting we seldom say to someone’s face what he lacks; partly to protect the relationship, and partly because we are afraid. The direction this chapter gives us is: respect first, then honesty; not in order to win, and not in order to shame, but so that this person still has a way forward. Some words left unsaid are love; some words left unsaid are fear. We need to tell them apart.
@@2102@@
What should I do if I find I need to tell an elder or a supervisor a hard truth?
@@2103@@
First pray for this person, and check your motive: am I doing this for his good, or to prove that I am right? Then choose a suitable time and place, and speak privately and quietly. First acknowledge what the other person has given, then say clearly what you have seen, and finally offer a concrete way forward. If the matter involves safety or an imbalance of power, please first seek the counsel of a trusted companion or pastor; you do not have to carry it alone. Daniel spoke the truth in front of the king without any mockery, and that is a pattern we can learn from. If you are not sure how to begin, you can also write down what you want to say first and ask someone you trust to read it through once.
@@2104@@
VII. Living Out 321: Once We Know, We Bow Our Heads
@@2105@@
The three foundations: Jesus is my Role Model. Although he knew who he was, he humbled himself, was obedient to death, even death on a cross. He was not one who knew and did not bow; he was one who knew, and bowed. The Bible is my Standard, because this chapter is not asking us to know one more story; it asks us to respond after we know. The Holy Spirit is my Guide, because bowing the head does not rest on willpower; it rests on someone inside, who gently reminds me when I say “just a little longer.”
@@2106@@
The two core values: Let Jesus be King means that even on a relaxed evening he is my Lord; not only on the Lord’s Day, but also at my banquet, in my time, in my money. Let Jesus receive all the glory means giving back to God what was his all along: my breath, my gifts, the position I hold. The one purpose: to build God’s system. In God’s system there are people like the queen mother, who remember you; there are people like Daniel, who speak what must be spoken without seeking a reward; and we are, together, a community that watches over one another.
@@2107@@
Changing our mindset changes our life: when we think “knowing” is the same as “having,” our life stays in information; when we realize that “knowing” must become “bowing the head,” our life begins to change. From self to selflessness: the person with self says “I know, let me think about it,” and the selfless person says “I know, Lord; today I will do it.”
@@2108@@
In the family, this means we no longer say, “I’ll apologize when the children are older,” but bow our heads now; in the workplace, it means we no longer say, “I’ll deal with that matter after this project is over,” but do it today; in the church, it means we no longer say, “I’ll serve when there’s an opportunity,” but take the first step this very week. Bowing the head is a matter for today.
@@2109@@
How do we concretely practice “once we know, we bow our heads” in everyday life?
@@2110@@
You can start with three very small steps. First, write down in one sentence the thing you know. Second, write down one smallest next step, such as making a phone call or saying one sentence of apology. Third, agree on a specific time; do it before that time, and tell a companion. Bowing the head is not an emotion; it is an action. Do not wait until you feel ready; you can act first and let your heart follow slowly. The action is small, but little by little it teaches your heart to obey again. A month later, look back, and you will see that your heart is a little softer than before.

<p><strong>A decree that looks as if it honors the king is in fact a trap made for one man.</strong></p><p>These men did not go straight to accuse Daniel. They were clever: they designed a law. On the surface it honored the king and put him in the place of God, a thirty-day loyalty test. This decree did not mention Daniel in a single word, yet it turned Daniel’s daily life into a crime.</p><p>Notice the language they used: “consulted together,” “all agreed.” It is a very common method: to use “everyone is doing it” to apply pressure. In fact Daniel, one of the three presidents, was never asked. <strong>The most dangerous law is not the one that plainly targets you, but the one that looks ordinary and happens to make it impossible for you to worship as usual.</strong></p><p>Then comes the key moment: “make it unchangeable, according to the law of the Medes and Persians.” Darius signed. With this one act the king too became a prisoner: the law he had established, even he could not take back. It is a deep irony and also a foreshadowing in this chapter: a law made by man can trap the one who made it, while the law of God is what sets people free.</p><p>This passage contains no miracle and no voice from heaven, only a decision by a group of clever people in a meeting room. But perhaps that is exactly what many believers face more often today: not an obvious persecution, but a seemingly reasonable rule that makes “worshiping as usual” something that costs us.</p>

He was not ignorant; he “knew, and still…”. This is another picture of 3:18, “let it be known to you.”</p><p><strong>וְכַוִּין פְּתִיחָן לֵהּ בְּעִלִּיתֵהּ נֶגֶד יְרוּשְׁלֶם</strong> (his windows open in his upper room toward Jerusalem): “open” is a participle, meaning “always open.” It was not opened for the occasion; it was his habit all along.</p><p><strong>כְּדִי־הֲוָא עָבֵד מִן־קַדְמַת דְּנָה</strong> (as he did before): the word “before” (from earlier times) stresses not this one time but continuity over many years.</p>
@@2201@@
<p><strong>He knew, and then, as before.</strong></p><p>This is the quietest verse of the chapter, and one of the most important in the whole book. Verse 10 says: “When Daniel knew that the writing was signed, he went into his house.” He did not call a press conference, did not rally companions, did not go around asking for help, and did not hide. He simply went home, went upstairs, and went to that window. <strong>The greatest witness is often not a big action but carrying on as usual under pressure.</strong></p><p>Notice these details. First, the window was open “toward Jerusalem.” This was Solomon’s hope in the temple dedication prayer: when the people are carried away captive and pray toward this house, God will hear (1 Kings 8:48). His window was a memory of home and also a direction of hope. Second, “open”: the original suggests the window was always open. It was not for today; it was his daily habit. He did not open it specially to make a statement, nor close it to avoid trouble.</p><p>Third, “he kneeled on his knees before his God, and gave thanks.” Hear that word: thanks. This is a man about to be thrown into the lions’ den, and his prayer has thanksgiving in it. Fourth, “as he did before”: these words are the strength of the whole chapter. He did not increase the number of times because of danger, and he did not reduce it because of danger. He did not perform, and he did not retreat.</p><p>Today we may not meet a lions’ den, but there are many “little lions”: the unwritten rules of the workplace, the pressure to work on the Lord’s Day, a setting where you feel awkward saying “let me pray first.” Daniel is not teaching us how to confront others; he is teaching us how to carry on as usual under pressure. This is one picture of selflessness: I do not need to prove anything to anyone; I simply return, as usual, into God’s presence.</p>

@@2204@@
<p><strong>The king has supreme power, yet cannot save the man he most wants to save.</strong></p><p>The camera turns to the king’s side. The accusers come in and first remind the king: you signed the decree. It is a very shrewd step: first make the king nod, then bring up Daniel’s name. The king answered, “The thing is true, according to the law of the Medes and Persians, which cannot be altered.” When he said this he did not yet know that those words would become a rope binding himself.</p><p>Then they said: “Daniel, who is of the children of the captivity of Judah, pays no attention to you.” Notice how they named Daniel: one of the exiles. They did not say “your president” or “Daniel, who has served the king for years”; they said only “of the captivity.” It was a stripping of identity: turning a man from a contributor into an outsider. And “pays no attention to you” is exactly the wording used against the three friends in chapter 3. The enemy’s language is always alike.</p><p>Then the king’s reaction: “Then the king, when he heard these words, was very displeased, and set his heart on Daniel to deliver him; and he labored till the going down of the sun to rescue him.” This king is not a bad man. He loved this man; he wanted to save him; he spent the whole day looking for a way. But his way was blocked by his own signature. The accusers came again: “Know, O king… no decree or statute which the king establishes may be changed.” <strong>Human power can set up laws, but it cannot save a man from a law it has set up itself.</strong></p><p>Do you remember Nebuchadnezzar’s sentence in chapter 3? “Who is that god who will deliver you out of my hands?” In this chapter the king himself becomes the one asking that question: who can deliver me from the law I made myself? Only God can answer that question.</p>

@@2207@@
<p><strong>The king said something he himself was not sure of: “Your God whom you serve continually will deliver you.”</strong></p><p>They brought Daniel and threw him into the lions’ den. Notice that the text does not describe his reaction; no cry, no plea, only the king’s sentence: “Your God whom you serve continually, he will deliver you.” This sentence is a little like comfort, a little like a blessing, and more like a man with no assurance, grasping at the one hope left at the last moment. <strong>The king’s sentence is the most honest of the whole chapter.</strong> All his power could not save him; he could only hand Daniel over to Daniel’s God.</p><p>Notice the word “continually”: “your God whom you serve continually.” What the king saw was not one brave moment of Daniel’s, but his long years. This is an important reminder: a person’s trust in a time of crisis is built up, day by day, in ordinary days. Though the king was a foreigner, from Daniel’s long life he reached this conclusion: the God this man serves is real.</p><p>Then a stone was laid at the mouth of the den, and the king sealed it with his own signet and with the signet of his nobles. This picture reminds us of another mouth of a tomb, another stone, also sealed (Matt 27:66). We do not treat it as a type; we can only say: it reminds us. People seal death in the firmest way, and at dawn God has his own work to do.</p><p>Finally, the king’s night: fasting, no musical instruments, no sleep. That night the king was less at peace than Daniel. In the palace there was no one who could comfort him. Human power can seal a den, but it cannot seal a conscience.</p>

<p><strong>At dawn it is the king who speaks first, and then Daniel who bears witness.</strong></p><p>Verse 19 says: “Then the king arose very early in the morning, and went in haste to the den of lions.” This king probably did not sleep. He went “in haste,” with a word from the same root as his own fright in chapter 5. His hurry was that of a frightened man at daybreak, rushing to confirm something he dared not confirm. Coming near the den, he cried out “with a troubled voice”: “Daniel, servant of the living God, is your God, whom you serve continually, able to deliver you from the lions?” Notice his title for Daniel: servant of the living God. He had not said that yesterday. One night of silence changed his title.</p><p>Then Daniel’s voice: “O king, live forever!” His first words were a blessing for the king. A man who had just come out of the lions’ den did not blame, did not accuse, did not cry injustice; he first blessed the king who had thrown him in. <strong>This is one picture of selflessness: when hurt, still blessing.</strong> Then he gave his witness: “My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions’ mouths.” Notice: the one who shut the lions’ mouths was God, not his prayer and not his faith, but the angel God sent.</p><p>He went on: “because as before him innocence was found in me; and also before you, O king, I have done no harm.” We will discuss this sentence separately later, but notice one order first: first “before God,” then “before the king.” He answers to God first, and then to the king. He said it very calmly: he had not disobeyed the king in anything reasonable; he had only, on the matter of “asking from God,” chosen God.</p><p>Finally the text gives a conclusion: “no kind of harm was found on him, because he had trusted in his God.” This sentence is not saying how great Daniel’s faith was, but that the One he trusted is trustworthy. His faith is not a condition to be filled up; it is a place: he placed himself in God’s hand.</p>

Two foreign kings, each with a decree, proclaim the same God to all the earth.</p>

<p><strong>The ending of this chapter has a very heavy picture and a very glorious decree.</strong></p><p>Verse 24 is the hardest verse of the chapter to read: the king ordered the accusers, together with their wives and children, to be thrown into the lions’ den. We cannot pretend this verse is not there. The laws of the ancient Near East often practiced collective punishment; the text describes what happened and does not say it was good. Deuteronomy 24:16 says: “The fathers shall not be put to death for the children, neither shall the children be put to death for the fathers. Every man shall be put to death for his own sin.” So when we read this verse we are not celebrating, and we are not making excuses for any violence; we see that one who used harming the innocent as his means finally fell into the pit he himself dug. Whether this collective sentence was just, we set out the different views in the issues.</p><p>Then comes the decree. Like Nebuchadnezzar in chapter 4, this king also proclaims to all the earth: “men tremble and fear before the God of Daniel, for he is the living God, and steadfast forever. His kingdom is that which will not be destroyed.” Notice that these words are almost word for word the same as those of chapters 2 and 4. Two foreign kings in a row use the same language to bear witness to all the earth. This is what the whole long Aramaic section keeps saying: <strong>God’s kingdom is indestructible, and human kingdoms, however great, will come to bow before him.</strong></p><p>The last verse: “So this Daniel prospered in the reign of Darius, and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian.” He outlived one empire, and then another. From the youth of chapter 1 he walked to here, a man who carried on “as usual”: resolving as usual, serving as usual, praying as usual. This is the theme of the chapter and the pattern the whole book wants us to learn: in different kingdoms, still the servant of the same God.</p>

I. A Daily Life with Nothing to Hold Against It, and a Law Made Just for Him (6:1–9)
@@2483@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Let me first ask you to think: if one day your boss, your colleagues or your neighbors examined your life carefully, going through your accounts, your words, your time, one thing at a time, what would they find? Today we read about a man who was examined this way; they searched for a long time, and in the end could reach only one conclusion.
@@2484@@
Daniel chapter 6: a new king, a new regime. Darius appointed one hundred twenty satraps, and over them three presidents, and Daniel was one of them. The text says: “Then this Daniel was distinguished above the presidents and the local governors, because an excellent spirit was in him; and the king thought to set him over the whole realm.” He had walked through one empire and into another, and was not pushed out. Why? Because of his steady faithfulness. Then comes that very old story: someone does well, and someone else begins to look for ground against him.
@@2485@@
Verse 4 says: “they could find no occasion or fault, because he was faithful. There wasn’t any error or fault found in him.” They searched everywhere, accounts, administration, speech, and found nothing. The strongest witness is not a beautiful speech but a daily life no one can find fault with. So they came to a conclusion: “We won’t find any occasion against this Daniel, unless we find it against him concerning the law of his God.” This is the highest praise an enemy gave Daniel. They admitted that this man’s only weakness was his obedience to God. The reversal is ironic: they wanted to find his fault, and found his greatest glory.
@@2486@@
Then they designed a law. They did not go straight to accuse Daniel; they were clever: “whoever asks a petition of any god or man for thirty days, except of you, O king, he shall be cast into the den of lions.” The law sounded good, it honored the king; it did not mention Daniel in a single word; yet it turned Daniel’s daily life into a crime. Notice how they put it: the presidents, deputies, satraps, counselors and governors of the kingdom, all consulted together. All of them. Yet Daniel was also a president, and was never asked. It is a very common method: to use “everyone is doing it” to apply pressure.
