@@4310@@
Please notice another detail: “I, Daniel, alone saw the vision; for the men who were with me didn’t see the vision; but a great quaking fell on them, and they fled to hide themselves.” There is an experience others cannot see. You may have had it: a burden others do not understand; a moment only you and God know. Please do not doubt its reality because no one saw; and please do not feel alone because others did not see. God is the one who sees.
@@4311@@
Then Daniel’s reaction: “So I was left alone, and saw this great vision. No strength remained in me; for my face grew deathly pale, and I retained no strength.” A man who had mourned for the people for three weeks, at the sight of the glory, fell down. This reminds us of chapter 8, where he also fell asleep. Before glory man has no strength; this is honest. But this chapter does not stop here. Next, a hand came.
@@4312@@
Perhaps you are mourning for someone right now: a child who has gone astray, an elder who is gravely ill, a church that is struggling. You need not dress this mourning up as full confidence. Daniel’s mourning was real, and God answered him with reality. You can first sit down in a quiet place and say that person’s name, not beautifully, only honestly. This small act is often the beginning of intercession.
@@4313@@
Is mourning for others a kind of unbelief?
@@4314@@
No. Mourning for others is an expression of love. Daniel mourned for the people three weeks; the text does not blame him, and instead, while he mourned, God sent a messenger to answer him. The real question is not whether you mourn but whether you bring your mourning before God. You can fix a time each day, say to God the person or matter that makes you mourn, and then place it in his hand. Then mourning is no longer a burden carried alone but becomes intercession. After practicing this for a few weeks you will find that mourning is no longer only a stone pressing on your chest but becomes a small road into God’s heart.
@@4315@@
II. A Hand, a Name and the Words “From the First Day” (10:10–14)
@@4316@@
The man on the ground did not stand up by himself. The text says: “Behold, a hand touched me, which set me on my knees and on the palms of my hands.” Notice this picture: he had not yet stood; he only rose a little on his knees and palms. This is a very gentle, step-by-step raising. God does not force a man who has fallen to stand straight at once; he first touches him and lets him prop himself up a little with knee and hand.
@@4317@@
Then the first words the visitor spoke were not blame and not a command but a name: “Daniel, you greatly beloved man, understand the words that I speak to you, and stand upright; for I have been sent to you, now.” Do you remember? In chapter 9 Gabriel called him this too. A man on the ground without strength, before any other words, was first called greatly beloved. This is what God says first to the weak: not “be strong” but “you are beloved.” Then the text is honest: “I stood trembling.” When he stood, he was still trembling. God does not require that we stop trembling before we stand. We can tremble and stand at the same time.
@@4318@@
Then the most important verse of this chapter: “Don’t be afraid, Daniel; for from the first day that you set your heart to understand, and to humble yourself before your God, your words were heard. I have come for your words’ sake.” Notice three things. First, do not be afraid: this is the first thing God’s messenger said to a trembling man. Second, from the first day: not the twenty-first day, not when the fasting reached three weeks, but from the first day, his words were heard. Third, I have come for your words’ sake: his prayer was the reason the messenger came.
@@4319@@
Have you prayed a long time and had no response? Sometimes we think God has not heard. This verse says to you: from your first day, your words were heard. Delay does not mean not heard.
@@4320@@
Then the text says: “But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days; but, behold, Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me because I remained there with the kings of Persia.” This sentence has long been read in many different ways: some think it is a spiritual power; some think it is the ruler of Persia or his representative; and some think it is a language in which heaven and earth correspond. We set the views side by side and say plainly: we do not turn this verse into teaching on territorial spirit warfare, and we do not teach you to identify or attack the spirit of a place. What this verse gives us is a deep comfort: over your prayer, in heaven, much is going on that we cannot see. You need not know the details; you only need to know: the words have been heard.
@@4321@@
One more small picture is worth pausing over: the hand set him on his knees and palms and did not pull him straight to standing at once. God knows how much a person can bear, and he is not in a hurry. You need not be in a hurry either. If today you can only prop yourself up a little, that is already grace; you need not compare your speed with others, and need not blame yourself for being too slow.
@@4322@@
How do I use the words “from the first day” in my own waiting?
@@4323@@
You can use them this way: each morning, do not ask “is there an answer today?” but say, “From the first day, you heard.” Write this sentence on paper and put it where you see it every day. Then divide your waiting into two parts: what you have heard, and what I can do. What I can do is one small thing today; what you have heard is my whole waiting. Then your waiting is no longer empty but a journey that has been heard and held. Please remember, this is not a formula and does not guarantee the answer will come at once; it only brings you from anxious guessing back to a resting assurance: he heard.
@@4324@@
III. Light from the Original: Greatly Beloved, First Day, Touch, Strong (10:11, 12, 18, 19)
@@4325@@
Four words in the original of this chapter deserve a slow reading. The first is “greatly beloved,” Hebrew חֲמוּדוֹת (chamudot). Literally “desired, very precious.” In this book it appears three times: 9:23, 10:11, 10:19. All three were said at the moment when Daniel was weakest and most in need. This name is not a reward but an acceptance: you are beloved, whether or not you have strength now.
@@4326@@
The second is “set your heart,” Hebrew נָתַתָּ לִבְּךָ (natata libbekha), literally “you set your heart here.” “Setting the heart to understand” is putting the whole heart on understanding. It is joined with “humble yourself” (לְהִתְעַנּוֹת): on one side placing the heart on understanding, on the other humbling oneself before God. These two things are, the messenger said, the reason his words were heard. Notice that it is not because he did much, but because he set his heart in the right place.
@@4327@@
The third is “touch,” Hebrew נָגַע (naga). This word appears three times in this chapter: in 10:10 a hand touched him, in 10:16 one touched his lips, and in 10:18 one touched him again. Three touches, three results: standing, opening the mouth, strength. In the Bible, touch is often the closest act between God and man. You can think of it: Jesus too often used his hand to touch people.
@@4328@@
The fourth is “strong,” Hebrew חָזַק (chazaq). In 10:18, “strengthened me” (Piel, to make strong); in 10:19, “be strong” (a command); in 10:19, “I was strengthened” (reflexive); in 10:19, “you have strengthened me” (Piel). The same root, four times in all. See the order: first he is made strong, then commanded to be strong, and then says: you have made me strong. Strength is given, and also answered.
@@4329@@
Please notice again: this chapter does not teach us a formula; it shows how a gentle God treats a weak servant. We put the emphasis on his work, not on our method, because methods make people tense, and knowing him lets people rest.
@@4330@@
How do “setting the heart to understand” and “humbling yourself” fit together?
@@4331@@
The way they fit is that one is upward and one is downward. Setting the heart to understand is putting the heart on God’s word and God’s heart; humbling yourself is lowering yourself before God and admitting your limits. With only the former it can become the pride of pursuing knowledge; with only the latter it can become heavy self-blame. Together they let you be hungry and humble at once. When you read the Bible you can pause and say to God in one sentence: I want to understand, but I also know I need you. Then your reading is not only adding knowledge but, in humility, being led step by step by God’s word and God’s Spirit, becoming softer the more you read.
@@4332@@
How can I experience God’s “touch” today?
@@4333@@
From several channels. First, in quiet prayer, put your hand on your chest and say to yourself: I have been touched. Second, through a friend willing to stay with you: an embrace, a word, a bowl of hot soup. Third, through Scripture: read this chapter slowly and read the word “touch” into your heart. God’s touch often comes through people and through his word. If you feel alone for a long time, please find a safe person to walk with you, and if needed, professional help. If you long feel that no one comes near you, pray, and also actively find a reliable companion, and let God’s hand reach you through a human hand.
@@4334@@
IV. The Speechless Man Touched Twice (10:15–21)
@@4335@@
Then the text says: “When he had spoken these words to me, I set my face toward the ground, and was mute.” He fell again, unable even to speak. Then: “Behold, one in the likeness of the sons of men touched my lips. Then I opened my mouth, and spoke and said to him who stood before me, ‘My lord, by reason of the vision my sorrows have overtaken me, and I retain no strength.’” Touched. Lips. God not only raises people to stand; he also lets the mute open their mouths.
@@4336@@
Look at the first thing he said: “For how can the servant of this my lord talk with this my lord? For as for me, immediately there remained no strength in me. There was no breath left in me.” He was honest. He did not pretend to be strong and did not say “I am fine.” He said his helplessness just as it was. God does not despise such words. You can pray this way too: Lord, I have no strength, I cannot find words, I come to you just like this. Heaven hears a prayer like this.
@@4337@@
Then the text says: “Then one like the appearance of a man touched me again, and he strengthened me.” This is the second touch. The first raised him to stand; this one gave him strength. Then came the command: “Greatly beloved man, don’t be afraid. Peace be to you. Be strong. Yes, be strong.” See this order: first he touched, first he gave strength, and only then said, “be strong.” This is not asking a man without strength to make himself strong; it is first making him strong, and then telling him to be strong.
@@4338@@
You may have had such moments: told “be strong” and with no strength. This passage tells you: true strength is not gritted teeth; it is touched into you, spoken into you, given. In the end he said: “Let my lord speak; for you have strengthened me.” He had strength not because of himself but because of you. This is the strength of having no self.
@@4339@@
Then the visitor said: “Do you know why I have come to you?” He said he would still return to fight, and afterward the prince of Greece would come. We likewise do not turn it into a tool for predicting geopolitics. At the end he said: “I will tell you that which is inscribed in the writing of truth. There is no one who holds with me against these, but Michael your prince.” In heaven there is a book of truth; what God will accomplish is already written, not on a sudden whim. History is not a tangle of threads; one has already written its direction. You are not alone. This chapter ends very quietly: no dates, only one answer: your words were heard; you were touched twice; be strong; there is a book of truth.
@@4340@@
Please imagine: on your most weary night, you cannot say anything, only tears. Then a hand gently touches your lips, and you are able to open your mouth. This is not a romantic imagining but the picture of promise the text gives us: God knows our muteness, and knows how to let us open our mouths again. You only need to come; that is enough.
@@4341@@
Can I use the words “be strong” to comfort others?
@@4342@@
You can, but please follow the order of the text: first touch, then speak. To a person with no strength, saying “be strong” at once is often stopping his tears. You can first sit with him a while and say: I am here; ask him: what do you need? If needed, give him a bowl of hot soup, an embrace. When he has a little strength, then say: I believe you are beloved, and I believe God will give you strength. Then “be strong” will not become pressure but blessing. Also watch your tone: first accompany, then encourage, so that the other feels held rather than demanded of; this is God’s order.
@@4343@@
The first deviation is to turn “the prince of Persia withstood me twenty-one days” into “if a prayer has no answer, it must be blocked by a territorial spirit.” The point of the text is: from the first day, your words were heard; the description of the prince and of Michael has long been read in different ways, and these notes set them side by side and do not teach identifying or attacking territorial spirits.
@@4344@@
The second deviation is to turn the three weeks of fasting into “to get an answer you must fast for a certain number of days.” Daniel’s fasting was an expression of mourning and of humbling himself, not a condition for exchanging for an answer; those with health conditions should first consult medical care, and God looks at the heart.
@@4345@@
The third deviation is to declare that the man in linen is Christ, or to insist he is a particular angel, and belittle other readings. The one who comes in 10:5–6 has long been read variously as an angel, as Christ and so on; these notes set the views side by side and say no more than the text.
@@4346@@
The fourth deviation is to use “be strong” to tell the weak to be strong at once, or to blame them for not having enough faith. The order of the text is that he first touched and first gave strength, and only then said, be strong; toward those who are hurting or have lost their strength we first accompany and do not blame.
@@4347@@
The fifth deviation is to turn this chapter into a tool for predicting history, saying that the princes of Persia and Greece are certain countries or powers of today. The text does not say this; we set up no timetable and identify no one today.
@@4348@@
Someone uses this chapter to teach “territorial spirit warfare.” How should I respond?
@@4349@@
You can first affirm his seriousness about the spiritual world, then gently return to the text: what this chapter says is that Daniel’s prayer was heard from the first day; the prince and Michael are background the messenger gave Daniel, and there is no command for us to identify or attack the spirit of a place. You can ask: does this teaching make you trust God more, or become more tense? Then bring the focus back to 10:12: setting the heart to understand, humbling oneself before God, and God heard. No need to argue; bring the atmosphere back to quiet. Finally, bring it back to the text’s own point, so that people love the Lord more and are quieter, rather than more tense and more afraid of things the eye cannot see.
@@4350@@
The first question: we are very used to “not making people worry.” Children toward parents, parents toward children, friends among themselves, we are used to keeping mourning in the heart and saying with a smile, “I am fine.” Daniel was also a man who could endure, but in this chapter he honestly said, “I have no strength.” You can learn to say one true sentence in front of a suitable person too. If your situation does not allow telling people, you can first tell God.
@@4351@@
The second question: we are very used to the expectation that “asking brings an answer,” and easily read “no immediate response” as “God is ignoring me” or “I have not done enough.” This chapter gives another way of seeing: from the first day your words were heard; the delay is because there are things in heaven we cannot see. This is not a formula, and not every delay has an explanation, but it gives us a rest: he heard.
@@4352@@
It is very hard for me to say “I have no strength” in front of people. How do I begin?
@@4353@@
You can begin with three small steps. First, say it to God, in writing or aloud, not beautifully. Second, choose someone you trust and say only one sentence: I have been very tired lately. No need to explain much. Third, if the other responds well, say a little more; if not, do not blame yourself; try another person, or a pastor or counselor. Telling the truth is not weakness; it gives another a chance to love you, and gives God a chance to touch you. This is a practice that takes time; give yourself patience, and give those around you patience; God’s hand is always there.

VII. Living Out 321: First Touch, Then Speak: Be Strong
@@4355@@
Three foundations: Jesus is my example. In his extreme anguish an angel from heaven appeared to him, strengthening him; he is also the Lord who often stretched out his hand, touched people and raised them up. The Bible is my standard, because for the prince of Persia and Michael, and for the twenty-one days, we do not rely on guessing but on the point the text itself gives: from the first day, your words were heard. The Holy Spirit is my guide, because in weakness we do not need more gritted teeth; we need a Spirit who is with us, to touch our lips and give us strength.
@@4356@@
Two cores: Let Jesus reign, which means in delay still bringing our words to him, not explaining God’s silence for him, and letting him touch our lips and give us strength. Let Jesus receive all the glory, which means that when we stand up we say: it was not that I am strong; he gave me strength. One purpose: to build God’s system. This is a people who touch one another and strengthen one another: accompanying when someone mourns, waiting when someone is speechless, not hurrying when someone trembles; not teaching people to attack unseen powers but teaching them to be touched first and then stand.
@@4357@@
Change the concept and the life changes: when we think “being strong is my own responsibility,” our life is held up by gritted teeth; when we know “strength is given,” our life has rest. From the self-life to the no-self life: the self-life proves itself by pretending to be strong; the no-self life honestly says it has no strength, lets God touch, lets God give strength, and lets man be man and God be God.
@@4358@@
So when we read these hard-to-understand descriptions, we can rest in stopping where the text stops, neither adding nor taking away; to remain honest and humble about what is uncertain is itself a respect for God’s word.

What does “no self” have to do with “being touched”?
@@4360@@
The relationship is that being touched requires putting down defenses. A person who feels “I must hold up by myself” finds it hard to let others, even God, come near. Having no self is not disappearing but being willing to be touched. You can try: on an occasion where you want to pretend all is well, gently say one true sentence and see what happens. Most of the time you will find that the feeling of being held is stronger than holding up by yourself. Practicing no-self in this way, you will slowly learn that the rest of being held is deeper and longer lasting than the strength of toughing it out alone, and it also better lets you accompany others along the same road.

This “king” has long been read in two main ways: one holds that it is still the earlier “contemptible person” and the text only pulls the camera into his heart; the other holds that here the text turns to another, more end-time figure of self-exaltation; some hold that the two are, in the text, one description that advances layer by layer. These notes set the views side by side, do not decide for you, and do not turn any reading into an identification of a person today.</p><p>But three things are clear whichever way one reads it. First, his self-exaltation begins in the heart: “he will do as he pleases,” and “exalt himself above every god.” This is the same illness as the pride of the two kings in 4:30 and 5:20. Second, he has his god: the “god of fortresses,” to whom he gives gold, silver and precious stones; that is, a man without God in fact has a god, and his god is his power and his security. Third, his prospering has a boundary: until the indignation is accomplished.</p><p>The text describes it so not for us to guess who he is but for us to see that the end of pride, whatever name it bears, is the same end. This gives us a deep rest: we need not, and should not, identify that man for history; we only need not let our own heart walk the same road.</p>

<p><strong>The last verse of chapter 11 has no song of victory, only one quiet sentence: his end will come, and no one will help him.</strong></p><p>The last six verses are the hardest stretch of the chapter. The series of wars described in 11:40–45 has some details that do not fully fit the reconstruction of Hellenistic history through the ages, and so interpreters have divided here into several readings: some hold that it is still the same king and that the text uses “prophetic writing” whose details are not meant to correspond to the history books item by item; some hold that from here the text turns to a still later time; and some hold that the text uses a structure that advances layer by layer so that details of history and of a later matter reflect each other. We set them side by side among the issues, and say plainly: these notes do not use this passage to draw up any timetable and do not identify any person of today.</p><p>But look at what the text finally gives in this passage: a king who has won victory after victory enters the nations, takes the treasures and pitches the tents of his palace; and then one sentence: “he will come to his end, and no one will help him.” This “help” shares a root with the “help” Michael gave Daniel in chapter 10. Seen side by side, this chapter and the one before form a very moving contrast: God’s people have a great prince standing on their side, while the self-exalting man, in the end, has no one standing beside him.</p><p>So this chapter is not for teaching us to read the news but for teaching us to rest: however the nations move, whoever rises and whoever sinks, what the text gives us is an ending, and that ending is “appointed.” Our task is not to calculate but, today, to know God and be strong and take action.</p>
