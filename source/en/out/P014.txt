I. Someone Else’s Battlefield, Our Land (11:1–4)
@@4798@@
Peace to you, brothers and sisters. Today I would like to begin by asking you to imagine a picture: the place where you live has become someone else’s road to war. Great men meet over it, make alliances, break covenants, passing through again and again; and you are only the one passed through and decided about. Your home, your school, your church are all at the edge of someone else’s chessboard. If you have felt this, then Daniel chapter 11 was written especially for you.
@@4799@@
This chapter begins with a very unremarkable sentence: “As for me, in the first year of Darius the Mede, I stood up to confirm and strengthen him.” The speaker is still the messenger of chapter 10. Notice: as soon as he opens his mouth, he lifts our eyes from earth to heaven. The first year of Darius is just when Babylon had fallen; Daniel at that time did not know that in heaven someone was standing up and supporting. However fast empires change on earth, in heaven there is one who has stood all along.
@@4800@@
Then the messenger uses a very weighty word: “truth.” He says, “Now I will show you the truth.” The long run of kings and kings that follows is not a novel and not casual guessing but something spoken of as “true.” Three more kings in Persia; the fourth is far richer and stirs up all to attack Greece; then a mighty king rises, rules with great dominion and does as he pleases. But the text goes on: as soon as he rises, his kingdom breaks and is divided toward the four winds of heaven, and does not go to his posterity.
@@4801@@
Most readers through the ages have read this mighty king as the beginning of the Hellenistic age; for the details, the matchings differ from one reading to another, and we set them side by side among the issues. Here we do not match every name; we first hold on to a very practical reminder: the moment a man is strongest is often the moment his kingdom breaks most easily. This makes us think of the image in chapter 2: even the most majestic head of gold was only one stage.
@@4802@@
Please be quiet here a moment: have you put your security on something that looks very strong? A very secure job, a very influential person, a very large organization? These things are not necessarily bad, but what the text says is: all of them will have a time of breaking; and the one who was standing in the first year of Darius is still standing today.
@@4803@@
Think again: why did the messenger mention the first year of Darius in the very first sentence of chapter 11? Perhaps he wanted Daniel to know that everything he now saw had not begun to be arranged only today. Before you knew, heaven was already standing for you. Such seeing gives us a little more quiet and a little less panic in the face of uncertainty.
@@4804@@
Perhaps you stand in a very small place and feel your prayer is of no account. Please remember: in the first year of Darius, the one the messenger stood up to support was a small people passed through in turn by great empires. God in history often does his greatest work from the most unremarkable position; your position is among them.
@@4805@@
How can I use the sentence “I stood up to confirm and strengthen him” in my situation today?
@@4806@@
You can use it this way: take this verse as a reminder, not as a doctrine of angels. The reminder is: behind the situation you can see, there is a support you cannot see, which began in the first year of Darius. Each morning you can say one sentence to yourself: today, the place where I am is not only someone else’s battlefield but a place where someone stands beside me. Then name the person or matter you worry about most and hand it to the God who stands unmoved. This practice does not deny the hardness of your situation; it slowly brings your eyes back from seeing only the movements of great men to the God who has stood all along.
@@4807@@
II. Kings of the South and of the North, and One Word: Cannot Stand (11:5–20)
@@4808@@
From verse 5 the text begins to speak of the king of the south and the king of the north; over a dozen verses it is one coming and going after another: marriage, alliance, broken covenant, war, triumph, retreat. Many readers get lost here, because the names are not spelled out and the details are many. Most readers through the ages have understood the two kings as two dynasties of the Hellenistic age, around Egypt and Syria; the matchings of details differ from one reading to another. These notes set them side by side, do not match them one by one for you, and set no dates for these events.
@@4809@@
I would like to ask you not to be dragged away by the details but to hold on to three words the text itself repeats. The first is “stand.” The text says again and again that no one can stand before him, that the arms of the south cannot stand, that this thing will not stand. This word in the original is exactly the undercurrent of the whole book: stand. Human strength falls one after another. The second is “fall”: the commander will stumble, fall, and not be found. The third is the “glorious land”: the two kings go back and forth, and both must pass through it and use it.
@@4810@@
Notice that marriage: the daughter of the king of the south is sent to the king of the north to make a covenant. It looks like a covenant of peace. But the text says just one thing: the strength of this woman’s help cannot stand. The most beautiful covenants of men often wrap the deepest calculation, and the text finishes such a covenant with two words: cannot stand.
@@4811@@
There is one more detail worth a pause: the text says that “violent ones” want to “fulfill the vision” yet will fall. The meaning of the original is wanting to “make the vision stand.” This is a very painful reminder: some want to accomplish God’s word with their own hands, and fall as a result. God’s plan does not need us to push it forward by scheming.
@@4812@@
Not one line of this passage says which king God’s people should follow. This is important: the text does not teach us to take sides but to see that when great nations fight each other, God’s people are to fix their eyes on the God who stands unmoved, not on who wins and who loses. Then we will not lose our peace in the rise and fall of the news.
@@4813@@
Please take this passage as a mirror: in our life too there are many comings and goings between north and south, many beautiful alliances wrapping motives not so beautiful. You can ask yourself quietly: among the alliances and relationships I am now relying on, is there one that was gained by calculation? No need to hurry to condemn; simply to see honestly is already a step taken.
@@4814@@
One more thing is worth adding: although the text gives so much space to kings and kings, not one line puts God’s people against these kings to attack anyone. Their task is to “stand,” and to “know God.” This reminds us that in turbulent years, holding one’s own place matters far more than commenting on someone else’s place.
@@4815@@
When I read this long stretch of history it feels remote. How should I read it so that it helps?
@@4816@@
You can read it with three questions: First, in this passage, what can stand and what cannot? Second, the thing I am relying on now: which side does it belong to? Third, if I were the people being passed through, what comfort does this passage give me? Write these three questions in a notebook, and use them each time you read historical details like these. You will find that although the names are unfamiliar, human nature is very familiar; and what the text gives is not a history lesson but the wisdom of how to stand firm on someone else’s battlefield. This is a habit that builds up slowly; you need not understand every detail at once, and each time you see a little more, that is progress.

III. Light from the Original: Stand, Contemptible, Appointed Time, Know (11:1, 21, 27, 32)
@@4818@@
In the original of this chapter there are four words worth a slow reading. The first is “stand,” Hebrew עמד (amad). From the start of Daniel this word has been moving beneath the surface: standing before the king, standing before the image, standing before the throne in heaven. In this chapter it appears most often: cannot stand, no one can stand before him, I stood up to support. Human strength is a strength that cannot stand; and on God’s side, someone stands and supports.
@@4819@@
The second is “contemptible,” Hebrew נִבְזֶה (nivzeh). Literally “one who is despised,” a passive form: this man, in men’s eyes, originally had no right. He gains the kingdom not by lawful succession but by “smooth words” (חֲלַקְלַקּוֹת). This word appears again and again in 11:21, 11:32 and 11:34: smooth words are the language of this age. The truth is not always pleasant to hear, but smooth words most easily make people fall.
@@4820@@
The third is “appointed time,” Hebrew מוֹעֵד (mo'ed), often joined with קֵץ (end): “the end will still be at the appointed time.” This word recurs in 11:27, 11:29 and 11:35. Its meaning is “the designated time.” This time is not in the king’s hand but in God’s. The fourth is “know,” Hebrew יָדַע (yada), “knowing in relationship,” not merely knowing some information. “The people who know their God will be strong, and take action.” This sentence is the heart of the whole chapter.
@@4821@@
Finally, please join these four words into one line: standing is the mark of God’s side; smooth words are the language of the other side; the appointed time is the boundary God gives history; knowing is the only thing needed on our side. You need not grasp all the details of the original at once; only remember: in darkness, the one who knows God, held up, can stand.
@@4822@@
If you want to bring this lesson into daily life, you can try this small practice: whenever you hear news that makes you tense, first say silently, “the end belongs to God,” then say silently, “today I will know him,” and only then decide whether to keep watching and whether to forward it. These three steps take less than a minute, yet little by little they change your heart.
@@4823@@
Why are “smooth words” the key word of this chapter?
@@4824@@
Because it is the hardest power to guard against: it uses no sword, only good words. 11:21 says the contemptible man gains the kingdom with flattery; 11:32 says he corrupts, by flatteries, those who act wickedly against the covenant; 11:34 says many join themselves to the fallen wise with flatteries. Such words often sound very warm and reasonable, yet lead people away from the truth. When you hear words that are especially pleasing, you can pause three seconds first and ask yourself: is there truth in these words? What do they ask me to pay? This small pause is the beginning of drawing near to the truth. When you meet words especially pleasing and especially to your liking, take still more care, because what smooth words do best is make people lay down their guard.
@@4825@@
What is the difference between “knowing God” and “knowing a lot about God”?
@@4826@@
The difference is relationship. Knowing a lot about God can come from reading; knowing God needs time and ordinary days. Think: you know a friend not because you have read his file but because you have eaten with him, walked through low valleys with him, been silent together. Knowing God is the same: on ordinary days, spend a little time each day drawing near to him; on nights with no answer, still speak to him. In chapter 10 God first touched Daniel and gave him strength; the strength in this chapter shares a root with “support” in 11:1. So knowing God is not the result of gritted teeth but of one who is held up, still standing in the darkness.

IV. The Contemptible Man, Lying at One Table, and the Defiled Sanctuary (11:21–35)
@@4828@@
Then the text draws the camera close and rests it on one man: “In his place a contemptible person will stand up.” Many through the ages have read this “despised man” as a ruler who persecuted God’s people; others read from other angles, and we set them side by side. We need to say plainly: these notes do not connect this man to any person of today, nor use him to label anyone in the future in advance. What we need to recognize is his way: flattery, deceit, scheming, scattering wealth.
@@4829@@
We are not strangers to such a way. It rests not on lawful trust but on pleasing people, on smooth words, on handing out benefits to buy hearts. It exists in companies, it exists in clans, and sadly, sometimes it exists in churches too. But the text leaves one line: “...even for a time.” However refined his plans, they lie inside a boundary.
@@4830@@
Then there is that table. Two kings sit at one table, eating and telling lies, each thinking in his heart how to harm the other. The verdict the text gives this scene is short: the plan will not prosper, for “the end will still be at the appointed time.” The end is not in their strategies but in a timetable they cannot touch. We must be especially careful: this sentence does not teach us to count days; it teaches us to let go: the end belongs to God.
@@4831@@
Next, the camera turns to the sanctuary: his heart is against the holy covenant, he profanes the sanctuary, takes away the continual offering and sets up the abomination that makes desolate. This echoes the descriptions in chapters 8 and 9. For this passage too, different historical matchings have been offered through the ages; we set them side by side and set no date for it. In such darkness the text leaves two groups: one is the people drawn away by smooth words; the other is “the people who know their God,” who are strong and take action.
@@4832@@
Finally, the wise. They make many understand, yet fall by the sword, in flame, into captivity and plunder. The text gives their falling a meaning: to refine the rest of them, to make them pure and white. This is hope for those who suffer; please do not take this verse and say to someone who is suffering, “this is God refining you.” We should become the “little help” in the text: accompany first, and speak afterward.
@@4833@@
Perhaps you ask: since the text says this, how should we face real darkness? The answer: do not flee, do not exaggerate, and do not add our own lists to it. We honestly see the power of darkness, and honestly admit that we do not need to see all the details before we can stand firm. We need only, today, in our own place, to know God, and to become that little help to one person beside us.
@@4834@@
Notice one more detail: when the wise fall, the text says they are “helped with a little help.” These words are small but very true. God’s help is often not removing all the pain at once but giving strength little by little at the moment of greatest need. If we learn to treasure that little by little, we will not miss the grace God has already given for lack of seeing a great miracle.
@@4835@@
Does the fall of the wise mean that God lets good people suffer for a purpose?
@@4836@@
The text gives this situation one meaning; it does not say all suffering is for this purpose. To a person who is suffering, we should not hurry to explain on God’s behalf, nor say, “this is God refining you.” You can first sit beside him and ask: what do you need? When needed, give him a bowl of hot soup, an embrace, a stretch of time without words. When he has strength, if he himself asks, we can slowly read this verse together. This verse is hope written for a suffering people, not a standard answer in the hands of those standing by. If someone beside you is suffering, remember: accompanying needs no beautiful words, and a figure who does not leave is often more powerful than a hundred explanations.

@@4839@@

@@4843@@

The first question: we are very used to “keeping our heads down to survive” in a large environment. In an environment where faith cannot be expressed openly, how does this chapter help me?
@@4847@@

<p><strong>וְרַבִּים מִיְּשֵׁנֵי אַדְמַת־עָפָר יָקִיצוּ</strong>: “many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake”; <strong>רַבִּים</strong> (many) here does not necessarily mean “a part of the whole”; it can also mean “very many,” and it has long been read in different ways; see the issues.</p><p><strong>לְחַיֵּי עוֹלָם</strong>: “to everlasting life”; this is one of the few Old Testament verses that speak plainly of “eternal life.”</p>
@@4946@@
<p><strong>In the darkest place of the whole book, the text lights up one word: awake.</strong></p><p>Verse 2 of chapter 12 is one of the clearest Old Testament verses on resurrection. “Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake.” Death is called “sleep,” and sleep is something one wakes from. This verse gives all who have fallen for the faith a hope: falling is not the end. Do you remember chapter 11? The wise fell by the sword, in flame, into captivity and plunder; here the text answers them: they will awake.</p><p>The verse goes on to say that some will have everlasting life, and some shame and everlasting contempt. The scope of “many” has long been read in different ways: some read it as “all,” some as “many, that is, those concerned”; “everlasting contempt” too has been understood in different theological ways. We set them side by side among the issues. But first see the most direct point of the text: death is not the end; God has a final justice and also a final hope.</p><p>In the New Testament, Jesus and the apostles also speak again and again of resurrection and judgment (John 5:28–29, Acts 24:15). This quiets us: we need not build a complete end-time chart on this verse; we need only remember that those you love, and those who suffered for the Lord, are not forgotten. In God’s eyes they are asleep, and will awake.</p>

<p><strong>הַמַּשְׂכִּלִים</strong> (the wise): the same word as “the wise” in 11:33 and 11:35; 11:35 says they fall, 12:3 says they shine. The fallen will shine.</p><p><strong>וּמַצְדִּיקֵי הָרַבִּים</strong>: “those who turn many to righteousness”; a participle, showing continuing action; through the ages this phrase has been used to describe those who lead people toward God.</p>
@@4949@@
<p><strong>The wise who fall will shine; those who turn many to righteousness will shine like the stars forever.</strong></p><p>This verse is to be read together with chapter 11. 11:33 says the wise instruct many yet fall; 11:35 says they fall to refine them; 12:3 says they will shine as the brightness of the expanse. The same word, two scenes: on earth, they fall; in heaven, they shine. This is one of the greatest reversals of the whole book: failure in men’s eyes, glory in God’s.</p><p>Notice how the text describes these people: not “those of highest standing” and not “those of greatest achievement” but “those who turn many to righteousness.” The meaning of the original is turning people toward righteousness, toward God. This description does not belong to one office alone; anyone who, with life or with words, accompanies others to come nearer to God is within this description.</p><p>This verse is also an answer to those who fell by the sword and in flame in chapter 11: they did not fall for nothing. In men’s eyes they failed; in God’s eyes they are the brightness of the sky. If you have grieved for such a person, this verse is worth reading slowly and receiving slowly.</p><p>Stars do not shine by themselves; they are set by God in the sky to guide people in the night. In the same way, the wise do not have their own light; they are set by God in the night. This keeps us from pride and from discouragement: our task is not to shine the brightest but, in the night, not to go out.</p>
