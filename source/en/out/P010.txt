<p><strong>חָזוֹן נִרְאָה אֵלַי אֲנִי דָנִיֵּאל</strong> (a vision appeared to me, even to me, Daniel): the verb here is a passive form, “appeared” (נִרְאָה); Daniel did not go looking for the vision, it “appeared” to him. @@3138@@
<p><strong>The opening of chapter 8 has only one verb, “appeared”: the vision appeared to Daniel; he did not go looking for it.</strong></p><p>This chapter differs from chapter 7 in one obvious way: the language returns from Aramaic to Hebrew. Why? There have long been different views, and we have set them out in the stance of the whole book; we do not draw a conclusion here. <p><strong>The verb “magnify himself” appears four times in this chapter: the ram, the male goat, the little horn and the little king all use the same word.</strong></p><p>The text first gives us two animals: a ram standing by the river with two horns, the later one higher; and a male goat coming from the west, crossing the whole earth, not touching the ground, with a notable horn between his eyes. This is not a threat but a very quiet reminder: no horn can keep rising forever.</p>

<p><strong>The holy one in heaven asked a question we too often ask: “How long?” And the answer is a number made of “evenings and mornings.”</strong></p><p>Verse 13 is very special: Daniel heard two holy ones speaking to each other. One was speaking, and the other asked: “How long will the vision … be?” This question is not Daniel’s; it is asked by those in heaven. <p><strong>In this chapter, rarely, the text itself opens the symbols: the ram is Media and Persia; the male goat is Greece.</strong></p><p>In the whole book of Daniel there are a few times when the text tells us directly what the symbol means: in chapter 2, “you are the head of gold”; in chapter 7, “these great animals, which are four, are four kings”; in this chapter, “the ram which you saw, that had the two horns, they are the kings of Media and Persia.” These places are the steadiest footing when we read this book: what the text itself says, we follow in saying, without adding and without changing carelessly.</p><p>So in this passage there is nothing we need to “guess”: the two-horned ram is Media and Persia, two horns, one higher and one lower, the later one higher, which does not conflict with Persia’s coming later to surpass; the rough male goat is the king of Greece; the great horn between his eyes is the first king. The text goes this far, and gives him no name and no dates. We do not say more than the text.</p><p>Then the horn that was broken: “in the place where four stood up, four kingdoms will stand up out of the nation, but not with his power.” We have already seen this picture: when he was strong, the great horn was broken, and four came up in its place. The text does not say which four nations, and we do not chart a schedule. But notice one detail: “but not with his power.”</p><p>Why does the text say this specially? Perhaps as a reminder: <strong>power is diluted.</strong> A very great horn, once broken, is replaced by four that do not equal it. The powers people build, passed from one generation to the next, often become smaller, more fragmented, more quarrelsome than the original. I. Two Animals, One Verb (8:1–8)
@@3432@@
Peace, brothers and sisters. Have you ever, on some night, stared at the ceiling and asked one question: how long? How long will this illness last; how long will this unfairness last; how long will this prayer without an answer go on? In this lesson we read a very special picture: it is not only people who are asking; the holy ones in heaven are asking too. This is Daniel chapter 8.
@@3433@@
First notice a change. From this chapter on, the language of the original returns from Aramaic to Hebrew. Why? There have long been different views, and we set them side by side in the issues rather than conclude here. You do not need to agree with any one of them in order to read this chapter. We can quietly see this much: the long section before spoke of the affairs of the nations, and now, speaking of the sanctuary, the holy people and the end, it uses the holy people’s own language.
@@3434@@
The text says: “In the third year of the reign of king Belshazzar a vision appeared to me, even to me, Daniel, after that which appeared to me at the first.” Notice that verb: appeared. The vision appeared to him; he did not go looking for it. Then, in the vision, he was at Susa, by the river Ulai. He lifted up his eyes and looked, and there was a ram with two horns; both horns were high, and the later one was higher. “I saw the ram pushing westward, northward, and southward. @@3435@@
Next came a male goat, from the west, crossing the whole earth, not touching the ground, with a notable horn between his eyes. He ran at the ram, broke its two horns, threw it to the ground and trampled it. Then again that sentence: there was none who could deliver the ram out of his hand. Before a great power people are helpless; this is true, and the text does not deceive you. But look at the next verse: “The male goat magnified himself exceedingly. When he was strong, the great horn was broken.” When he was strong, the great horn was broken. The highest point of power is often also the beginning of the turn.
@@3436@@
Please remember the undercurrent of this chapter: magnifying oneself. This verb is used of the ram, of the male goat, of the little horn, and later of the little king. One word, four different subjects. It tells us that whichever power it is, as long as it forgets that it was given, it walks the same road and makes itself great. We heard this story in chapter 4: a king stood on the roof of his own palace and said, “Is not this what I have built?” This chapter is only the same story in the shape of different animals.
@@3437@@
One more thing must be said plainly. Later in this chapter the text itself opens the symbols: the two-horned ram is Media and Persia; the male goat is Greece. This is one of the rare places where the text says it directly. So we follow what it says, without adding and without carelessly changing. As for the names of “the first king” and of the “four kingdoms,” the text does not give them, and we do not speak for it. The purpose is to give us steady footing as we read the vision: what the text says, we say; what it does not say, we do not say for it.
@@3438@@
Why does “magnify himself” keep recurring in this chapter?
@@3439@@
Because the text wants us to see that pride is a road, not one person. The ram, the male goat, the little horn and the little king have different names but the same action: making themselves great. This reminder is not only for distant powers; it is for each of us. When we have been given something, that is when we most easily forget that it was given. You can use this word to examine yourself: today, in which matter did I want to make myself great? Then put it back in God’s hand. You might also write in a journal the one thought today in which you most wanted to make yourself great, and beside it write one line: this was given. After practicing this a few times you will find that the strength clenched in your heart slowly loosens.
@@3440@@
II. The Little Horn, the Sanctuary and the Question from Heaven (8:9–14)
@@3441@@
Out of one of the four horns came a little horn. Notice: first “little,” and then it grew great toward the south, toward the east and toward “the glorious land.” In the Bible, “the glorious land” often means the land God promised to his people. The direction of this horn pointed to where God’s people were.
@@3442@@
Then its three actions. First, it grew great to the host of heaven, cast some of the stars to the ground and trampled on them: it pulled the things of heaven down and put them under its feet. Second: “Yes, it magnified itself, even to the prince of the army; and it took away from him the continual burnt offering, and the place of his sanctuary was cast down.” This lays hands directly on God. Third, the sanctuary was cast down. For one person to carry off another’s home is one thing; to tear down the place where a people worship God is another, for that is where they meet with him.
@@3443@@
Then the text says something very heavy: “The army was given over to it together with the continual burnt offering through disobedience. It cast down truth to the ground, and it did its pleasure and prospered.” “Was given over” is in the passive. The text does not say whose sin it was, and does not say that every suffering is because of someone’s sin. Please do not turn this verse into blame on those who suffer. What we see is only this: evil has a season of prospering. If you are watching someone who speaks big words, tears down God’s house, and seems impossible to stop, you are not the first to be confused. The text wrote this honestly.
@@3444@@
About this little horn there have long been different readings. Many scholars think it refers to a king of the Greek period who defiled the sanctuary and abolished the continual burnt offering; some think the description also foreshadows a later figure; others stress that the text gives a pattern. We set the views side by side in the issues; we do not identify anyone today, and we do not set a timetable. What we see is its direction and its end.
@@3445@@
Then I heard a voice from heaven: “Then I heard a holy one speaking; and another holy one said to that certain one who spoke, ‘How long will the vision about the continual burnt offering, and the disobedience that makes desolate, to give both the sanctuary and the army to be trodden under foot be?’” Did you hear it? This question was not asked by Daniel; the holy one in heaven asked it. How many times in the Psalms the holy people cry like this: Yahweh, how long? This is not unbelief; it is an honest faith. If you have asked it, you need not feel weak because of it. Heaven has asked too. At this point, please pause, and quietly say your own “how long.” No need to dress it up, and no need for an answer.
@@3446@@
The answer is a number: “To two thousand and three hundred evenings and mornings. Then the sanctuary will be cleansed.” The original counts evenings and mornings. This number has long been read in different ways, and we do not use it to count days. For the point of the answer is not the number but the second half: the sanctuary will be cleansed. The verb in the original is more like saying that the sanctuary is declared right again. This destruction has a limit: there is an evening, and there is also a morning. For someone in the dark of night this is a great comfort: the night is numbered. This is the hope we can hold in our hands: not a date, but a direction.
@@3447@@
Does a question like “How long?” mean my faith is not enough?
@@3448@@
No. The text itself records that the holy one in heaven asked how long the vision would last, and the Psalms are full of such prayers. An honest question brings the question to God; it does not bury it in the heart, and it does not leave him. Unbelief is no longer bringing the question to God, or turning to trust some other way out. You can take “how long?” as a prayer and say it together with your tears. God did not blame the holy one in heaven and will not blame you. He gives only a gentle answer: there is a morning. If you often have this question lately, you can also find a safe friend or a pastor and say it aloud, and let someone ask it with you. That too is a way of being raised up.
@@3449@@
III. Light from the Original: Magnify Himself, Evenings and Mornings, Set Upright (8:4, 14, 18)
@@3450@@
Three words in the original of this chapter deserve a slow reading. The first is “magnify himself,” Hebrew הִגְדִּיל (higdil). Its root is גָּדַל (gadal), “to become great”; in the causative form the verb means “to make himself great.” It appears four times, in 8:4, 8, 11 and 25: the ram, the male goat, the little horn and the little king. You can think of it as a stamp: whoever wants to make himself great is under this stamp. And in the prayer of Daniel in chapter 9 we will see another kind of “great”: God’s greatness.
@@3451@@
The second is “evenings and mornings,” Hebrew עֶרֶב בֹּקֶר (erev boqer). Together these two words recall Genesis 1: there was evening and there was morning. The original does not say “days,” so whether the two thousand three hundred is 2,300 days or 1,150 days, with an offering each morning and evening, has long been read in different ways. We do not count days. What we see is the suggestion in this language: night was never the last word; after it comes morning.
@@3452@@
The third is “set me upright,” Hebrew וַיַּעֲמִידֵנִי (vaya'amideni). Its root is עָמַד (amad), “to stand,” here in the causative form: “made me stand.” Do you remember 8:4? “No animals could stand before him”: the same root. This chapter has an undercurrent of standing. The ram could not stand; Daniel, asleep on the ground, was raised by a hand and stood where he had stood. We are able to stand not because we are strong but because we have been raised.
@@3453@@
These three words together are the route of this chapter: those who make themselves great cannot stand; the one in the dark of night waits for the morning; the one who is frightened and asleep is raised up. You can ask yourself: which word am I in lately? Am I wanting to make myself great, waiting in the dark, or needing to be raised up? Today you might choose one of the three words as your prayer for this week.
@@3454@@
How does the root “stand” help me live today?
@@3455@@
It helps this way: you do not have to stand by your own strength. In this chapter the ones who cannot stand are the proud beasts, and the one who can stand is the one who was raised up. So when you feel you can hardly stand, do not blame yourself first; lift up your hands first and say: Lord, raise me up. Then do one small thing today: get up, wash, make one phone call. These small things are the first steps of someone who has been raised up. Your standing is not proof that you are strong; it is proof that he raised you. So you need not become the strongest person, only a person willing to be raised. Each morning, say first: Lord, raise me; and then begin your day.
@@3456@@
How should I read the two thousand three hundred evenings and mornings so that I do not go astray?
@@3457@@
You can read it this way. First, admit there are different readings, and do not treat one as the only one. Second, do not use it to calculate dates, and do not use it to endorse any event. Third, hold the text’s own point: this destruction has a limit, and the sanctuary will be vindicated. Fourth, let it change how you live today, not how you calculate tomorrow. Read this way you need not be an expert, and you will not fall into the pit of timetables; you will instead be quieter and more hopeful. The road the text gives is quiet: no arguing, no calculating, only holding this one point and bringing it into today’s prayer.
@@3458@@
IV. Gabriel’s Explanation and the Man Who Was Sick for Some Days (8:15–27)
@@3459@@
When Daniel had seen the vision, he “sought to understand.” This is a very good habit: having seen, he was not in a hurry to speak, nor in a hurry to worry; he wanted to understand. Then one with the appearance of a man stood before him, and at the same time a voice called: “Gabriel, make this man understand the vision.” This is the first time in the whole Bible that an angel’s name appears. Gabriel means “mighty one of God.” Notice that a voice commanded him. Understanding is given; it is not something you squeeze out by hard thinking.
@@3460@@
Then Daniel’s reaction: “So he came near where I stood; and when he came, I was frightened, and fell on my face; but he said to me, ‘Understand, son of man; for the vision belongs to the time of the end.’” Even Daniel was frightened. The angel called him “son of man,” the Hebrew form of address, meaning: you are a human being, a finite one. In Ezekiel, God addresses the prophet this way too. This is a different word and a different position from the one in chapter 7 who came with the clouds of the sky like a son of man; we do not mix the two together. This form of address is not praise but a reminder: you are only a man, so you need to be raised up.
@@3461@@
Then the text says: “Now as he was speaking with me, I fell into a deep sleep with my face toward the ground; but he touched me, and set me upright.” Even before the most important revelation, a man falls asleep. And the first thing the angel did was not to blame him but to touch him and set him upright. Before, the ram could not stand; now Daniel is raised up by a hand. The first act of God’s messenger toward a weak man is to raise him up. Then the angel said: this belongs to the appointed time of the end. What was given to Daniel was understanding, not a schedule of days. This is an important order: first be raised, and only then have strength to stand; first be touched, and only then have strength to work.
@@3462@@
At the end of his explanation the angel speaks of a king “of fierce face.” The text gives his features: understanding dark sentences, great in power but not by his own power, prospering in what he does, destroying the mighty ones and the holy people, magnifying himself in his heart, standing up against the prince of princes; “but he will be broken without hand.” This reminds us of the stone in chapter 2 that was cut out without hands. The text does not connect the two, and we say only that it reminds us: people cannot save people, but there is One who does not need human hands. Who this king is we set side by side in the views; we do not identify anyone today.
@@3463@@
Finally the angel said: “seal up the vision.” Not because it is unimportant, but because the time has not yet come. Then comes the most ordinary and most comforting verse: “I, Daniel, fainted, and was sick for some days. Then I rose up, and did the king’s business. I wondered at the vision, but no one understood it.” Having seen the greatest vision, he was sick. Even spiritual people get sick. Then he rose up. This rising is something only one who has been raised up can do. Then he did the king’s business: he returned to his post. Even the one who saw did not understand it all. The practice this verse gives us is: when you do not understand, return to your post. So today, if you feel you do not understand, do not hurry to find answers; first return to your post and do well the one thing in front of you. That is a very good beginning.
@@3464@@
Having seen so great a vision, why did Daniel still go back to the king’s business?
@@3465@@
Because a vision is not meant to make people leave their posts but to make them more faithful at their posts. He saw suffering and the end, yet he did not resign, hide, or spread panic and preach it everywhere. He was sick, he rested, and then he rose and did the work in front of him. This is a very steady posture when we do not understand: not chasing the answer with our own strength, and not putting down today’s responsibility. Your post may be an office, a kitchen, a classroom or a hospital ward; doing it well is also worship. This is not passivity but a deeper faithfulness: you hand the outcome to God and finish today steadily.
@@3466@@
The first deviation is to calculate the “two thousand three hundred” as calendar dates, or to match it to some event of today. The original has two thousand three hundred evenings and mornings, which has long been read in different ways; these notes set them side by side and do not count days. What the text wants us to see is that this destruction has a limit.
@@3467@@
The second deviation is to identify the little horn or the little king as some figure or regime of today, calling him the antichrist. The text gives features and an end; it names no one today. Identifying someone makes the congregation panic and hurts the innocent. The fruit of this chapter is endurance, not panic.
@@3468@@
The third deviation is to confuse the “son of man” of 8:17 with the “one like a son of man” of chapter 7. The former is Hebrew, addressing Daniel as a finite human being; the latter is Aramaic, describing the one who came with the clouds of the sky. The wording and the position of the two differ, and we do not force them together.
@@3469@@
The fourth deviation is to turn the “through disobedience” of 8:12 into “people who suffer must have done something wrong.” The text does not say whose sin it was, and does not say that every suffering is because of someone’s sin. For the suffering we do not blame; we only accompany.
@@3470@@
The fifth deviation is to use “seal up” as a reason to refuse to learn, or conversely to use our own guesses to add authority to this vision. “Seal up” says the time has not yet come; we need not understand it all now, and we need not pretend to.
@@3471@@
What should I say to someone who is very sure and gives the exact date of the “two thousand three hundred days”?
@@3472@@
You can first affirm his zeal for God’s word, then ask gently two questions. First, does the original say days, or evenings and mornings? Second, does this way of counting make you love the Lord more and be more patient, or become more anxious and more eager to persuade others? Then return to the purpose Gabriel gave: to make people understand, not to give a schedule of days. You need not argue and need not win; you can close with one sentence: what comforts me most in this chapter is that the night is numbered and the morning will come. And remember to stay gentle as you answer, so it does not turn into a debate; your aim is to bring him, and yourself, back to the purpose Gabriel gave: understanding, and hope.
@@3473@@
The first question: we are used to “calculating.” Calculating feng shui, calculating days, calculating luck, calculating the right time; in a Chinese setting this is often a search for a little security in the midst of unease. The two thousand three hundred of this chapter is easily taken up for calculating. But the text wants us to see that what God gives is understanding, not a schedule of days; the morning will come, not on a particular date. You can be quiet and patient; do not let “calculating” take the place of “trusting.”
@@3474@@
The second question: we often “hold on.” However tired, we finish the work; however much it hurts, we say nothing; however little we understand, we pretend there is no problem. This holding on is a virtue, and also a burden. This chapter gives another posture: being raised up. Daniel was frightened, fell asleep, got sick, and then, raised by a hand, rose to do his work. You can let yourself be raised up first, and then return to your post. If your weariness or unease has lasted a long time, please find a safe person or professional help, and do not hold on alone.
@@3475@@
I am used to “holding on.” How do I learn to “be raised up”?
@@3476@@
You can start with small things. First, say it aloud: find someone reliable, or tell God, “I cannot hold on.” Second, rest. Being sick for some days was Daniel’s reality; you can give yourself a day, an afternoon. Third, accept help: let someone take one small thing from you. Fourth, return to your post with a lighter posture. Being raised up is not weakness; it is admitting: I am a man, a finite man, and he is the God who raises people. In this way your faithfulness will last longer than holding on. The process need not be finished at once; every small “being raised up” is God’s grace, and every one is a step toward being more like Jesus.
@@3477@@
VII. Living Out 321: The Night Is Numbered, and the Lord Raises Me Up
@@3478@@
Three foundations: Jesus is my example. He took Peter’s mother-in-law by the hand and raised her up, and he stretched out his hand and touched a leper, and said, “I will. Be made clean.” He is also the Lord who, when the disciples fell asleep, did not blame them but brought them back to prayer. The Bible is my standard: for “how long?” and “two thousand three hundred” we do not rely on guessing but on the point the text itself gives: there is a limit, and the sanctuary will be vindicated. The Holy Spirit is my guide: when we do not understand, we do not need more panic; we need a Spirit who is with us, to make me still and to stand me up.
@@3479@@
Two cores: Let Jesus reign, which means handing the question “how long?” to him, not putting a schedule of days in place of trust in him, and, once raised up, returning to today’s duties and post. Let Jesus receive all the glory, which means saying, as we stand up: it was not that I am strong, it was that he raised me. One purpose: to build God’s system. This system is a people who have been raised up, who accompany one another when they do not understand, who do not frighten one another and do not set up schedules of days; each returns to his post and faithfully does the work in front of him.
