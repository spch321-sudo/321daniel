How does “the third year of the reign of Jehoiakim” fit with the dating of 2 Kings 24 and Jeremiah?
@@5413@@
Daniel says the exile happened in the third year of Jehoiakim’s reign (1:1), but the dating of 2 Kings 24:1 and Jeremiah 25:1 and 46:2 looks different: Jehoiakim’s fourth year corresponds to Nebuchadnezzar’s first year. How are these to be read together?
@@5414@@
Two ways of reckoning
@@5415@@
Babylon was accustomed to count the year a new king came to the throne as his accession year and the next year as year one; Judah used another reckoning. With the two counts differing, the same year can differ by one, so the third year and the fourth year can refer to the same stretch of time.
@@5416@@
Two campaigns
@@5417@@
Nebuchadnezzar first pressed Judah in the third year of Jehoiakim and took away a group of people; later Jehoiakim rebelled, and then came a larger military campaign. Daniel records the first.
@@5418@@
A historical discrepancy
@@5419@@
Some hold this is a gap in the author’s memory or sources and regard it as evidence of a later work.
@@5420@@
These notes take the first two readings as primary: the two ways of reckoning in use together, and the two campaigns, both let the text agree with itself. Whatever the position, the point lies in 1:2: the Lord gave the king into his hand.
@@5421@@
Do not let details of dates like these turn into a dispute over “the Bible has errors” or “my view is right.” First hold on to the subject of verse 2, and deal with the dates slowly afterward.

God’s sovereignty

The subject of the text is “the Lord” (אֲדֹנָי); it is he who gave the king over, and it was not that Babylon prevailed over him.

Dictated by the scene
@@5477@@
The Chaldeans spoke to the king in Aramaic (2:4), and the narrative goes on being written in that language; Aramaic was the common language of the courts and trade of the Near East at that time.

Editing and composition
@@5481@@

Why is there judgment before the kingdom is given?
@@5883@@
Judgment as the handling of justice
@@5884@@
Evil is dealt with and those who were wronged are vindicated (7:22); without this step, the giving of the kingdom would be incomplete.
@@5885@@
The judgment is a “seated” one
@@5886@@
The judgment in 7:10 and 7:26 is quiet and orderly, not emotional revenge.
@@5887@@
The kingdom is given
@@5888@@
After the judgment the kingdom is given to the son of man and the holy ones; this is God’s sovereignty, not human merit.
@@5889@@
These notes set the three together: God’s judgment is in justice and also in order; the kingdom is given, not seized.
@@5890@@
Do not use the theme of judgment to frighten people or to find excuses for your own revenge; you need only hand matters to the one who sits in judgment.

@@5892@@
Why does the original return to Hebrew from chapter 8?
@@5893@@
A shift of structure and theme
@@5894@@
2:4–7:28 is in Aramaic and speaks of the nations and the history of the world; from chapter 8 the return to Hebrew speaks of the sanctuary, the holy people and the end; language and theme correspond.
@@5895@@
Discussion of composition and editing
@@5896@@
Scholars hold different views on the origin of this bilingual structure, including different stages of composition, editing and intended readers; these discussions have been set side by side in the book-wide positions.
@@5897@@
A position that does not affect reading
@@5898@@
However this change of language is explained, the message of the text does not change: God rules, and the holy ones receive the kingdom.
