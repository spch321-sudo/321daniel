@@2487@@
The most dangerous law is not the one that plainly targets you, but the one that looks ordinary and happens to keep you from worshiping as usual. Then Darius signed. This act made the king too a prisoner: the law he had established, even he could not take back. A law made by man can trap the one who made it, and that is a foreshadowing. But please pause here first and think: in your own setting, are there seemingly ordinary rules that make you pay a price to worship as usual? They may not be a law; they may be a habit, a pressure, or a phrase like “everyone does it.”
@@2488@@
Daniel’s daily life was not the display of a perfect personality but long years of faithfulness: on time, honest, not greedy, not lazy. These very ordinary things look unremarkable in peaceful days, but when pressure came they became his firmest protection. We easily want to become brave only in a moment of crisis and forget that courage is trained, little by little, in ordinary life.
@@2489@@
Why can devotion be treated as a crime?
@@2490@@
Because an innocent person makes other people’s guilt show, and because those in power demand total loyalty, while a devout person gives his “final loyalty” to God. These two reasons often exist together. The text does not ask us to go looking for chances to be persecuted, nor to call every criticism persecution. What we can do is what Daniel did: first make our daily life one no one can find fault with, then let God be Lord. You can begin with one small thing near you, and make your daily life a little cleaner and a little more honest.
@@2491@@
II. The Window Was Open (6:10–15)
@@2492@@
Now we come to the quietest verse of this chapter: “When Daniel knew that the writing was signed, he went into his house (now his windows were open in his room toward Jerusalem) and he kneeled on his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did before.” He knew, and then, as before. Notice what he did not do: he did not call a press conference, did not rally companions, did not go around asking for help, and did not hide. He simply went home, went upstairs, and went to that window. The greatest witness is often not a big action but carrying on as usual under pressure.
@@2493@@
Notice a few details. First, the window faced Jerusalem. This was Solomon’s hope in the temple dedication prayer: when the people are carried away captive and pray toward this house, God will hear. His window was a memory of home and also a direction of hope. Second, the window was open; the original suggests it was always open, not for today but as his daily habit. He did not open the window specially to make a statement, nor close it to avoid trouble. Third, he knelt, prayed and gave thanks. Do you hear it? A man about to be thrown into the lions’ den has thanksgiving in his prayer. Fourth, “as before”: these words are the strength of the whole chapter. He did not increase the number of times because of danger, and did not reduce it because of danger. He did not perform, and he did not retreat.
@@2494@@
Then they came and found Daniel praying and making petition before his God. They went to the king, first made the king nod, and then brought up Daniel’s name, calling him “Daniel, who is of the children of the captivity of Judah.” Notice what they called him: not “your president,” not “Daniel, who has served the king for years,” but only “of the captivity.” It is a stripping of identity: turning a man from a contributor into an outsider. And the phrase “pays no attention to you” is exactly the wording used against the three friends in chapter 3. The enemy’s language is always alike.
@@2495@@
Then the king: “Then the king, when he heard these words, was very displeased, and set his heart on Daniel to deliver him; and he labored until the going down of the sun to rescue him.” This king was not a bad man; he loved this man, he wanted to save him, and he spent the whole day looking for a way. But his way was blocked by his own signature. The accusers came again: “Know, O king, that it is a law of the Medes and Persians, that no decree nor statute which the king establishes may be changed.” Human power can set up laws, but it cannot save a man from a law it has set up itself. Do you remember Nebuchadnezzar’s sentence in chapter 3? Who is that god who will deliver you out of my hands? In this chapter the king himself becomes the one asking the question: who can deliver me from the law I made myself? Only God can answer that question.
@@2496@@
If I cannot kneel down and pray three times every day, is my prayer not enough?
@@2497@@
No. Daniel’s “three times a day” was his devotional habit over many years, not a number set by the law. The point of this chapter is “as before”: continuity, not the number. You can find a rhythm that suits you, morning, noon, bedtime, or one session of ten minutes; what matters is that under pressure you do not change it out of fear. If your body, your work or the care of your family makes a fixed schedule impossible, God also sees your heart, and you can begin with the smallest session. If your time cannot be fixed, you can fix a “starting point” instead, for example one quiet minute before you turn on the computer each time.
@@2498@@
III. Light from the Original: As Before, Serve, Trust (6:4, 10, 16, 23)
@@2499@@
The original of this chapter has three words worth reading slowly. The first is “as before,” Aramaic מִן־קַדְמַת דְּנָה (min qadmat denah), meaning “from earlier times,” that is, continuity over many years. “As before” stresses not this one time but the long years. You can imagine that a man who does not waver in the fire did not learn not to waver in the fire; he stood firm in ordinary days, one day at a time.
@@2500@@
The second is “serve,” Aramaic פְּלַח (pelach), which we already met in chapter 3: “our God whom we serve.” In 6:16 the king of this chapter says: “Your God whom you serve continually,” and the original uses תְּדִירָא (tedira), meaning “continually.” This word appears only twice in the whole book, both in this chapter, both spoken by the king. The king did not know Daniel’s God, but he saw Daniel’s long years of service. Witness is often seen by unbelievers in everyday life.
@@2501@@
The third is “trust,” Aramaic הֵימִן (hemin). 6:23 says: “because he had trusted in his God.” This word shares a root with the “faithful” (מְהֵימַן) of 6:4. His faithfulness and his trust are one and the same thing: he was faithful because he trusted, and he trusted, so he was faithful. This means we need not think of “trust” as a feeling; it is a continual, everyday, steady entrusting.
@@2502@@
Put these three words together and they are the route of this chapter: “as before” is time; “serve” is the object; “trust” is the foundation. You can ask yourself: do I have an “as before” habit? Whom do I serve? What do I trust? These three questions are much easier to ask in ordinary days than at the edge of the lions’ den.
@@2503@@
Today you can use these three words for a very simple check. As before: do I have a fixed time each day to meet with God? Serve: where have my time and strength been flowing lately, and to whom? Trust: when pressure comes, do I put my weight on my own methods, or in God’s hand? You need not answer everything at once; answer one a day, and bring the answer into prayer.
@@2504@@
Does “innocence” mean that Daniel had no sin at all?
@@2505@@
No. The original זָכוּ (zakhu) is a courtroom word meaning that on this one accusation he was clear. Elsewhere Daniel often confessed his own sins and the people’s (see chapter 9). When he said this he was bearing witness to the king: before God and before the king I have done nothing to harm the king. So we need not read this verse as “the righteous do not suffer,” and we need not be afraid, because of our weaknesses, to say “in this matter I am clear.” Honesty is needed on both sides. Honestly admitting weakness does not diminish your witness; it makes people believe you more. His honesty made his witness stronger.
@@2506@@
“Trust” and “faithful” share one root; what does that mean for us?
@@2507@@
It means that faithfulness is not held up by gritting our teeth; it grows from trust. If you only think “I must be more faithful,” you will be very tired; if you think “I will trust more in the God who is trustworthy,” you will find that faithfulness is a natural result. So when you feel you are not faithful enough, you can first ask: have I put my weight in a place that cannot bear it? Then slowly move it into God’s hand. Once a week, look back and see where you put your weight most steadily. You need not change all at once; just move your weight a little toward God each day.
@@2508@@
IV. The Mouth of the Den, Dawn, and Two Endings (6:16–28)
@@2509@@
Daniel was thrown into the den. The text does not describe his reaction; it records only the king’s sentence: “Your God whom you serve continually, he will deliver you.” This sentence is a little like comfort, and more like a man with no assurance grasping, at the last moment, at the one hope left. Notice that word: continually. What the king saw was not one brave moment of Daniel’s but his long years. A person’s trust in a time of crisis is built up, day by day, in ordinary days. Then a stone was laid at the mouth of the den, and the king sealed it with his own signet and the signet of his nobles. This picture reminds us of another mouth of a tomb, another stone; we do not treat it as a type, we only say: it reminds us.
@@2510@@
Then the king’s night: “passed the night fasting. No musical instruments were brought before him; and his sleep fled from him.” That night the king was less at peace than Daniel. Human power can seal a den, but it cannot seal a conscience. At dawn the next day the king went in haste to the den of lions and cried out in a troubled voice: “Daniel, servant of the living God, is your God, whom you serve continually, able to deliver you from the lions?” Look at his title for him: servant of the living God. He had not said that yesterday. One night of silence changed his title.
@@2511@@
Then Daniel’s voice, and his first words: “O king, live forever!” A man who had just come out of the lions’ den did not blame, did not accuse, did not cry injustice; he first blessed the king who had thrown him in. When hurt, still blessing: this is one picture of selflessness. Then he gave his witness: “My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions’ mouths, and they have not hurt me; because as before him innocence was found in me; and also before you, O king, I have done no harm.” The one who shut the lions’ mouths was God, not his prayer and not his faith, but the angel God sent. The text says: “no kind of harm was found on him, because he had trusted in his God.” This sentence is not saying how great Daniel’s faith was, but that the One he trusted is trustworthy.
@@2512@@
Finally, two endings. One is very heavy: the accusers, together with their wives and children, were thrown into the den. We cannot pretend this verse is not there. Ancient laws often punished whole families; the text describes and does not say it was good. Deuteronomy 24:16 says each man shall be put to death for his own sin. When we read this verse we are not celebrating and not making excuses for any violence; we only see that the one who used harming others as his means finally fell into the pit he dug himself, and that innocent family members were drawn in as well, which is the bitter fruit of sin.
@@2513@@
The other is very glorious: King Darius sent a decree to all the earth: “men tremble and fear before the God of Daniel; for he is the living God, and steadfast forever. His kingdom is that which will not be destroyed. His dominion will be even to the end.” Do you remember chapter 4? Nebuchadnezzar’s decree said almost the same words. Two foreign kings bear witness to all the earth of the same God. The last verse: “So this Daniel prospered in the reign of Darius, and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian.” He outlived one empire, and then another. From the youth of chapter 1 he walked to here, a man who carried on as usual: resolving as usual, serving as usual, praying as usual.
@@2514@@
Does the story of the lions’ den mean that as long as you pray, you will surely not be hurt?
@@2515@@
No. In chapter 3 of the same book the three companions said: “But if not.” They might be saved or might not, but their faithfulness was not conditional on the result. Daniel’s rescue was God’s work, and we do not turn it into a formula. Many faithful people prayed and still suffered, even died as martyrs, and it was not because their faith was less. The hope of this chapter is that God is with us in the den, and that in the dark of night God was not asleep. So when we read this chapter we can say both “God is trustworthy” and “I will still hold fast.” We do not make promises on God’s behalf that he did not make, and we do not take away the comfort he has already given.
@@2516@@
The first deviation is to teach Daniel’s prayer as “believers may break any government law at any time.” Daniel was faithful in serving the king in everything reasonable and gave way only on the one matter of “asking from God.” Acts 5:29 says, “We must obey God rather than men,” which is a line between obedience and apostasy, not a pass for defiance. We need to discern, not to make excuses.
@@2517@@
The second deviation is to teach the punishment of the accusers’ wives and children in 6:24 as a retribution God delights in, or to use it to curse enemies. The text describes an ancient punishment that fell on whole families and does not say it was good; Deuteronomy 24:16 says each man shall be put to death for his own sin. We do not defend any violence, and we do not take this verse as material for revenge.
@@2518@@
The third deviation is to teach Daniel’s rescue as “as long as you pray, you will surely not be hurt.” His rescue was God’s work; chapter 3’s “But if not” is in the same book. Some people prayed and still suffered, even died as martyrs, and it was not because they had less faith. Do not use this chapter to blame anyone who is suffering.
@@2519@@
The fourth deviation is to teach “praying as usual” as a public performance, or to encourage public declarations in dangerous places. Daniel went back to his own house with his window open; he did not call a press conference. For brothers and sisters under persecution, we must protect people even more, and not make public any details that could be traced. Quiet is not weakness; protecting is also love.
@@2520@@
The fifth deviation is to turn this chapter into a slogan of “fighting the world.” Daniel did not curse the king, did not criticize the system, and did not rally crowds; he simply carried on as usual. His witness began with a daily life no one could find fault with, went on to kneeling under pressure, and then to blessing when hurt. This is a different kind of power: not the power of confrontation, but a quiet, faithful power.
@@2521@@
If I am in a setting where I cannot make my faith public, how do I “carry on as usual”?
@@2522@@
Carrying on as usual is not necessarily public. Within safe limits you can keep your relationship with God: quiet prayer, thanksgiving in the heart, low-voiced intercession with trusted companions. Daniel’s window was open, but he was in his own house. You do not need to put yourself or others in danger to prove your faith. Protecting yourself and your brothers and sisters is wisdom; staying open to God in your heart is faithfulness. God sees, and God remembers. Your peace is that God is still with you, not that the situation is certainly safe. Protecting people is the gentlest application of this chapter.
@@2523@@
The first question: we are very used to “going along.” Going along with the boss, with the family, with the circumstances; in a Chinese setting this is often a virtue and also a kind of wisdom. But after going along for a long time we slowly find that some things have been quietly adjusted: prayer time has been shortened, attendance on the Lord’s Day has dropped, and the thanks before a meal has become an embarrassed motion. This chapter does not ask us not to go along; it invites us to ask ourselves: in which things has my going along become a change? If one day a small rule made it cost you something to carry on as usual, what would you do?
@@2524@@
The second question: we often say “keep a low profile,” and we are very afraid of “being talked about.” In this chapter Daniel was neither high-profile nor hiding. His way was to carry on as usual. This gives us a great insight: a low profile does not mean hiding your faith; a high profile does not mean courage. True courage is not changing your daily life because of other people’s eyes. You can stay faithful in quietness, and when needed, calmly state your position. The point is not the volume but the continuity.
@@2525@@
What should I do if my family does not understand my praying, or even opposes it?
@@2526@@
You can respect first, then persevere. First respect your family’s feelings, without criticizing or arguing; then, within what does not provoke conflict, find a fixed time and place, such as early morning, bedtime or a quiet corner, to meet with God. You can also, in your actions, love your family well first and let your daily life be a witness. If there is pressure, bring the matter to a trusted pastor or companion and pray together; do not carry it alone. Daniel’s way was to carry on as usual, not to confront. If you feel powerless, you can also ask a companion to keep a fixed time of prayer for you.
@@2527@@
VII. Living Out 321: The Window Open Toward Him
@@2528@@
The three foundations: Jesus is my Role Model. Luke says Jesus came out and went, as his custom was, to the Mount of Olives, and under the greatest pressure still returned to the Father and said: “Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done.” The Bible is my Standard, because a window toward Jerusalem comes from God’s word and God’s promise, not from emotion. The Holy Spirit is my Guide, because under pressure we do not need more willpower; what we need is a Spirit who is with us, so that I can still kneel.
@@2529@@
The two core values: Let Jesus be King means that before any law, rule or unwritten rule, I still return to him first. Let Jesus receive all the glory means that when we are rescued, we say: God sent his angel and shut the lions’ mouths; it was not my faith and not my cleverness. The one purpose: to build God’s system. In every kingdom there are people whose windows are open toward him; they are not confronting and not performing, only carrying on as usual. People like this, reminding one another, become a silent but firm witness.
@@2530@@
Changing our mindset changes our life: when we think “my circumstances decide my worship,” our life is pushed along by circumstances; when we know “God is the reason I worship,” our life has roots. From self to selflessness: the person with self says “first I protect myself, then I talk about worship,” and the selfless person says “first I return into his presence, and I leave the rest in his hand.”
@@2531@@
How do we concretely practice “as usual” in everyday life?
@@2532@@
You can begin with a fixed time and place, for example after getting up each morning or before bed each night, and prepare a “window” for yourself: a small, fixed place to meet with God. Add a very small ritual, such as first saying one sentence of thanks and then one sentence of request. When pressure comes, do not make it harder and do not suddenly stop; just keep the smallest session. A month later you will find that this small habit has become your steady strength. A month later you will see that this small place has shielded you from many unnecessary shakings.
@@2533@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, One Window
@@2534@@
The proud type says, “I must let people see my faith, or else it is weakness.” This motive may come from courage, but it may also be mixed with wanting to be seen. Daniel did not call a press conference; he simply carried on as usual. You can ask yourself: am I doing this to be seen, or for God?
@@2535@@
The fearful type says, “I’m afraid that if I pray as usual, I will lose my job, my relationships, even my safety.” This fear is real. This chapter does not say there is no danger; it says God is in the den, and God is at dawn. If your situation is truly dangerous, please first protect yourself and others, and stay open to God in your heart; this too is carrying on as usual.
@@2536@@
The ashamed type says, “I have already changed my habits; I have no right to go back.” Your window can be opened again. God is not counting how many days you closed it; he is at the window, waiting for you to come back. Today is the first time.
@@2537@@
The wounded type says, “I was hurt because of my faith; I dare not carry on as usual again.” That wound is real. Please first find a safe person for yourself and tell your story. Do not force yourself to go back to the way things were all at once; you can begin with the smallest session, and God does not hurry you.
@@2538@@
First, every day at a fixed time and place, pray and give thanks quietly for five minutes, not shortening it because of busyness or circumstances.
@@2539@@
Second, write down the most recent spiritual habit you changed because you feared trouble, and write down one smallest step to restore it.
@@2540@@
Third, find a companion and agree to remind each other each day with one phrase: as usual; no showing off, no comparing.
@@2541@@
Fourth, for someone who has hurt you, say one word of blessing each day, without needing him to know.
@@2542@@
Fifth, in the setting where you are, do one unnoticed daily thing that no one can find fault with, so that your witness builds up in ordinary days.

What this chapter gives us is not a story about courage but a story about continuance: one man who, under pressure, carried on as before.

<p><strong>This chapter is the hinge of the whole book: the six chapters before are stories, and the six after are visions, beginning with this night.</strong></p><p>Chapter 7 differs from chapters 1–6 in one important way: the earlier chapters are stories about “others,” telling of Daniel and his three friends living in the court of foreign kings; this chapter is “Daniel’s own” dream. This chapter is not meant to frighten, but so that when we see the wind rising on the sea, we remember: the wind is “heaven’s” wind. Even chaos is in heaven’s hand.</p>

Before, four beasts came up one after another; now there is one sentence: “I watched until thrones were placed.” Notice that it is not “a son of David” and not “an army,” but “thrones.” The beasts are busy scrambling for position; in heaven a place has long since been prepared.</p><p>Then the one who sat, “the Ancient of Days.” His clothing was white as snow, and the hair of his head like pure wool. <p><strong>The four beasts before were “like beasts”; this one is “like a man.”</strong></p><p>We come to the highest point of the whole book. Before there were four beasts: like a lion, like a bear, like a leopard, and one beyond description. Now it changes: “there came with the clouds of the sky one like a son of man.” It is a strong contrast: what came up from the sea were beasts; what came with the clouds from heaven was “one like a man.” Human kingdoms in history have often been beastly; the kingdom God establishes has a human face.</p><p>Notice that he did not walk up to the throne himself; he was “brought near” before the Ancient of Days. The kingdom was not seized by him; it was given. “Dominion was given him, and glory, and a kingdom.” These three stand in sharp contrast with the fourth beast’s “devoured and broke in pieces”: the beast seizes, the son of man receives; the beast swallows, the son of man is given. <strong>The true kingdom is not taken by force; it is entrusted.</strong></p><p>Then the range of this kingdom: peoples, nations and languages should serve him. However it is read, this scene tells us: <strong>history’s end is not a still greater beast, but one like a man, given the kingdom.</strong></p>

<p><strong>קַדִּישֵׁי עֶלְיוֹנִין</strong> (the saints of the Most High): <strong>עֶלְיוֹנִין</strong> (Most High) is plural, meaning “the Highest (One),” a plural of majesty. <strong>קַדִּישֵׁי</strong> “holy ones” is also plural. “The saints of the Most High” are the holy people who belong to the Most High. Later interpreters differ on who the “saints” are: the people of God on earth, the angels of heaven, or both. See the issues.</p><p><strong>יְקַבְּלוּן מַלְכוּתָא</strong> (will receive the kingdom): <strong>קבל</strong> (to receive): in 5:31 Darius “received” the kingdom, and here the saints too will “receive” the kingdom. The same verb: the kingdom is something received.</p>

<p><strong>Even Daniel was frightened by this dream; he did not rush to say he understood, he went and asked.</strong></p><p>Verse 15 says: “my spirit was grieved within my body, and the visions of my head troubled me.” A man who did not waver in the lions’ den this time was frightened by a dream. This is important: <strong>faith is not the absence of feeling.</strong> Daniel was frightened, but he did one thing: he came near to one of those who stood by and asked him the truth. He did not interpret it himself, and did not guess; he went and asked.</p><p>The attendant’s explanation was short: “These great animals, which are four, are four kings, who will arise out of the earth.” The four beasts arise out of the “earth,” consistent with what was said before, that they came up from the “sea.” Then a very powerful sentence: “But the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom, and possess the kingdom forever, even forever and ever.” The word “But” is the turn of the whole explanation: before it, four beasts, rising and falling; after it, an everlasting kingdom.</p><p>Notice that the saints’ receiving the kingdom is also a “receiving,” the same verb as Darius’s “received the kingdom” in 5:31. The kingdom is not gained by seizing or by human effort; it is given. Who are the “saints”? They may be God’s people on earth, or may include a company in heaven; the various views read it differently. We need not draw a line here to set the son of man against the saints: between them there is a connection, not a conflict, and we set this out in the issues.</p><p>For us today this sentence holds a deep comfort: <strong>the people you belong to may look very small and overlooked in the world’s eyes, yet the text says they will receive the kingdom.</strong> This is not a hope with pride in it but a hope with humility: we receive the kingdom not because we are strong but because the kingdom is given.</p>

<p><strong>וְדִינָא יְהַב לְקַדִּישֵׁי עֶלְיוֹנִין</strong> (judgment was given to the saints of the Most High): literally “judgment was given to the saints of the Most High.” This can be read as “the judgment was pronounced in their favor” (vindicating them), or as “the authority to judge was given to them.” The two readings are set side by side, but both point to the saints receiving justice.</p><p><strong>יִסְבַּר לְהַשְׁנָיָה זִמְנִין וְדָת</strong> (he will plan to change the times and the law): <strong>סבר</strong> (to plan) is joined with <strong>שנה</strong> (to change): what this little horn wants to change is not one thing but “time” and “law,” that is, the framework of time in which people live and the norms by which they live.</p><p><strong>עִדָּן וְעִדָּנִין וּפְלַג עִדָּן</strong> (a time and times and half a time): the original is “a time, two times, and half a time,” using the same word (עִדָּן) as the “seven times” of 4:16, 23. The length is unclear; see the issues.</p>
@@2634@@
<p><strong>Even Daniel most wanted to know about the fourth beast, because that one was the hardest to face.</strong></p><p>Verse 19 is a very honest sentence: “Then I desired to know the truth concerning the fourth animal.” He did not ask especially about the first three. He asked especially about the fourth, because that one was different from the others, exceedingly terrible. He said his fear plainly. This is a very good example: there is no need to pretend we are not afraid. Bring the thing you fear most before God, and ask: what is this?</p><p>The attendant’s explanation has three layers. First: the fourth beast is a fourth kingdom on earth, different from all the kingdoms. Second: the ten horns are ten kings; afterward another arises, different from the former. Third: this king does three things: he speaks words against the Most High; he wears out the saints of the Most High; he plans to change the times and the law. <strong>All three put God somewhere else: speaking big words belittles God; wearing out the saints attacks those who belong to God; changing the times and the law means rewriting the times and norms God has set.</strong></p><p>Who this little horn is has long been read in many different ways; we set them side by side in the issues, and the position of these notes is clear: we do not identify anyone of today, and we do not count days. “A time and times and half a time” is in the original “a time, two times, and half a time,” and we do not decide its length for it. What we see is its direction: the saints “given into his hand” is limited, bounded, and has an end.</p><p>Then comes the turn. Verse 22 says: “until the ancient of days came, and judgment was given to the saints of the Most High, and the time came that the saints possessed the kingdom.” These words are spoken to all who are in suffering. <strong>What you have suffered was not suffered in vain; what you are waiting for is not something you will wait for in vain.</strong> The text does not say which day this time is; it says only: it will come. For those under persecution this is a great comfort; for those not under persecution it is a reminder: do not make light of, or make use of, someone else’s suffering.</p>
