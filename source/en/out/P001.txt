@@10@@
Has there ever been a time when your circumstances did not force you to give up your faith, but slowly handed you a new name, a new position, a new standard, until little by little you became what they wanted you to be? Daniel chapter 1 begins with a besieged city, yet it turns at a small table: God’s people were carried off into someone else’s kingdom, their names were changed, their futures were arranged, and Daniel quietly made up his mind in his heart. This chapter asks us: in someone else’s kingdom, who has set my heart in place?
@@11@@
People can change your name and arrange your future, but they cannot take away a heart that has already been set before God; and what makes a person stand is the favor and wisdom God gives.
@@12@@
In the third year of Jehoiakim king of Judah, the king of Babylon besieged Jerusalem, and the Lord gave the king and some of the vessels of the temple into his hand. The king ordered that young men, handsome and well versed in learning, be chosen from among the Israelites, to be taught the writing and language of the Chaldeans, and to be given a daily portion of the king’s food and wine for three years. Among them were Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah, and they were given new names. But Daniel resolved not to defile himself with the king’s food, and he asked the chief of the eunuchs for permission. God gave him favor, and after a ten-day test their faces looked even better. God also gave the four young men knowledge and skill, and Daniel understood all kinds of visions and dreams; when the king questioned them, he found them ten times better than all the magicians and enchanters in his whole realm. Until the first year of King Cyrus, Daniel was still there.
@@13@@
“But Daniel resolved”: a name can be changed and circumstances can be arranged, but the decision set in the heart is where God’s servant finally stands.
@@14@@
Every turn in this chapter has God as its subject: “the Lord gave,” “God made him find favor,” “God gave them knowledge.” Grace does not mean I first stand firm and then God helps; he moves first, and only then is it possible for me to have a settled heart.
@@15@@
Selflessness is not suppressing yourself; it is letting go of the thought, “I will arrange the outcome in God’s place”: let the heart settle, ask, and leave the consequences with him.
@@16@@
“Now God made Daniel find kindness and compassion in the sight of the prince of the eunuchs.”
@@17@@

@@47@@
<p><strong>וַיִּתֵּן אֲדֹנָי</strong> (the Lord gave): the active verb that opens the whole book, and its subject is not Nebuchadnezzar but <strong>אֲדֹנָי</strong> (the Lord). Verse 1 looks like a military move by the king of Babylon, but verse 2 at once corrects it: it was the Lord who handed the king over.</p><p><strong>אֶרֶץ־שִׁנְעָר</strong> (the land of Shinar) is the plain where the tower of Babel was built in Genesis 11; from its very first line the book leads the reader back to that city that wanted to “make a name” for itself.</p>
@@48@@
<p><strong>Verse 1 says that Nebuchadnezzar besieged the city; verse 2 says that it was the Lord who gave the king into his hand.</strong> It is the same event read two ways, and this whole book is teaching us to read it the second way.</p><p>First, the history: the third year of Jehoiakim was about the time when Babylon first reached out its hand toward Judah. The city was not destroyed, only besieged; the king was not killed, only handed over; the temple was not burned, only some of its vessels were taken. This was the first group of exiles, not large in scale, but already a signal: God’s people could no longer count on the protection of walls and sanctuary.</p><p>Now the wording. The word “gave” recurs through this chapter: the Lord gave the king, God gave Daniel favor before the chief of the eunuchs, and God gave knowledge and skill to the four young men. Who gives and who receives—the text reminds us again and again: <strong>the hand of history is not in the hand of the king of Babylon.</strong></p><p>The vessels were brought into “the house of his god” and put into “the treasure house of his god.” In Babylonian eyes, this was proof that their god had beaten Yahweh. But the text speaks very quietly: not beaten, but <strong>handed over by the Lord himself</strong>. The most painful thing about exile is that it looks as if God lost; this book is going to tell us that God did not lose, he has only begun to work in another place.</p><p>Today we too often see only the besiegers in our own days of being “besieged.” Verse 1 lets us know: the siege is real, the pain is real, but the subject of verse 2 is the Lord.</p>

@@50@@
<p><strong>לְלַמְּדָם סֵפֶר וּלְשׁוֹן כַּשְׂדִּים</strong> (to teach them the writing and language of the Chaldeans): <strong>סֵפֶר</strong> originally meant “something written,” and here it covers a whole body of learning and literature, not only literacy. A three-year course is a complete replacement of the mind.</p><p>The <strong>וַיָּשֶׂם</strong> (he set) of 1:7 and the <strong>וַיָּשֶׂם</strong> (he resolved) of 1:8 are the same verb in the same form: the chief of the eunuchs “set” new names, and Daniel “set” his heart. The two “settings” meet face to face in the next verse.</p>
@@51@@
<p><strong>Babylon did not want prisoners; it wanted colleagues.</strong> This passage is the first trap of the whole book, and it is laid very gently.</p><p>Look at the conditions for choosing them: royal family, nobles, young, without blemish, handsome, intelligent, knowledgeable, able to stand. This is not picking forced labor; it is picking successors. Then look at the treatment: the king himself appointed their food and wine, nourished them for three years, and then they would “stand” before the king. They ate the king’s meal, studied the king’s books, and would one day stand in the king’s palace. All the conditions were so good that one could hardly feel anything but gratitude.</p><p>Then the names. Daniel means “God is my judge,” Hananiah “Yahweh has been gracious,” Mishael is generally taken as “Who is what God is?”, and Azariah “Yahweh has helped.” Four names, and every one of them carries Yahweh. And the new names? Belteshazzar is generally thought to be connected with the Babylonian god <strong>Bel</strong>; Abednego means “servant of Nego (Nabu)”; the origins of Shadrach and Meshach are disputed among scholars, and we need not force an answer. The point is: <strong>names were “set,” not “asked.”</strong> They were not asked whether they were willing; they were simply given new titles.</p><p>Please notice one detail: the text uses <strong>the same verb</strong> for two things. The chief of the eunuchs “set” names, and in the next verse Daniel “set” his heart. People can change your name, but they cannot change what you have set in your heart.</p><p>This is the situation of many people today: workplace, school, and surroundings do not need to force you; they only need to give you very good conditions, and you slowly become what they want. Only when we see this “gentle assimilation” do we understand why Daniel was so serious in the next verse.</p>

<p><strong>וַיָּשֶׂם דָּנִיֵּאל עַל־לִבּוֹ</strong> (Daniel set it upon his heart): this is the Hebrew idiom for “making up one’s mind.” The text does not say he “felt” or “hoped”; it says “set upon the heart,” that is, before anything happened, there was already an unmoving place in his heart.</p><p>The stem of <strong>יִתְגָּאַל</strong> (to defile himself) is reflexive: he was unwilling to <strong>let himself</strong> become one who is defiled. The second occurrence, <strong>יִתְגָּאָל</strong>, carries different vowel points, a difference in the traditional reading; the meaning is unchanged.</p>
@@54@@
<p><strong>The most important act of the whole book takes place at a dinner table, and before anyone forced him.</strong></p><p>The word “resolve” in the original is “set upon the heart.” The timing matters: the king’s food had just been assigned, no one had yet said that refusing to eat meant death, and no one had stepped forward to object, yet Daniel already had his answer. Many people’s failure is not at the moment of greatest pressure but in <strong>not having resolved beforehand</strong>: deciding only when the pressure comes is usually too late.</p><p>What he resolved was not “shall I eat or not,” but “I will not defile myself.” The text does not explain why he considered the king’s food defiling: perhaps the meat and wine had been offered to idols, perhaps it was food forbidden by the law, or perhaps accepting the king’s table meant accepting the king’s sovereignty. This is where the text deliberately says nothing, and we need not supply Daniel with a single answer; what is truly worth watching is <strong>the way he handled the problem</strong>.</p><p>He did not protest publicly, refuse to attend, or secretly pour out the food. He “asked” the chief of the eunuchs. The word is gentle: <strong>resolve is hard; speech is gentle.</strong> His firmness was not wrapped in arrogance, and his respect was not bought with compromise. This is wisdom for life in someone else’s kingdom: the heart must be settled, and the attitude must be humble.</p><p>Back to 1:7: their names were set, and Daniel set his own heart. <strong>What people can take away is name, surroundings, and future; what they cannot take away is a decision already set upon the heart.</strong> Today, at work, at home, at school, you may have a “small table.” You do not have to change the world at once, but you can decide in your heart today: this one thing, I will not touch.</p>

<p><strong>וַיִּתֵּן הָאֱלֹהִים</strong> (God gave): the subject is God and the verb is “give,” echoing the “the Lord gave” of 1:2. <strong>לְחֶסֶד וּלְרַחֲמִים</strong> (kindness and compassion) are two words used together; in the Old Testament they are often paired like this only when explaining God’s character.</p><p><strong>וְחִיַּבְתֶּם אֶת־רֹאשִׁי</strong> (you would endanger my head): literally, “you would make my head guilty,” a way of speaking in which the head is the payment.</p>
@@57@@
<p><strong>The chief of the eunuchs was not the villain; he feared the king, but God had already begun working in his heart.</strong></p><p>The order in verse 9 is delicate: God first gave Daniel favor, and only then came the chief’s answer. Daniel did not get his answer first and then resolve; <strong>he resolved, and then God opened the way</strong>. Many people reverse the order: they wait until every condition is clear before deciding. The order the text gives us is: first settle the heart, then the road opens.</p><p>Now look at what the chief said. He said, “I fear my lord the king.” It is not that he lacked sympathy; he simply did not dare. His “head” was at stake. If these four young men looked worse, he would pay with his life. This is not obstruction; it is a person living wedged under power telling his real fear.</p><p>This gives us a reminder: <strong>in an unbelieving environment, those who oppose you are not necessarily bad people; very often they are only afraid.</strong> If we treat everyone who disagrees as an enemy, we cannot see the favor God has already prepared in their hearts.</p><p>What God gave was “kindness and compassion.” This was not Daniel’s eloquence or his popularity. All he did was settle his heart and open his mouth; the rest was God’s work.</p><p>In 321 language, this is the starting point of “selflessness”: not suppressing yourself, but no longer preparing outcomes in God’s place. A person settles the heart and leaves the consequences with him.</p>

@@59@@
<p><strong>הַזֵּרֹעִים</strong> (vegetables) comes from the root for “seed,” literally “things sown.” It does not refer only to greens; it can include legumes and grains. The English “vegetables” is enough, and there is no need to speculate further.</p><p>The ten-day “test” is <strong>a proposal that can be checked</strong>: Daniel did not say, “Trust me, God will keep us,” but “see for yourself.” Faith does not shy away from testing.</p>
@@60@@
<p><strong>Daniel did not demand a miracle; he offered a method the other side could accept.</strong></p><p>The chief of the eunuchs said no, so Daniel changed the person he spoke to: he went to the steward who managed them directly, and set out one condition. Ten days, vegetables and water, and then <strong>look with your own eyes, and deal with us according to what you see</strong>. This was not a gamble; it was handing the decision to the other side, and handing over the consequences with it: if it does not work, you may go back to the king’s way. </p><p>There is a spiritual order worth noticing here: he did not demand that others be like him, and he did not criticize the king’s food. He simply asked, for himself and for his three companions, for a space in which to obey conscience. Firm in position, calm in tone, clear in terms, transparent in result.</p><p>The result in the text is simple: after ten days, their faces looked better and their bodies were fatter. The steward took away the king’s food and wine and gave them vegetables. Please notice that this does not say vegetarian food is healthier, and it is not any teaching about diet; the text does not say that the result came from the food itself. What it says is: <strong>when a person resolves in conscience not to defile himself, God takes responsibility for the consequence that person was afraid of.</strong> They feared looking worse, and God made their faces look better.</p><p>We need not turn the ten days of vegetables into a set of rules, nor expect that every act of holding firm will bring a visible good result. Chapter 3 will say: even if not. But here, God first gave these four a small witness: he remembers the decisions set upon the heart.</p>

@@62@@
<p><strong>נָתַן לָהֶם הָאֱלֹהִים</strong> (God gave them): this is the same verb as the <strong>וַיִּתֵּן אֲדֹנָי</strong> of 1:2 and the <strong>וַיִּתֵּן הָאֱלֹהִים</strong> of 1:9. Three times in chapter 1 there is a “giving”: the Lord gave the king, God gave favor, God gave wisdom.</p><p><strong>הֵבִין</strong> (understand) is a causative stem, with the sense of “making others understand.” Daniel’s “understanding” was not something he worked out himself; it was given to him.</p>
@@63@@
<p><strong>This verse turns the first chapter over and reads it from the other side: the one truly “educating” these four young men was not Babylon’s three-year course, but God.</strong></p><p>The king’s conditions were: intelligent, knowledgeable, well versed in all learning. But this verse says that these things were <strong>given by God</strong>. The same intelligence that the Babylonians thought they had trained, the text says God gave. They used the king’s books and learned the king’s learning, but the source of wisdom lay somewhere else.</p><p>One person is singled out by name: Daniel “had understanding in all visions and dreams.” That sentence prepares every story that follows: in chapter 2 he interprets Nebuchadnezzar’s dream; in chapter 4 he interprets the king’s second dream; from chapter 7 on, he himself is given visions. Notice: <strong>his ability did not come from an examination; God gave it to him specially, and not for his own sake.</strong></p><p>This is a reminder to believers who study and work in the world: you can study well, and you should; but what finally lets you stand is not your degree but the understanding God gave. This is not a call to stop working hard; it is knowing, while working hard, to whom the credit finally belongs.</p><p>One more thing: the verb “understand” later becomes a mark of the whole book. In chapters 8, 9 and 10, Daniel asks God again and again to “make me understand,” which shows that he never treated this understanding as his own possession.</p>

<p><strong>עֶשֶׂר יָדוֹת</strong> (ten times) is literally “ten hands,” a way of stressing “far better”; it need not be taken as an exact number.</p><p><strong>הַחַרְטֻמִּים הָאַשָּׁפִים</strong> (the magicians and the enchanters) are a group that recurs in chapter 2 and again in chapters 4 and 5. <p><strong>The three years of study were over, the king examined them himself, and the result was: among all these, none could compare with these four.</strong></p><p>The point of this passage is not the grades but “the standard used.” What the king asked about was “matters of wisdom and understanding,” that is, the ability to govern and to judge, not whether they were vegetarian. <p><strong>וַיְהִי דָּנִיֵּאל</strong> (Daniel was): the verb <strong>הָיָה</strong> only means “to be.” The last sentence of the chapter uses no verb of power; it says only: he was there.</p><p>“The first year of King Cyrus” (about 539–538 BC) is the end of the exile. @@69@@
<p><strong>The last sentence of chapter 1 has only a few words: Daniel was still there.</strong></p><p>The weight of this sentence can only be read against the whole history. Nebuchadnezzar’s dynasty ended, Babylon fell, Media and Persia took over; all the power that once changed names, set banquets, and picked talent passed away, one after another. <strong>Only one young man who had been carried off was still there.</strong> He did not stand by power, and he did not stand by compromise, but because his heart, at that first table, had already been set.</p><p>The text uses the most ordinary verb: “was.” It does not say he “succeeded,” “held power,” or “was honored”; it says only that he “was there.” Sometimes the greatest praise God gives a person is not what he accomplished, but that, through long years and one king after another, <strong>he still stood before God.</strong></p><p>This sets the tone for the next eleven chapters: this is not a book asking “when is the end,” but a book about how, in someone else’s kingdom, God’s servant stays. You and I can also, in our own generation and at our own post, simply be someone who is “still there.”</p>

I. Who Set Your Name? (1:1–7)
@@346@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Today I want to begin the lesson with a name tag. You have just started at a company, and the personnel office hands you a name tag. What is printed on it is not your name, but an English name the company picked out for you: Peter, Amy, Jason. You smile and take it, because this way colleagues can call you easily, clients can remember you, and it is all very convenient. But one day you stand in front of the mirror and suddenly ask yourself: am I still me?
@@347@@
Daniel chapter 1 begins in exactly this kind of situation. This book is written for people who live in someone else’s kingdom: the exiled, the foreigners, and also those who, in someone else’s city and someone else’s system, have slowly had their names changed. Verse 1 says that Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came to Jerusalem and besieged it. It looks like a war. But in verse 2 the subject changes: “The Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand, with part of the vessels of the house of God.” It was not that Babylon won; the Lord handed them over.
@@348@@
The same event, two ways of reading it. Verse 1 is read with the eyes; verse 2 is read with faith. The whole book of Daniel is going to teach us to learn the second way of reading. Exile really hurts; the vessels were carried into another god’s temple, and it looked as if the other god had triumphed. But the text says that God did not lose; he had only begun to work in another place.
@@349@@
Think about it: what were the three years of study actually teaching? Verse 4 says they were to be taught the writing and language of the Chaldeans. That was not only literacy but a whole way of seeing the world: how to write, how to calculate, how to judge, how to worship. It was a gentle project of replacing the mind. People are not replaced in a single day; they are replaced slowly, in a portion at the table each day, a page in the classroom, a sentence among colleagues. So when we read this chapter today, we are not looking for a villain, but learning to see: when is someone setting my heart for me?
@@350@@
Then the king ordered the selection: members of the royal family and nobles, young, without blemish, handsome, well versed in learning, to be taught the writing and language of the Chaldeans. Each day they would be given the king’s food and wine, and be nourished three years. You see, this is not looking for forced labor; it is picking successors. Babylon’s greatest skill was not the whip but training. Being crushed is easier to recognize; being groomed is the hardest, because you say: this is an opportunity, this is a blessing, I should be grateful.
@@351@@
Next, they were given new names. Daniel means “God is my judge”; Hananiah is “Yahweh has been gracious”; Mishael is generally taken as “Who is what God is?”; Azariah is “Yahweh has helped.” Four names, and every one of them carries God. And the new names? Belteshazzar is connected with a Babylonian god, and Abednego is the servant of a certain god. More importantly, the names were “set”; no one asked them whether they were willing. Sometimes what we lose is not our faith, but the very name by which we speak our faith.
@@352@@
Was it malicious of the chief of the eunuchs to change their names?
@@353@@
The text does not state his motive. In ancient courts it was common to register new names for those brought into the palace, so it may have been only an administrative habit, or it may have been a deliberate attempt to reshape their identity. We need not condemn him, but we must see the result: the names carried God, the new names carried other gods, and whoever has the right to name a person has the right to decide who he is. This reminds us that in any environment, what matters most is not what others call me, but whether, before God, I still remember who I am and to whom I belong. A name can be changed, but the identity of being known by God, no one can change.

II. “But”: The Turning Point of the Whole Book (1:8–10)
@@355@@
In verse 8 there is a very small word that I ask you to notice: “But Daniel purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself with the king’s dainties, nor with the wine which he drank. Therefore he requested of the prince of the eunuchs that he might not defile himself.” That word is “But.” Everything before it was done by others: the king gave orders, the chief of the eunuchs changed names, the government assigned the food. Until verse 8: but Daniel. This “but” is the turning point of the whole book.
@@356@@
He resolved. The meaning of the original is “set upon the heart.” The “gave names” of 1:7 and the “resolved” of 1:8 use the same verb, meaning “to set.” The chief of the eunuchs set the names, and Daniel set his own heart. Two “settings,” face to face in this verse. People can change your name, change where you live, change your future, but there is no way for people to change the decision you have set in your heart.
@@357@@
We should also look at verse 10, the chief of the eunuchs’ answer: “I fear my lord the king, who has appointed your food and your drink. For why should he see your faces worse looking than the youths who are of your own age? Then you would endanger my head with the king.” He was not a bad man; he was afraid. His head was at stake. Sometimes we treat everyone who disagrees as an enemy, but many people are only caught in the middle of power, and also want to save their own lives. When we see this, it is easier to be gentle when we speak: I am not fighting you; I am asking you to give me a chance.
@@358@@
Please notice the timing: when did he resolve? Before anyone threatened him, before the food was set on the table. The pressure had not yet come, and his heart already had a place. Many people’s weakness is not failing at the moment of greatest pressure, but at the moment before they had resolved. Deciding only when the pressure comes is usually too late.
@@359@@
Now look at how he spoke. He did not pound the table, did not go on strike, and did not declare before the king who was right and who was wrong. The text says: he “requested.” Resolve is hard; speech is gentle. The heart must be settled and the attitude humble; this is wisdom for living in someone else’s kingdom. Verse 9 goes on: “Now God made Daniel find kindness and compassion in the sight of the prince of the eunuchs.” The order is: he resolved first, and then God opened the way. Many people want to see the road before they decide; the order the text gives us is: first settle the heart, then the road opens.
@@360@@
The text does not say what was wrong with the king’s food. May we supply an answer ourselves?
@@361@@
We may guess, but we must not speak too confidently. Perhaps the food came from sacrifices offered to idols, perhaps it involved meat the law did not permit, or perhaps accepting the king’s provision symbolized allegiance. The text deliberately says only “defile,” putting the emphasis on how he handled it, not on which explanation is right. If we insist on supplying a single answer, it is very easy to turn this chapter into a diet list, and miss the real point: his single-mindedness and his gentle attitude. What we are to learn is his heart, not to draw a line in other people’s diets.
@@362@@
III. The Light of the Original: Resolve, Defile, Give (1:8, 9, 17)
@@363@@
There are three words in this chapter worth taking apart. The first is resolve. The verb in the original is שׂוּם, literally “to place, to set,” and together with “upon his heart” it becomes the Hebrew idiom for “making a decision.” Notice that the Hebrew “heart” is not only for feelings; it is the place where decisions are made. So “resolve” is not a sudden burst of emotion, but placing a matter in the position of decision and no longer moving it.
@@364@@
The second is defile, in the original גָּאַל. This word is connected with priestly duty and worship, and means to pollute, to make unclean. The text uses a reflexive form: Daniel was unwilling to “let himself” become one who is defiled. Please notice that his point is not “which food is dirty,” but “what kind of person I will not let myself become.” What he guarded was his own position.
@@365@@
The third is give, in the original נָתַן, the word that occurs most in chapter 1. Verse 2: the Lord gave. Verse 9: God made him find favor. Verse 17: God gave them knowledge. Three times the verb, and the subject is God. Babylon thought it was the one giving: giving names, giving food, giving a curriculum. But the text says, again and again, that the giver is God.
@@366@@
Now put these three words into your week. Resolve is the one thing you are willing to settle first before God; not defiling is the kind of person you are unwilling to let yourself become; give is the favor and wisdom that you know you cannot manage yourself, yet believe God will give. If you have only resolve and no giving, you will grow more and more tired; if you have only giving and no resolve, you will wait until the thing happens and only then start to scramble. You cannot do without either one.
@@367@@
Here is an example from daily life. Someone at work has already decided in his heart beforehand: I will not falsify accounts, I will not lie, I will not tamper with contracts. This decision was set upon his heart before anyone asked him to do anything. One day the supervisor really does open his mouth, and he does not need to think it over again or struggle in panic; he only needs to say in a calm voice: I cannot do this; could there be another way? This is the power of “setting upon the heart”: it lets a person still have his own position at the most tense moment.
@@368@@
Put together, these three words are the roadmap of chapter 1: a person settles the heart, and this is resolve; a person does not let himself leave his position, and this is not defiling; and all the strength that makes him stand and lets things work is not his own but given by God. This is in line with the 321 ideology: letting Jesus be King is not me bracing myself with effort, but my settling the heart, and his doing the work.
@@369@@
Are “resolve” and “willpower” the same thing?
@@370@@
No. Willpower is holding up by yourself, and when you cannot hold up, you fail; resolve is placing the heart before God, and then God himself opens the way. After Daniel resolved, the text immediately says, “God made Daniel find favor,” which shows that the road was not one he paved himself. So a person who resolves is not stronger, but clearer about whom he depends on. When we fail, a person of willpower blames himself, while a person of resolve returns to God’s presence and settles the heart again. The biggest difference between the two is which direction one walks after failing. A very practical test: when you have resolved and then failed, what is your first reaction? Is it blaming yourself and gritting your teeth to try again, or returning before God, saying it honestly, and setting it again? The former is often willpower; the latter is resolve.
@@371@@
Is the Hebrew “heart” the same as what we call “mood”?
@@372@@
No. The Hebrew “heart” (לֵב) is the center of thought, will and decision, not only of feeling. So “setting a matter upon the heart” means putting it into the position of decision, so that it does not waver whether the mood is good or bad. This is also why Proverbs says that the springs of life flow from the heart (Prov 4:23). We often say, “With the heart, you are glad; with the head, you worry,” meaning that when we place decisions in the heart and rely on the Spirit, rather than only calculating with the head, we are quieter in the face of pressure and also stronger. So when we say “set the heart before God,” we are not speaking of a feeling but of a decision: whatever my mood today, I choose to let him be Lord.
@@373@@
IV. A Request That Can Be Tested, and One Who Was Still There (1:11–21)
@@374@@
The chief of the eunuchs said no, so Daniel changed the method. He went to the steward who managed them directly and made a request that could be tested: “Test your servants, I beg you, ten days; and let them give us vegetables to eat, and water to drink. Then let our faces be examined before you, and the face of the youths who eat of the king’s dainties; and as you see, deal with your servants.” He did not ask others to believe him; he asked only for a chance that could be seen with the eyes. Faith does not shy away from testing.
@@375@@
There is another detail: notice that he said “your servants,” not “me.” He spoke for himself and also for Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah. The four of them resolved together and accepted the ten-day test together. In someone else’s kingdom, one person can easily fail to hold on, but four people who remind one another are very different. This is why we need small groups: not to monitor one another, but so that when we are most likely to relax, someone asks: are you still there?
@@376@@
The result: after ten days, their faces appeared fairer and they were fatter in flesh than the youths who ate the king’s food. Please be careful: this is not teaching vegetarianism, and it is not saying vegetables are healthier. What the text means is that when a person, for conscience’ sake, does not defile himself, God takes responsibility for the consequence he feared. They feared looking worse, and God made their faces look better. But chapter 3 will tell us that not every act of holding firm brings this kind of result right away; so we take this verse as a small witness, not a guarantee.
@@377@@
This is also why the chapter does not stop at “they were better than the others.” Verse 17 says that Daniel also understood all kinds of visions and dreams, a sentence that prepares the road for chapters 2, 4 and 7 that follow. His understanding was not for his own reputation, but so that in the king’s dream and in the king’s fear there would be someone who could speak for God. When God gives a person a gift, it is often not to make him stand high but so that others, in the dark, may see a little light.
@@378@@
Then verse 17: “Now as for these four youths, God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom; and Daniel had understanding in all visions and dreams.” They studied the king’s books, but the real teacher was God. Verse 20: when the king questioned them, he found them ten times better than all the magicians and enchanters. The last verse has only one sentence: “Daniel continued even to the first year of king Cyrus.” Babylon passed away, dynasties changed again and again, and Daniel was still there.
@@379@@
The greatest praise God gives a person is not necessarily what he accomplished, but that through long years he still stood before God. This is the hope chapter 1 gives us.
@@380@@
May I apply the witness of the ten days of vegetables to my own food decisions?
@@381@@
I do not advise it. The text does not say that vegetables themselves have power, and the subject of the result is God. If you have health concerns, you can consult a doctor and nutrition professionals separately; that is another matter. What to take away from this passage is: when I have settled my heart, God takes responsibility for the consequences; not that if I eat the right things, I will get good results. Turning this verse into a diet formula would not only drift from the text, but would also make those who are sick or cannot follow this way of eating feel they are not spiritual enough. These are people we must be careful to protect. The favor God gives often takes different forms; sometimes it is a visible benefit, sometimes it is peace in the midst of difficulty, and we need not prescribe its shape for God.
@@382@@
V. A Few Deviations We Must Recognize
@@383@@
The first deviation is turning this chapter into “only vegetarian is spiritual” or “vegetarian food is healthier.” The text does not teach dietary rules, and the subject of the result is God; using food to measure other people’s spirituality leaves the point of this chapter.
@@384@@
The second deviation is turning “resolve” into willpower, man conquering all. Resolve is setting upon the heart, but God first gave Daniel favor, and the wisdom afterward was also God’s gift; resolve and dependence are the same thing.
@@385@@
The third deviation is turning “faces looked fairer” into “hold firm and you will surely benefit.” This is a witness for this occasion, not a guarantee; chapter 3 has “But if not,” and chapter 6 has a night in the pit. Turning a witness into a transaction hurts those who held firm and did not see the result.
@@386@@
The fourth deviation is turning the learning of Chaldean learning into “believers should not receive worldly education.” The text says that God gave these four knowledge and skill in all learning. What it opposes is being assimilated, not learning; young people can study well and work well, and at the same time keep their hearts.
@@387@@
The fifth deviation is taking this chapter as the basis for “do not associate with unbelievers.” Daniel lived in Babylon and served in the king’s palace, and the text does not rebuke him. What is to be kept is single-heartedness, not isolation; not defiling, not not touching.
@@388@@
If my boss asks me to do something against conscience, should I “request” as Daniel did?
@@389@@
First discern whether it is “a small matter of conscience” or “an obvious sin.” If it is a small matter, ask peacefully as Daniel did, and offer something that can be tested; if it is clearly illegal or harmful to people, you cannot go along, and you must say no clearly and look for someone you trust and for whatever help is needed. “Asking” is an attitude; it is not a cure-all, and it does not mean you must endure forever. In no situation do you need to carry it alone. If you are under this kind of pressure, this week please find someone you trust and tell them what is happening. Finally, remember too that Daniel was not alone before the king: he had three companions, and he had God’s own preparation of favor for him. You do not need to be alone either.
@@390@@
VI. Two Real Questions in the Chinese Context
@@391@@
The first question: we often say, “when in Rome, do as the Romans do,” and at work and in society, to avoid being excluded we must be like everyone else. This saying has its wisdom, but it also has a trap: little by little, we lose even the judgment of “whether we should go in at all.” Daniel did not refuse Babylon’s learning and work; he went in. What he refused was “defiling.” We must also learn to discern which are cultural differences that I can blend into, and which is the position of the heart that I may not give up. This discernment must be made before God, before the pressure comes. A common way is, in a cultural conflict, to ask first “what will others think of me,” and not “how do I see myself before God.” The result is that we give way a little in every small place, and in the end we can no longer even count how much we have given.
