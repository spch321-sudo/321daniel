I. The Most Powerful Man in the World Could Not Sleep (2:1–13)
@@795@@
Brothers and sisters, peace to you. Have you ever had a night like this: tomorrow there is something big, you toss and turn in bed, and your mind is like a machine that cannot stop. You have thought of many ways, talked with many people, looked up much information, and still your heart is uneasy. In this lesson we read about a man who could not sleep; he was no ordinary man, he was the most powerful man in the world at that time.
@@796@@
The word “troubled” in the original means to be struck, to be stirred: the king’s spirit, as if struck again and again by a hand, could find no rest. Each of us has such times: the body is very tired and the mind cannot stop; things have been handed off and the heart cannot let go. This kind of unease is not necessarily bad; it is often God reminding us: there is a problem you cannot hold alone.
@@797@@
Daniel 2:1 says: “In the second year of the reign of Nebuchadnezzar, Nebuchadnezzar dreamed dreams; and his spirit was troubled, and his sleep went from him.” Please think about this picture: a king with armies, wealth, the best doctors and the best advisers, and yet he could not sleep. There is an unease that power cannot soothe. So he did what he thought would be most effective: he summoned all of Babylon’s experts, the magicians, the enchanters, the sorcerers, the Chaldeans, and told them to tell him the dream.
@@798@@
Think of the situation of those experts. Each of them, on ordinary days, was a respected person: with a teacher behind him, with experience, with a method that made sense. But when the king wanted a real answer, all their expertise lost its weight in an instant. We have such moments today: the doctor says, we have done all we can; the financial expert says, there is no other way; the counselor says, this is a long-term lesson. People need experts, and also need to admit that experts have limits. Admitting limits is not failure; it is the beginning of learning humility.
@@799@@
But the king made a demand: you tell me my dream first, and then I will believe your interpretation. This demand was both fair and cruel. In the past, no one could ever verify an interpretation of a dream; now the king wanted verification. The Chaldeans said one very honest sentence: “There is not a man on the earth who can show the king’s matter.” Then they added: “except the gods, whose dwelling is not with flesh.”
@@800@@
They were only half right. They said the gods do not dwell with flesh, that is, God is far from people; but Daniel is about to say: “the light dwells with him.” The first point is very simple: human wisdom will always come to a day when it reaches its end. That day is not failure; it is the time when the God of heaven begins to speak. But this end is very dangerous: the king ordered all the wise men destroyed, and Daniel and his three friends were counted in. They had not failed, yet because they belonged to that group, they faced death together. This is the situation of exiles.
@@801@@
Did the king really forget the dream, or was he testing the magicians?
@@802@@
The original can be read two ways: the CUV takes “I have forgotten,” and some read it “the matter is settled by me.” Whichever, the king’s demand is the same: first tell the dream, and only then does it count. The point is not the king’s memory but that this demand pushes human wisdom to its limit, so that the God of heaven becomes the only answer. If we get tangled here too long, we will miss the most important thing in this chapter: God is the God who reveals secrets. And the point of this chapter is not “who is more accurate,” but “who is the one who reveals secrets.” To move the eyes from the details of the dream to the God who lets people know is the best way to read this passage.
@@803@@
II. Ask First, Seek First, Bless First (2:14–30)
@@804@@
When Daniel heard the decree, he did not run away, cry injustice or pick up a sword. To Arioch, the captain of the guard who was going to kill, he asked a very quiet question: “Why is the decree so urgent from the king?” A man ready to kill was stopped by a quiet question. Then he went in to see the king, and did not say “I guarantee I can,” but only “grant me a delay.” Then he returned to his house and told Hananiah, Mishael and Azariah, asking them to seek mercy from the God of heaven.
@@805@@
Please do not forget that this Daniel, who could still speak quietly under a death threat, is the Daniel of chapter 1 who resolved at the table. A person’s steadiness in big things is often trained in small things. If he had not learned “seek first, speak afterward” in the small matter of food, he could not have been so quiet when the decree went out and the killing was on its way. So the seemingly small decisions you face today are all preparation for big things to come.
@@806@@
Please notice the order: ask first, seek first, companions first, and only then the interpretation. In chapter 1 they resolved together; in chapter 2 they prayed together. In someone else’s kingdom we are not isolated individuals; we are a small group that guards one another. Do you have someone you can seek together with today? If not, this week is the time to begin looking.
@@807@@
There is another detail worth slowing down to see: when Daniel did all this, there was no guarantee of the outcome. He did not say to the king, “I can surely do it”; he only asked for a chance, and then brought the matter before God. This is a very quiet kind of faith: not guaranteeing one’s own answer, but guaranteeing that one will not leave God. Many people think faith means being very sure of the result, but the faith in this chapter is being sure of the God who can reveal secrets, while the outcome remains open.
@@808@@
Then the answer came in the night. Daniel did not rush off to the king; he stopped first and blessed the God of heaven: “Blessed be the name of God forever and ever; for wisdom and might are his. He changes the times and the seasons. He removes kings, and sets up kings. He gives wisdom to the wise, and knowledge to those who have understanding.” This short prayer is a map of the world: time is in his hand, kingship is in his hand, wisdom is in his hand, and even darkness is in his hand.
@@809@@
At last he stood before the king. The king asked: “Are you able?” This is a question that makes it very easy to say “I can,” because saying “I can” brings a future. But he said “cannot” first: “wise men, enchanters, magicians, or soothsayers” cannot tell the king, and then, “there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets.” Then he said the watershed of the whole book: “not for any wisdom that I have more than any living.” Pride is leaving one’s position, wanting to take away God’s glory; humility is returning to one’s position, giving the glory back to God.
@@810@@
If my prayer does not receive an answer as quickly as Daniel’s, is it that I did not ask enough?
@@811@@
No. The text here records one prayer that was heard, but it is not a formula. In chapter 3 the three friends say “But if not”; in Daniel’s prayer in chapter 10, the angel also says he was held back for twenty-one days. The point of prayer is not intensity but placing ourselves before God. If you are waiting, please do not treat the waiting as your own failure; you can bless first, and then set the matter down. If you are waiting, let yourself and others know: waiting is not the same as having no faith; very often waiting itself is the posture in which faith stands. You can bless first, then be quiet, and you do not need to wait alone.
@@812@@
III. The Light of the Original: Secret, Reveal, Give (2:18–23, 28, 30)
@@813@@
There are three words in this chapter worth taking apart. The first is secret, in Aramaic רָז (raz), a word borrowed from Persian, meaning something hidden that a person cannot know by himself. This word recurs in this chapter. Much of human unease comes from “secrets”: I do not know tomorrow, I do not know the meaning of this event, I do not know what God is doing.
@@814@@
The second is reveal, in Aramaic גְּלָא (gela), meaning to uncover, to disclose. A secret is not untied by people; it is uncovered by God. You can work hard at the puzzle, but the final lid must be opened by God himself. The third is give, in Aramaic יְהַב (yehav), to give. Verse 21: he gives wisdom to the wise; verse 23: you have given me wisdom and might; verse 37: the God of heaven has given you the kingdom.
@@815@@
Look at one more detail: in all three words, the subject is not man. The secret is what God has; the revealing is what God does; the giving is what God gives. Man has only one verb: to ask, and one more: to bless. So this chapter puts people in a very relaxed position: you do not have to be responsible for solving every riddle; you only have to bring the matter before God and give the glory back to him. This is true release: I am not the final answer, and I do not need to be.
@@816@@
Put together, these three words are the roadmap of this chapter: there is a secret, there is God’s revealing, there is God’s giving. The place of man is to ask, to receive, and to bless. This is in line with the 321 ideology: Let Jesus receive all the glory is not a slogan but seeing that everything I have is given.
@@817@@
What is the difference between Aramaic and Hebrew? Why does this chapter suddenly change language?
@@818@@
Aramaic was the common language of the courts and trade of the ancient Near East, and a close relative of Hebrew. From 2:4b the Chaldeans spoke to the king in Aramaic, and the text simply continues in Aramaic, until chapter 7. The theme of this section is the foreign kingdoms and God’s sovereignty. As for the details of composition and editing, scholars hold different views, but whichever one we take, what we read is the same sentence from the same God, spoken in different languages. The Aramaic chapters in chapter 7 have the same theme: the rise and fall of foreign kingdoms, and the permanence of God’s Kingdom. This whole Aramaic section is like a large frame that holds God’s sovereignty in the middle.
@@819@@
IV. The Stone Cut Out Without Hands (2:31–45)
@@820@@
Daniel began to tell the dream. A great image: its head was of fine gold, its breast and arms of silver, its belly and thighs of bronze, its legs of iron, its feet part iron and part clay. The lower it went, the harder and the more mixed, and at last it stood on an unstable foundation. Then a stone: “You saw until a stone was cut out without hands, which struck the image on its feet that were of iron and clay, and broke them in pieces.” Notice that the stone did not strike the head of gold but the most fragile feet. Gold, silver, bronze, iron and clay were broken together, becoming chaff on the threshing floor, carried away by the wind, with no place found for them. This picture is much like many things we see today: splendid outside, inconsistent within; looking solid, yet standing in a place that could split at any time.
@@821@@
Then comes the most important sentence of the chapter: “In the days of those kings the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed, nor will its sovereignty be left to another people; but it will break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms, and it will stand forever.” This Kingdom has three marks: never destroyed, not left to another people, standing forever. Human kingdoms change hands; God’s Kingdom does not.
@@822@@
Then Daniel said a very heavy sentence: the God of heaven has given you the kingdom, the power, the strength and the glory, and you are the head of gold. This was said to a world ruler, both an affirmation and a reminder. What was affirmed is that his position was real; what was reminded is that the position was entrusted. One who is entrusted must give account to the one who entrusted. This foreshadowing, in chapter 4, will cost this king a great price, and also teach him a lesson he will never forget.
@@823@@
But this passage is the one that most tempts people to take out a map: the head of gold is Babylon, who is the silver, who is the bronze, who is the iron, and which year is which. But please notice: the text names only the head of gold as Babylon, and none of the others is named. So we do not need to turn this passage into a map. We need only listen to what it says: every kingdom has a beginning and an end; the later it comes, the harder and the more brittle; power can break everything, but it cannot make people truly one.
@@824@@
Please pay special attention to the structure of the image: the more precious the metal, the higher it is; the harder and more mixed the material, the lower it is. This is a picture opposite to our intuition. We often think that history progresses the further it goes; but this dream says that the later a human kingdom comes, the harder, the more divided, the less stable. This is not pessimism but honesty: human history has no power in itself to move toward salvation. Salvation must come from outside, like a stone cut out without hands.
@@825@@
And there are three more words: “without hands.” Human kingdoms are made by human hands; God’s Kingdom is not. God’s Kingdom does not grow out of human kingdoms and does not depend on human armies, systems or effort; its source is outside the human world. So we do not need to treat any kingdom as our final hope, nor despair at the rise and fall of any kingdom.
@@826@@
Which four kingdoms are the gold, silver, bronze and iron? May I ask my pastor?
@@827@@
You may ask, and you may also set the views side by side. The traditional reading is Babylon, Medo-Persia, Greece, Rome; another is Babylon, Media, Persia, Greece; and some think they are symbolic, representing worldly kingdoms one after another. This book does not ask us to argue about this, much less to draw up a chart or identify some nation today. You can first listen to each view, but please do not divide from brothers and sisters because answers differ. You can also, while reading, write the views side by side and then ask: what does this passage say to those who suffer, wait and hope? The answer is usually more powerful than a timetable.
@@828@@
The first deviation is matching the gold, silver, bronze and iron one by one with specific empires, years and today’s nations, and drawing up a timetable. The text names only the head of gold as Babylon; what these notes hold to is that human kingdoms have a beginning and an end, and God’s Kingdom is not made by human hands. Turning this passage into a map makes people busy counting and forgetting to look up. This is the most common and most tempting deviation, because it looks very professional and very diligent.
@@829@@
The second deviation is turning God’s Kingdom, or that stone, into a spiritual kingdom that people can build by striving or by political means. The stone was “cut out without hands,” and the Kingdom is “set up” by God. People are not the builders but the people brought in. Turning God’s Kingdom into a human movement often brings weariness, and also pride.
@@830@@
The third deviation is turning “not because of any wisdom of mine” into “people do not need to learn or work hard.” Daniel received three years of training in learning in chapter 1; what he spoke of is where the glory belongs, not a denial of learning. True humility is not belittling oneself but knowing that everything one has was given. True humility is not denying one’s talents but remembering, behind each talent, where it came from.
@@831@@
The fourth deviation is turning the king’s falling down into Daniel accepting human worship, or into believers being honored because of this. The text says only that the king fell down and commanded that an offering be made, and does not say that Daniel accepted it; the king’s words point to Daniel’s God.
@@832@@
The fifth deviation is turning the prayer in this chapter into a formula: “you ask, and God will certainly give you an answer quickly.” This chapter records one prayer that was heard, but chapter 3 has “But if not,” and chapter 10 has a prayer that was held back. Those who did not get what they wished for did not ask too little. What they need more is company, not another rebuke.
@@833@@
When different pastors explain this chapter differently, whom should I listen to?
@@834@@
First distinguish what the text clearly says from what are the views of the various schools. What is clear: God’s Kingdom is set up by him, never destroyed, and outlasts any human kingdom. As for the parts that are guesses, such as the names of the four kingdoms, they can stay in the discussion and need not be made a condition of faith. You can write down the views and read the Scripture with a companion, and you can also ask a pastor for the basis of his view. A mature believer does not need to be afraid because of differing opinions. What matters is not to draw a boundary in the church because of differing opinions. On what is clear in the truth we are one; on what is unclear, we bear with one another, and this too is how people of God’s Kingdom look.
@@835@@
The first question: we are used to “finding a way in, finding connections, finding a method.” When we meet difficulty, the first thought is often: who knows whom? Is there an acquaintance? These are not necessarily wrong; in reality they are also a kind of wisdom. But this chapter reminds us: there is a kind of problem that human connections cannot reach. At that time, do not hurry to say “I have no way,” but, like Daniel, first ask, first seek, first find companions, bless before God, and then speak. The church is the same. We easily put “methods” before “prayer”: first hold a meeting, first discuss the budget, first find resources, and only at the end say, “let’s pray a bit too.” This chapter does not say meetings are bad; it invites us to adjust the order: first stand firm before God, and then do what needs to be done.
@@836@@
The second question: we are often asked “which side are you on,” and the word “kingdom” is especially sensitive in different settings. Many people therefore have two reactions to the “God’s Kingdom” of this chapter: one is not daring to read it, the other is hurrying to match it with real politics. The direction this chapter gives us is very clear: God’s Kingdom is set up by him, not made by human hands; it is not a regime we are to establish, but a life we are brought into. So we can live quietly in any kingdom, while knowing where our true belonging lies. So we can quietly do the work before us, while knowing where our hope is placed.
@@837@@
If I live in a place where faith cannot be discussed openly, how can I read this chapter?
@@838@@
You can read it as a very quiet hope. In Babylon, Daniel never treated God’s Kingdom as a political platform to oppose the existing regime; he first kept himself from defilement in small things, and before the king he first said “not because of any wisdom of mine.” Within a safe range, in the smallest ways, you can guard your heart, pray, and support one another with companions. There is no need to write or say publicly details that could harm yourself and others. You can also ask a trusted brother or sister to be your prayer partner, and agree on a safe way of contact, so that when you need it, you know someone remembers you.
@@839@@
VII. Living Out 321—Bless First, Then Speak
@@840@@
The three foundations: Jesus is my Role Model. He also prayed at night in Gethsemane, not relying on his own methods but saying: “not what I desire, but what you desire.” The Bible is my Standard, because the blessing in this chapter starts from God’s word and God’s works, not from my own feelings. The Holy Spirit is my Guide, because the secret was not untied by reasoning but revealed by God himself in a night vision.
@@841@@
The two core values: Let Jesus be King means that before human ways run out, we first hand this matter over to him. Let Jesus receive all the glory means doing as Daniel did, and in a position where one could be exalted, saying first: not because of any wisdom of mine. The one purpose: to build God’s system—not that we build God’s Kingdom, but that God brings us into the Kingdom he has set up, and in the small group we guard one another and seek together.
@@842@@
In the small places of every day, this Kingdom is also real: when you choose not to strive for glory by your own methods, when you bring a difficulty to the small group to seek together, when you stop in the midst of busyness to bless, you are living out what “the Kingdom of God has come near” looks like. This is not a distant future but a life that can begin today.
@@843@@
How, concretely, do I “bless first, then speak” in daily life?
@@844@@
You can begin with a very small habit: before you make an important decision or say an important sentence, stop for thirty seconds and say in your heart, “God, wisdom and might are yours.” This is not a ritual but putting the position right. You will find that the tone of your speaking and the speed of your decisions slowly change. You can also agree with companions: before meetings, small groups, or family discussions, bless together for one minute. Over time this habit will change how you see “achievement”: achievement is no longer proving oneself but witnessing to him; failure is no longer being shamed but an invitation to return to him.
@@845@@
VIII. Four Kinds of Hearts, and One Kingdom
@@846@@
The proud type says: “I can handle this; I don’t need others, and I don’t need to keep praying.” Daniel had ability, yet first found companions and first blessed. Your ability is real, but only one who gives the glory to God can go far. You can begin today, hand one small matter to God, and let one companion know.
@@847@@
The fearful type says: “The decree has gone out; we can only wait to die.” Daniel did not wait; he asked first, sought first. Fear is real, but fear is not the last voice; you can, in fear, still open your mouth and ask.
@@848@@
The ashamed type says: “I have no right to be heard by God; I have just made a mistake.” This chapter does not say how perfect Daniel was; it says only that he knew who the God is who reveals secrets. You do not need to be perfect first in order to ask.
@@849@@
The wounded type says: “I once asked, and got no answer.” This wound is real. The hope of this chapter is not a guarantee of an immediate answer every time, but that it tells you: the God of heaven still changes the times and the seasons, he still rules, and you can bring your wound and keep standing before him.
@@850@@
First, every day before asking anything of God, take one minute to bless him: you change the times and the seasons, you remove kings and set up kings, wisdom and might are yours. This sentence can be very simple, only one sentence, but please say it every day.
@@851@@
Second, write down one thing that has recently succeeded for you, and write beside it: this was God’s gift, not my wisdom beating others. Writing it down is more powerful than just thinking it in your heart, and better reminds you.
@@852@@
Third, find a companion, say to each other one thing where human ways have run out, and agree to pray for each other every day this week.
@@853@@
Fourth, on an uneasy night, do not rush to find a method; first be quiet for five minutes, hand the matter to God, and then sleep. If you cannot sleep, do not blame yourself; take this unease as an invitation to prayer.
@@854@@
Fifth, where you have ability, speak up for someone in a weak position, as Daniel pleaded for all the wise men.
@@855@@
I declare: the God of heaven changes the times and the seasons, removes kings and sets up kings, and everything is in his hand.
@@856@@
I declare: secrets are revealed by God, not because of any wisdom of mine; I am only one who receives.
@@857@@
I declare: I will bless first, and then speak; seek together first, and then act.
@@858@@
I declare: the kingdom God sets up will never be destroyed; I will not put my hope in any kingdom that will pass away.
@@859@@
If I have not truly blessed God for a long time, how do I begin?
@@860@@
There is no need to begin with beautiful words. You can begin with the simplest sentence: “God, you are the God who rules.” Then add one small thing you saw him doing today. You can also read 2:20–23 straight through and make it your own prayer. The point is not the words but putting the position right: I am the creature, he is the Creator; I am the one who receives, he is the Lord who gives. If you can, ask a companion to read it with you, and after reading, each say one sentence that touched you most today. When the voice says it, prayer becomes more real.
@@861@@
Daniel chapter 2 begins with a king who could not sleep and ends at a stone cut out without hands. What this chapter gives us is not a historical timetable but a declaration: human wisdom has an end, human kingdoms have a beginning and an end, and only the God of heaven changes the times and the seasons, removes kings and sets up kings, and himself sets up a Kingdom that will never be destroyed.
@@862@@
I also want to invite you, this week, to find a quiet moment and slowly read 2:19–23, and make it your own prayer. You do not need many words; just let your heart follow this blessing, again and again putting the position right: he is God, I am man; he rules, I receive. This kind of practice will slowly change how you face unease and difficulty.
@@863@@
Brothers and sisters, let’s return to that sleepless night. When human ways run out, you can be like Daniel: ask first, seek first, find companions first, bless first. The focus of this lesson is Jesus: he is our Role Model, he is our King, and he is the one who will receive all the glory. The focus is not on whether I have a way, but on the God who holds the times and the seasons.

May God make us live in the Kingdom he has set up, until his Kingdom fills the whole earth. In the name of the Lord Jesus Christ we pray, amen.

The text will not say to us, “the fire is not that hot”; it will only say: <strong>in the hottest place, you are not alone.</strong> This is the point of the next passage.</p>

<p><strong>דָּמֵה לְבַר־אֱלָהִין</strong> (like a son of the gods): <strong>אֱלָהִין</strong> is plural, and it can be translated “gods,” or as a plural of majesty for “God”; coming from the mouth of Nebuchadnezzar, it sounds more like “a son of the divine beings, that is, a being of the divine kind.” In 3:28 the king calls this one “his angel” (מַלְאֲכֵהּ), which is his own explanation of who it was.</p><p>This verse is the core of the issue “the fourth who looks like a son of the gods.” Later interpreters differ: some hold it was an angel (3:28), some that it was an appearance of Christ in the Old Testament (a Christophany), some that it was a sign of God’s presence. This commentary sets the views side by side in the issues.</p>
@@945@@
<p><strong>God did not keep them from entering the fire; he came into the fire and was with them.</strong></p><p>The most breathtaking moment of the whole chapter is not the coming out of the furnace but the word “Look!” The king rose up in haste, not because they had escaped, but because the number in the fire was wrong: “Did we not cast three men bound into the middle of the fire?” “True, O king.” He confirmed it three times, and then said: “I see four men, unbound, walking in the middle of the fire, and they are not hurt.”</p><p>When you read this verse, look slowly at each word: <strong>four, unbound, walking in the fire, not hurt.</strong> Those who went in bound were loosed in the fire. The fire, which had come to devour, became the place where they walked. The most wonderful thing is that the fire was still there, the furnace was still there, and the king’s command had not been revoked. God did not first put out the fire and then let them in; he walked in while the fire was still burning.</p><p>Then that line: “the appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods.” Spoken from the mouth of a foreign king, the phrase does not mean what the church later called “the Son of God.” What the king said was: this one does not belong to humankind. In 3:28 he adds that God “sent his angel.” As for who this one was, the various schools hold different views; this commentary does not make it a required item of doctrinal proof. We say only: this brings to mind Immanuel, God with us (Matt 1:23).</p><p>This is the greatest comfort of this chapter: <strong>God did not promise us we would not enter the fire, but he promised to be with us in the fire.</strong> The fire you are walking through may not yet have gone out, but you are not alone.</p>
