@@3919@@
Is “greatly beloved” said only to someone like Daniel?
@@3920@@
Gabriel said it to Daniel, but this name was not because he was especially perfect; it is because God loves those whom he loves. The New Testament also tells us that in Christ we are called beloved children. You need not, because you feel you are not good enough, push this sentence off onto someone else. In today’s prayer you can try saying one sentence to yourself and to God: I am loved by you. This is not pride but a quiet, self-releasing trust. In other words, you need not first qualify in order to be loved; you are loved first, and then you learn how to respond. This order changes everything.
@@3921@@
IV. The Word Has Gone Out; the Seventy Sevens (9:15–27)
@@3922@@
Then the prayer enters its most moving stretch. He first remembers the exodus from Egypt: “who has brought your people out of the land of Egypt with a mighty hand, and have gotten yourself renown, as it is today.” In the darkest hour he first remembers what God has done. Then he says: “My God, turn your ear, and hear. Open your eyes, and see our desolations, and the city which is called by your name; for we do not present our petitions before you for our righteousness, but for your great mercies’ sake.” Please read this sentence slowly. Even prayer is not our merit. This is a very good order: first look back at what God has done, and then open your mouth about what God is to do now. Your prayer can begin this way too: think of one thing God has already done for you, say it, and then say your need.
@@3923@@
Then three short cries: “Lord, hear. Lord, forgive. Lord, listen and do. Don’t defer, for your own sake.” Hear, forgive, act. The last ground is “for your own sake,” his name. The whole prayer moves from the people to God’s name. This is the steadiest place in prayer: what we ask is not our face but his name. If you have ever measured your prayer by “am I worthy?” this passage will set you free. You need not be worthy; you only need to set your petition down. This is a very deep freedom in prayer: no need to prove, no need to pretend, only to come.
@@3924@@
Then the prayer was not yet finished. The text says: “while I was speaking in prayer, the man Gabriel, whom I had seen in the vision at the beginning, being caused to fly swiftly, touched me.” Gabriel arrived, not at the end of the prayer but in the middle of it. He said: “At the beginning of your petitions the commandment went out, and I have come to tell you; for you are greatly beloved.” Do you have a prayer that looks as if it has no answer yet? The text says the word has already gone out. He moves earlier than we do. And there is “the time of the evening offering”: the temple was gone, the evening offering had stopped, but Daniel still remembered that hour. He carried the altar into his own prayer. This teaches us a quiet waiting: not hurrying, not doubting, only believing he is already on the way.
@@3925@@
Then the answer came: “Seventy weeks [literally, sevens] are decreed on your people and on your holy city, to finish disobedience, and to make an end of sins, and to make reconciliation for iniquity, and to bring in everlasting righteousness, and to seal up vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most holy.” This verse is the most studied in the whole book, and the one that most easily leads people to calculate days. Let us say plainly first: these notes do not count days and do not set up a timetable. There have long been many different readings, and we set them side by side in the issues.
@@3926@@
We look at the direction of this verse. What Daniel prayed was: the seventy years are up; forgive, and let Jerusalem be desolate no more. God’s answer was a larger vision: the seventy years stretched into seventy sevens. Then six things were listed: to finish the transgression, to make an end of sins, to make atonement for iniquity, these three about sin; to bring in everlasting righteousness, to seal up the vision, to anoint the most holy, these three about fulfillment. Not one of these six is military or political; all deal with sin and righteousness. God’s answer to Daniel was not a schedule of days but a promise that sin would be dealt with.
@@3927@@
The last three verses are the hardest passage in the whole book: the Anointed One is cut off, desolation is decreed, and the end is decreed too. There have long been many readings; we set them side by side and set up no chart. We see only three things. First, the order of the text: first rebuilding, then the Anointed One is cut off, then destruction and war. Second, the word that recurs: desolation, which reminds us of chapter 8. Third, the last sentence: the one who destroys is, in the end, met by God’s wrath poured out. This passage is not meant to make people anxious but to say: desolation is decreed, yet desolation also has an end. So when you read a text this hard, you might pause first and return to that prayer, to that sentence: not because of our righteousness, but because of your great mercy.
@@3928@@
Reading such a hard text as the seventy sevens, what should I do?
@@3929@@
You need not be an expert to read this chapter. First, put the emphasis back on prayer: this is the answer to Daniel’s prayer. Second, hold the six things: sin is dealt with, and everlasting righteousness is brought in. Third, admit that there are different readings, and respect other brothers and sisters. Fourth, do not use it to calculate days, and do not use it to frighten people. You can say to yourself: I do not need to understand all of this verse; what I need is to trust more in the mercy of God who deals with sin. Then you have read this chapter rightly. Also, if in this process you feel anxiety rising, return to the simplest sentence: Lord, have mercy on me. That sentence is already enough.
@@3930@@
The first deviation is to calculate the seventy sevens into dates, or to lay out a timetable of history and the end. The original says only “seventy sevens,” and does not say days, months or years; there have long been different readings, and these notes set them side by side and do not count days or set up a chart. The point of this verse is that sin and righteousness are dealt with.
@@3931@@
The second deviation is to turn “the commandment went out” into: if only you pray, God will surely answer in our time and in our way. What the text says is that God’s word had gone out as the petitions began; it does not say that we can control the outcome; Daniel waited for God’s work, and still asked on the ground of mercy.
@@3932@@
The third deviation is to turn “we have sinned” into condemning the victims, or to turn collective confession into blaming those who suffer. Daniel confesses before God on behalf of the people; he does not blame any individual who suffers. For the suffering we do not blame; we only accompany and show mercy.
@@3933@@
The fourth deviation is to identify 9:26–27 as some figure or regime of today, or to call brothers and sisters who hold a different reading unbelieving. The Anointed One, the most holy, and the people of a prince have long been read in many ways, and these notes set them side by side; we identify no one today, and we respect the faithfulness of each tradition.
@@3934@@
The fifth deviation is to turn “not for our righteousness” into “so I need not repent.” Daniel confesses through the whole chapter; mercy and repentance are not opposed but two sides of the same prayer.
@@3935@@
Someone uses this chapter to “prove” a certain end-time timetable. How should I respond?
@@3936@@
You can first listen, then return to the purpose the text itself gives. You can gently ask him three questions: does the original of this verse say “days”? Does this way of counting make you love the Lord more and be more patient? Are the six things the text gives not all dealing with sin and righteousness? Then share your own point: this chapter is the answer to a prayer, and its end is mercy. You need not win; you only need to bring the atmosphere from arguing back to reverence. Many times a quiet witness is stronger than a debate. Finally, remember that your aim is not to beat the other person but to let him see that a merciful God is still waiting for everyone.
@@3937@@
The first question: we are very used to “merit” and “worthiness.” From childhood we learn that you must do good to have blessing, and be good enough to deserve help. So we naturally measure our prayer with the same ruler: am I spiritual enough today? I have made so many mistakes; will God still listen? This chapter gives another road: the petition is not for our righteousness but for his great mercy. You may be not good enough, and still come.
@@3938@@
The second question: we are very afraid of “losing face,” and afraid of making others lose face. So we often do not say “we were wrong,” but say “they” or “those people,” or say nothing at all. Daniel confessed with “we,” his face covered with shame, and yet he did not hide from God. His prayer shows us that admitting is not the end of losing face but the beginning of healing. If you are in a setting where you cannot say publicly that you were wrong, first say it before God, and find a safe person to walk with you.
@@3939@@
I am used to measuring myself by “worthy or not.” How do I change?
@@3940@@
You can do one small practice each day. In the morning, do not ask “am I worthy today?” but ask “what shall I set down today?” Put shame, weariness and failure before God one by one, and say: not because of my righteousness, but because of your mercy. Then thank him in one sentence for accepting you. After a few weeks of practice you will find that your prayer is no longer an examination but an act of coming home. If this habit is too deeply rooted, you can also ask a pastor or counselor to walk with you. If this habit cannot be changed, you can also ask a pastor or counselor to walk with you; this is not weakness but wisdom.
@@3941@@
VII. Living Out 321: For Your Own Sake, Do Not Delay
@@3942@@
Three foundations: Jesus is my example. He taught us to pray, and also opened his mouth with “we”: “Our Father in heaven”; on the cross he spoke for others: “Father, forgive them.” The Bible is my standard, because for the seventy sevens we do not rely on guessing but on the point the text itself gives: this is the answer to a prayer, the dealing with sin and righteousness. The Holy Spirit is my guide, because in confession and petition we do not need more self-blame; we need a Spirit who is with us, to let us see God’s mercy and to make us willing to say “we.” A prayer like this and the prayer of Jesus go the same direction: may your name be kept holy.
@@3943@@
Two cores: Let Jesus reign, which means bringing God’s promise to him in prayer, not letting calculating take the place of trust in him, and, in confession, not defending ourselves but looking only to his mercy. Let Jesus receive all the glory, which means that when the answer comes we say: it was not that I prayed well; it was his great mercy, for his own name. One purpose: to build God’s system. This system is a people who pray with “we”: not standing outside pointing the finger, but confessing together, interceding for one another, and placing the petition on God’s mercy.
@@3944@@
Change the concept and the life changes: when we think “God responds because I am good enough,” our prayer is an examination; when we know “he responds because of his mercy and his name,” our prayer is a homecoming. From the self-life to the no-self life: the self-life props itself up with qualification and calculation; the no-self life puts down the abacus, puts down qualification, sets the petition down, and lets man be man and God be God.
@@3945@@
What does “no self” have to do with “we confess”?
@@3946@@
The relationship is deep. A person with self always wants to assign sin to others, or to clear himself; a person without self is willing, before God, to stand with others and admit that we need mercy. Confessing with “we” is putting down the position of “I am a good person, they are bad people” and returning to my place: I am a man, and I need mercy. This is not losing oneself but no longer using strength to keep up an untrue image. You can begin with a small thing: this week, in one conflict, first say one sentence: “I have a share in this too.” Sometimes one sentence, “I have a share in this too,” can loosen a tense relationship a little; this is the power of having no self, and the gift this chapter brings us.

You need not become good first and then come; you can come with your shame. Shame belongs to us; mercy belongs to him.

This chapter does not ask you to blame yourself; it says there is a God who is righteous and also merciful. You do not have to have faith at once, and you do not have to forgive at once; God does not hurry you.

<p><strong>וְצָבָא גָדוֹל</strong> (even a great warfare): <strong>צָבָא</strong> can be “army,” and can also be “hard service, warfare”; <strong>גָדוֹל</strong> is “great.” This phrase has long been translated variously as “great warfare,” “great hardship” and “great army”; see the issues.</p><p><strong>שְׁלֹשָׁה שָׁבֻעִים יָמִים</strong> (three whole weeks): the word “days” (יָמִים) is added specially to distinguish this from the “seventy sevens” (שָׁבֻעִים) of chapter 9: here it is three sevens of “days,” not a set of seven “years.” This detail shows that the same word “seven” is decided by its context.</p>
@@4029@@
<p><strong>He mourned three whole weeks, ate no pleasant bread and did not anoint himself; this is a man mourning for the future of the people.</strong></p><p>This is the last vision of the whole book, running from chapter 10 to chapter 12. The text first gives the time: the third year of Cyrus king of Persia. Notice that this is already the Persian era. Babylon is past. Chapter 1 said, “even to the first year of king Cyrus,” and Daniel was still there; now it is the third year of Cyrus, and the old man is still there. His name is still called Belteshazzar, the name Babylon gave, which has followed him all along. But in this chapter his name makes us see that in a foreign land he is still Daniel, “God is my judge.”</p><p>The text says, “the thing was true, even a great warfare.” The original צָבָא גָדוֹל has long been read in many ways, and we set them side by side in the issues. We can quietly say first: whichever it is, this is a word about something hard and very great.</p><p>Then Daniel himself: “I, Daniel, was mourning three whole weeks.” Why was he mourning? The text does not say. We only know that he ate no pleasant bread and did not anoint himself with oil. This is the look of mourning. Over the years some have guessed that it was for the situation of the returned people, or for the state of Jerusalem; the text gives none of these, and we do not supply it for him. But we can see this: he was not a man for himself alone. His mourning was for the future of God’s people.</p><p>Please think: have you ever mourned over something not your own? For a church, a country, a child’s future. Such mourning is not weakness; it is love. This chapter begins with the mourning of an old man, and then God’s answer comes.</p>

<p><strong>אִישׁ אֶחָד לָבוּשׁ בַּדִּים</strong> (a man clothed in linen): <strong>אִישׁ</strong> (man), <strong>בַּדִּים</strong> (linen). In Ezekiel 9–10 the man in linen is also a messenger from heaven. The appearance here has some similarity to the descriptions in Ezekiel 1:26–28 and Revelation 1:13–15, but we do not equate them; see the issues.</p><p><strong>וְלֹא רָאוּ אֶת־הַמַּרְאָה</strong> (they did not see the vision): those with him did not see it, yet fled in quaking. This has a similar structure to Acts 9:7, where Saul’s companions “heard the voice, but saw no one,” though each stands on its own.</p>
@@4032@@
<p><strong>Only Daniel saw this vision; those with him did not see it, yet fled in quaking; only he was left.</strong></p><p>The text gives the appearance of this one who comes: clothed in linen, with a belt of pure gold; his body like beryl, his face like lightning, his eyes like torches, his arms and feet like burnished bronze, the voice of his words like the voice of a multitude. This is a glory beyond description. Who this is has long been read variously: some think he is a glorious angel, like Gabriel; some think he is a higher being, even Christ, because the description of Christ in Revelation 1 has some similarity; and some think the text does not say, and we need not guess. We set the views side by side in the issues; we do not decide for you.</p><p>I want you to notice another detail: “I, Daniel, alone saw the vision; for the men who were with me didn’t see the vision.” Those with him did not see it, yet fled in quaking. There is an experience others cannot see. You may have had it: a burden others do not understand; a moment only you and God know. Please do not doubt its reality because no one saw, and please do not feel alone because others did not see. God is the one who sees.</p><p>Then Daniel’s reaction: no strength remained in him, his face grew deathly pale, and he retained no strength; when he heard the voice of his words, he fell into a deep sleep on his face. A man who had mourned for the people for three weeks, at the sight of the glory, fell down. This reminds us of chapter 8, where he also fell asleep; and it reminds us of Isaiah, who said, “Woe is me! For I am undone.” Before glory man has no strength; this is honest.</p><p>But this chapter does not stop here. Read on: a hand came.</p>

<p><strong>וְעֲמֹד עַל־עָמְדֶךָ</strong> (stand upright where you stand): the root <strong>עמד</strong> (to stand) twice: first “stand up,” then “stand in your place.” He was not raised to another place; he was raised and returned to the place where he had been.</p><p><strong>אִישׁ־חֲמֻדוֹת</strong> (greatly beloved man): the same word (<strong>חֲמוּדוֹת</strong>) as Gabriel’s “you are greatly beloved” in 9:23. In this book it appears three times: 9:23, 10:11, 10:19.</p>
@@4035@@
<p><strong>A hand touched him and he rose a little on his knees and the palms of his hands; then a voice called him: greatly beloved man.</strong></p><p>The man on the ground did not get up by himself. The text says: “a hand touched me, which set me on my knees and on the palms of my hands.” Notice this picture: he had not yet stood; he only rose a little on his knees and palms. This is a very gentle, step-by-step raising. God does not force a man who has fallen to stand straight at once; he first touches him and lets him prop himself up a little with knee and hand.</p><p>Then the first words this visitor spoke were not blame, not a command, but a name: “Daniel, you greatly beloved man.” Do you remember? In chapter 9 Gabriel called him this too. The same name appears three times in this book. To a man on the ground without strength, before saying anything else, he was first called “greatly beloved.” This is what God says first to the weak: not “be strong” but “you are beloved.”</p><p>Then came the command: “understand the words that I speak to you, and stand upright; for I have been sent to you, now.” Please notice that the original has two “stands”: “stand up, in the place where you stand.” The first stand is being raised; the second is returning to your place. This is the same undercurrent as chapter 8: standing. The ram could not stand; Daniel was raised up.</p><p>Finally the text honestly says: “I stood trembling.” When he stood, he was still trembling. This detail is warm: God does not require that we stop trembling before we stand. We can tremble and stand at the same time. If you are trembling today, you need not wait to stop trembling before you come.</p>

<p><strong>נִשְׁמְעוּ דְבָרֶיךָ</strong> (your words were heard): the passive <strong>נִשְׁמְעוּ</strong>, literally “were heard.” The verb is passive, the subject “your words”; God is the one who hears, and this passive is a respectful way of speaking.</p><p><strong>שַׂר מַלְכוּת פָּרַס עֹמֵד לְנֶגְדִּי</strong> (the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me): <strong>שַׂר</strong> (prince, chief) is also the word used in 8:11, “the prince of the army,” and in 10:21, “Michael your prince”; “withstood me” (עֹמֵד לְנֶגְדִּי) is a posture of “opposing.” In this book the word <strong>שַׂר</strong> can refer to a heavenly or an earthly one, as context decides; see the issues.</p>
@@4038@@
<p><strong>The messenger said: from the first day you set your heart to understand, your words were heard; delay does not mean not heard.</strong></p><p>This is the most important verse of this chapter: “Don’t be afraid, Daniel; for from the first day that you set your heart to understand, and to humble yourself before your God, your words were heard. I have come for your words’ sake.” Notice three things. First, do not be afraid. This is the first thing God’s messenger said to a trembling man. Second, from the first day. Not the twenty-first day, not when he had fasted and prayed the full three weeks, but “from the first day” his words were heard. Third, I have come for your words’ sake. His prayer was the reason the messenger came.</p><p>Have you felt that you prayed a long time and there was no response? Sometimes we think God has not heard. This verse says to you: “from the first day.” Your words were heard from the first day. Delay does not mean not heard.</p><p>Then the text says one thing: “But the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days.” This sentence has long been read in many different ways, and we set them side by side in the issues. Some think it refers to a spiritual power; some think it refers to the rulers of Persia, or the king’s representatives; and some think the text uses a language in which heaven and earth correspond. We do not turn this verse into a teaching of “territorial spirit warfare”: we do not teach you to identify and attack the spirit of a place. What this verse gives us is a deep comfort: in heaven, over your prayer, much is going on that we cannot see. You need not know the details; you only need to know: the words have been heard.</p><p>Michael came to help. Please notice: help is given. Verse 12 is the whole point: <strong>from the first day</strong>. Not from the twenty-first day. This is the sentence this chapter wants you to remember.</p>

<p><strong>וַיְחַזְּקֵנִי</strong> (he strengthened me): the Piel of <strong>חָזַק</strong> (to be strong): “made me strong.” It occurs in 10:18 and 19; then the command of 10:19, “be strong” (<strong>חֲזַק</strong>), is the same root. Notice: first he was “made strong,” and only then “commanded to be strong.”</p><p><strong>שָׁלוֹם לָךְ אִישׁ־חֲמֻדוֹת</strong> (peace be to you, greatly beloved man): in Israel <strong>שָׁלוֹם</strong> is a blessing, not only “no conflict” but “wholeness.” This is the blessing the messenger gave Daniel in this chapter.</p>
@@4041@@
<p><strong>The speechless man had his lips touched; the man with no strength was touched twice before he was told: be strong.</strong></p><p>This is the gentlest passage of this chapter. “I set my face toward the ground, and was mute.” A man who had mourned for the people for three weeks, at the sight of the vision, could not even speak. Then one in the likeness of the sons of men “touched my lips. Then I opened my mouth.” Notice: he opened his mouth because his lips were touched. God not only raises people to stand; he also lets the mute open their mouths.</p><p>Then the first thing he said was honest: “For how can the servant of this my lord talk with this my lord? For as for me, immediately there remained no strength in me. There was no breath left in me.” See, he did not pretend to be strong and did not say “I am fine.” He said his helplessness just as it was. God does not despise such words.</p><p>Then: “one like the appearance of a man touched me again, and he strengthened me.” This is the second touch. The first raised him to stand; the second gave him strength. Then came the command: “Greatly beloved man, don’t be afraid. Peace be to you. Be strong. Yes, be strong.” See this order: first he touched and gave strength, and only then said: be strong. <strong>This is not asking a man without strength to make himself strong; it is first making him strong, and then telling him to be strong.</strong></p><p>You may have had such moments: told to “be strong,” and with no strength. This passage tells you: true strength is not gritted teeth. It is touched into you, spoken into you, given. In the end he said: “Let my lord speak; for you have strengthened me.” He had strength not because of himself but “because of you.” This is the strength of having no self.</p>

<p><strong>בִּכְתָב אֱמֶת</strong> (in the writing of truth): <strong>כְּתָב</strong> (writing) and <strong>אֱמֶת</strong> (truth): this is a way of saying “truth already written down.” In this book it forms a line with 7:10, “the books were opened,” and 12:1, “everyone who is found written in the book”: in heaven there is a book.</p><p><strong>שַׂרְכֶם</strong> (your prince): <strong>שַׂר</strong> (prince) with “your” (<strong>־כֶם</strong>): Michael is “your prince,” one who belongs to God’s people. 12:1 also has “Michael, the great prince who stands for the children of your people,” which echoes this.</p>
@@4044@@
<p><strong>He did not tell everything; he told Daniel only: there is a book of truth, and there is a prince who is yours.</strong></p><p>The messenger said: “Do you know why I have come to you?” This is a question, and also an invitation. He went on to say that he would return to fight with the prince of Persia, and afterward the prince of Greece would come. This sentence has long been read in various ways, and we set them side by side in the issues. We do not turn this verse into a tool for predicting geopolitics, nor into a call to “go and attack the spirit of a place.” This is a description in which heaven and earth correspond, and its aim is to let Daniel understand: in the history he saw it is not only people who are moving; a greater God is in charge.</p><p>Then he said: “I will tell you that which is inscribed in the writing of truth.” “The writing of truth” is a book written down. In chapter 7 we saw “the books were opened”; in chapter 12 we will see “everyone who is found written in the book.” In heaven there is a book. What God will accomplish is “already written,” not on a sudden whim. This gives us a very deep security: history is not a tangle of threads; one has already written its direction.</p><p>Finally he said: “There is no one who holds with me against these, but Michael your prince.” Michael is “your prince”: he belongs to God’s people. This sentence is not meant to make us learn the details of angelology but to say: you are not alone. In heaven, one stands on your side.</p><p>This chapter ends very quietly. He did not finish, and gave no dates. He left only one answer: your words were heard; you were touched twice; be strong; there is a book of truth. This is the reply to a man who mourned for his people.</p>

I. Three Weeks of Mourning, and a Glory Only He Saw (10:1–9)
@@4306@@
Peace, brothers and sisters. Have you ever mourned? Not over your own affairs but for others: for a child’s future, for the state of a church, for a place you love. In this lesson we read the mourning of an old man. He mourned three whole weeks, and then God’s answer came. This is Daniel chapter 10, and the beginning of the last vision of the whole book.
@@4307@@
The text first gives the time: “the third year of Cyrus king of Persia.” Notice that this is already the Persian era; Babylon is past. But Daniel is still here. His name is still called Belteshazzar, the name Babylon gave, which has followed him all along. But in this chapter he is still Daniel. The text says: “the thing was true, even a great warfare.” The meaning of the original has long been read in different ways, and we set them side by side in the issues. We can quietly say first: this is a word about something hard and very great.
@@4308@@
Then Daniel himself: “In those days I, Daniel, was mourning three whole weeks. I ate no pleasant bread. No meat or wine came into my mouth. I didn’t anoint myself at all, until three whole weeks were fulfilled.” Why was he mourning? The text does not say, and we do not supply it. We only see that he was not a man for himself alone. His mourning was for the future of God’s people. Such mourning is not weakness; it is love.
@@4309@@
Then the vision came: by the Tigris, he lifted up his eyes and saw a man clothed in linen, with pure gold of Uphaz at his waist, his body like beryl, his face like lightning, his eyes like torches, his arms and feet like burnished bronze, and the voice of his words like the voice of a multitude. This is a glory beyond description. Who is this? There have long been different readings: some think he is a glorious angel; some think he is Christ, because Revelation chapter 1 has a similar description; and some say the text does not say, and we need not guess. We set the views side by side and do not decide for you.
